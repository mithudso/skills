# Medicaid Third-Party Liability (TPL)

> **Educational only — NOT legal advice.** TPL rules vary by state and by claim type; consult a Medicaid attorney or state TPL unit for specific situations.

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22** *(compiled from training knowledge; Ahlborn/Wos case law stable; verify current NC DMA TPL unit procedures)*

---

## 1. Medicaid as Payer of Last Resort

**Federal law (42 U.S.C. § 1396a(a)(25))** requires Medicaid to be the **payer of last resort** — meaning Medicaid must pay only after all other available sources of coverage have paid first. This includes:
- Private health insurance
- Employer-sponsored insurance
- Medicare (Part A, B, D)
- Workers' compensation
- Auto insurance (Personal Injury Protection / MedPay)
- Liability insurance (tortfeasor's liability)
- VA benefits
- Indian Health Service

**Why this matters:** Medicaid serves as a safety net, but it is not intended to subsidize other payers. Federal law requires states to take all reasonable measures to identify and pursue third parties.

---

## 2. Assignment of Rights

As a condition of Medicaid eligibility, beneficiaries must **assign to the state their rights to recover from third parties** (Section 1912 of the SSA). This means:
- The beneficiary cannot settle a personal injury claim for $0 allocated to medical bills and keep Medicaid's payment
- The state steps into the beneficiary's shoes (subrogation) to recover what Medicaid paid
- Beneficiaries who knowingly fail to cooperate with TPL recovery may face enrollment consequences

**Assignment of support rights (for children):** When a Medicaid-enrolled child is entitled to child support, the custodial parent must assign support rights to the state as a condition of CHIP/Medicaid eligibility — enabling recovery of support payments to help fund Medicaid costs.

## 3. Identifying Third-Party Coverage

### State TPL Units
Every state Medicaid program has a **TPL unit** charged with:
- Cross-matching Medicaid enrollment data against insurance databases
- Enrolling beneficiaries' third-party coverage into the claims system
- Billing third parties before or after Medicaid pays

### Data Sources for TPL Identification
- **Commercial insurance data:** States query employer insurance databases, use HIPAA-compliant data sharing, and receive CHIP-required premium enrollment data
- **Workers' compensation:** States match against workers' comp carrier reports and WCMSA approvals
- **Auto/casualty:** Accident reports, police records, motor vehicle department data
- **Medicare:** CMS provides state Medicaid agencies with Medicare enrollment data for dual eligibles
- **Income verification:** Information from MAGI verification can reveal insurance coverage

## 4. Cost Avoidance vs Pay-and-Chase

### Cost Avoidance
The state **denies or delays** payment of a Medicaid claim until the third-party payer processes it first. The provider must bill the third party; Medicaid only pays residual balance (if any) after TPL payment.
- **Advantage:** State avoids paying amounts third parties will cover entirely
- **Disadvantage:** Can delay provider payment; may deter providers if third-party billing is complex

### Pay-and-Chase
Medicaid **pays the claim first**, then pursues recovery from the third party afterward.
- Federal law permits pay-and-chase in certain circumstances:
  - Health insurers: states must pay first and then recover (to avoid provider access issues when insurance denial is pending appeal)
  - Casualty/auto/workers' comp: pay-and-chase is common because liability isn't determined until claim is resolved
  - Prenatal/labor-and-delivery, preventive pediatric care, and emergency services: states must use pay-and-chase (cannot deny care pending TPL resolution)

### NC Approach
NC uses cost avoidance for known commercial insurance coverage and pay-and-chase for liability/casualty claims and cases where cost avoidance would delay critical care.

## 5. Medicaid Liens in Personal Injury Cases

### The Medicaid Lien
When Medicaid pays for injuries caused by a third party (e.g., car accident, slip-and-fall, medical malpractice), the state has a **lien** on any personal injury settlement or judgment the beneficiary receives, to the extent it represents compensation for medical expenses Medicaid paid.

### *Arkansas Dep't of Health & Human Services v. Ahlborn* (2006)
SCOTUS held that Medicaid may only recover from the portion of a settlement **allocated to past medical expenses**, not from funds representing lost wages, pain and suffering, or future damages. States may not recover more than Medicaid's proportional share of the total settlement.

### *Wos v. E.M.A.* (2013)
SCOTUS further limited state lien recovery, holding that state statutory presumptions (e.g., "one-third of settlement is medical expenses") that exceed the actual medical expense portion are preempted by federal law.

### Practical Implications
- Attorneys resolving personal injury cases for Medicaid beneficiaries must account for the Medicaid lien
- Settlement allocation language matters — parties negotiate how much of the settlement is "medical" vs. "non-medical" components
- States must accept **proportional reduction** when total settlement doesn't cover all claimed damages

## 6. Special TPL Contexts

### Workers' Compensation
When a Medicaid beneficiary is injured at work, workers' comp is primary payer. States:
- Pursue recovery from workers' comp carriers
- Coordinate with Medicare Set-Aside (MSA) arrangements if the beneficiary is or may become Medicare-eligible
- Watch for workers' comp settlements that carve out or reduce Medicaid's recovery rights

### Auto Insurance
- Personal Injury Protection (PIP/no-fault states): Medicaid may recover from PIP up to Medicaid's payment
- Liability insurance: pay-and-chase; state pursues recovery when liability is established

### Wrongful Death
In wrongful death claims, the Medicaid lien attaches only to medical expense components of the recovery, not to loss-of-consortium or survivor damages.

### Estate Recovery Interaction
If a Medicaid beneficiary dies and there is both a TPL claim and an estate recovery claim:
- TPL recovery is pursued first (and reduces what Medicaid paid, thus reducing estate recovery exposure)
- Estate recovery and TPL are tracked separately by NC DMA

## 7. Managed Care and TPL

Under Medicaid managed care, the MCO typically manages TPL identification and recovery for services within its capitated scope:
- MCOs must have TPL identification and billing procedures
- MCO capitation rates may be adjusted (reduced) when beneficiaries have known third-party coverage
- States may "carve out" certain TPL-heavy populations from managed care capitation

---

## 8. Beneficiary Rights During TPL Recovery

**Anti-lien and anti-recovery limitations:** Federal law restricts what states can recover from beneficiaries:
- States cannot place liens on the homes of living beneficiaries (only deceased beneficiaries — estate recovery)
- Recovery from minors and SSI-disabled individuals is limited in many states
- Hardship waiver provisions apply to estate recovery (similar restrictions apply to TPL)

**Right to counsel:** Medicaid beneficiaries facing large TPL recovery claims in personal injury settlements should seek legal counsel — the interaction between Medicaid liens, *Ahlborn*, attorney fees, and settlement allocation is legally complex.
