# Structure & Beat Systems (Screen-Specific)

Four frameworks dominate screen-structure craft, and they are not rival
religions so much as lenses at different zoom levels: Field's paradigm gives
the coarse macro shape, Snyder's beat sheet subdivides that shape into a
minute-by-minute recipe, McKee's framework replaces page-counting with a
causal, scene-level logic, and the sequence approach gives a mid-grain
drafting unit between McKee's scenes and Field's whole acts. All four assume
a feature-length screenplay unless noted; TV-specific structure is covered
in `references/tv-structure-and-adaptation.md`.

## Core Concepts

### (a) Syd Field's paradigm

Syd Field's *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* (1979) popularized
three-act structure for a mass screenwriting audience via what Field called
"the Paradigm": Act One (Setup, roughly 25% of the script), Act Two
(Confrontation, roughly 50%), Act Three (Resolution, roughly 25%).[^1]

Sources split between citing Field's original page targets against a
120-page screenplay (matching the era when spec scripts ran longer) and
rescaling to today's ~110-page norm (matching Snyder's later, more modern
standard); present both rather than a single hard number:

- **Plot Point 1** (end of Act One): page 25-30
- **Midpoint** (splits Act Two into 2a/2b): page 55-60
- **Plot Point 2** (end of Act Two / start of Act Three): page 85-90
- **Act Three** runs from Plot Point 2 to the end

A **plot point** is, in Field's own definition, "an incident, episode, or
event that hooks into the action and spins it around into another
direction" (functionally identical in purpose at both act breaks despite
occurring at different structural moments).[^2]

**The pinches.** Field added Pinch 1 and Pinch 2 in response to a practical
teaching problem: students found the roughly 60-page unbroken expanse of
Act Two undirectable. Pinch 1 sits roughly midway between Plot Point 1 and
the Midpoint (around the 3/8 mark); Pinch 2 sits roughly midway between the
Midpoint and Plot Point 2. Each is a scene that reminds the audience of the
central conflict or antagonistic force. Effect: the pinches subdivide Act
Two into four roughly 15-page sequences rather than one undifferentiated
block; this is the direct conceptual ancestor of the sequence approach in
(d).[^3]

When to reach for it: use Field's paradigm as the correct starting altitude
for diagnosing "what's structurally wrong with my screenplay" at a glance:
it is closer to descriptive theory than a fill-in-the-blanks checklist.

### (b) Blake Snyder's Save the Cat! beat sheet (original screenplay version)

Blake Snyder published *Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll
Ever Need* in 2005, targeting film screenwriters specifically. (The later
*Save the Cat! Writes a Novel*, published by Jessica Brody after Snyder's
2009 death, is the prose adaptation and lives in `fiction-writing-craft`'s
plot-structure reference, not here.)[^4]

Snyder codified 15 beats against a 110-page standard screenplay, each with a
specific suggested page:

| # | Beat | Page(s) |
|---|------|---------|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1-10 |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 |
| 5 | Debate | 12-25 |
| 6 | Break into Two | 25 |
| 7 | B Story | 30 |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30-55 |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55-75 |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75-85 |
| 13 | Break into Three | 85 |
| 14 | Finale | 85-110 |
| 15 | Final Image | 110 |

Because each screenplay page is conventionally treated as about one minute
of screen time, the beat sheet functions as a minute-by-minute template, not
just a structural sketch; this is the key distinction from Field and
McKee. Snyder framed this as an evolution of three-act structure meant to
solve the "saggy middle" problem of Act Two by giving writers roughly ten
times more granular checkpoints than Field's two plot points.[^5]

Two beats do the heaviest lifting. The **Midpoint** (page 55) is a false
victory or false defeat that spins the story in a new direction, often
shifting the protagonist from reactive to active. **All Is Lost** (page 75)
is the lowest point, frequently carrying a "whiff of death," and feeds the
Dark Night of the Soul before the protagonist finds the answer that drives
Break into Three.

When to reach for it: choose Save the Cat when you want dependable
commercial pacing and a diagnosis for a specific slow patch: "Fun and
Games" and "Bad Guys Close In" give concrete permission to escalate. Its
risk is mechanical sameness (see Anti-Patterns): the beat sheet's precision
(exact pages, exact beat names) makes formula-following most visible on the
page, and critics have argued the industry's close adherence to it has
contributed to a perceived homogenization of studio filmmaking.[^6]

### (c) Robert McKee's Story framework

McKee's *Story: Substance, Structure, Style, and the Principles of
Screenwriting* (1997) organizes structure around a five-part causal chain
rather than a page-based template:[^7]

1. **Inciting Incident**: radically upsets the balance of forces in the
   protagonist's life, launching a conscious or unconscious pursuit of an
   Object of Desire to restore balance.
2. **Progressive Complications**: the body of the story between the
   Inciting Incident and the final Crisis/Climax; the protagonist faces
   escalating conflict and antagonistic force, passing successive points of
   no return.
3. **Crisis**: the ultimate decision point.
4. **Climax**: the direct consequence of the Crisis-point choice, giving
   the protagonist not necessarily what they wanted but what they needed.
5. **Resolution**: the closing beat where normal life resumes, or a new
   equilibrium is established.

**Value shifts and the scene/sequence/act hierarchy.** McKee defines a
**scene** as an action through conflict, in more or less continuous time and
space, that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at
least one value with perceptible significance: the **value shift**. A
scene where nothing changes in value is, in McKee's terms, a "nonevent."
Structural hierarchy runs scene to sequence to act to story: scenes turn on
value shifts, sequences build toward a larger reversal, acts culminate in a
major reversal, and the final act's climax is a major, irreversible
change.[^8]

The story/plot distinction, as commonly synthesized from McKee's framework:
"plot" is the strategic design of events and causation the writer builds,
while "story" is the fuller pattern of meaning that emerges from that
design: a framework oriented around *why* events connect, not *where* on
the page they land. (This specific phrasing is a synthesis drawn from
secondary summaries rather than a verbatim quote from McKee's text; treat
it as a reasonable paraphrase, not a direct citation.)

When to reach for it: McKee's framework is best for scene-level diagnostic
work (is this scene earning its place? Does a value actually shift?) and
for writers working in causally complex or non-formulaic material, since it
has no fixed page numbers at all.

### (d) The sequence approach (Frank Daniel / Gulino's eight sequences)

Frank Daniel, who chaired screenwriting programs at USC, AFI, and Columbia,
is credited as the originator of the sequence method as formally taught in
American film schools. Paul Joseph Gulino (Chapman University) codified and
published the approach in *Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach* (2004; 2nd
ed. 2024), the primary book-length treatment.[^9]

Core mechanics: a feature is divided into eight sequences, each roughly
10-15 minutes (about 15 pages) long. Each sequence functions as a
self-contained "mini-movie" with its own compressed three-act shape
(protagonist, tension, rising action, resolution), but unlike a true
standalone short, a sequence's conflicts are only partially resolved, and
that partial resolution opens the issue driving the next sequence.
Sequences 1-2 map to Act One, sequences 3-6 to Act Two, and sequences 7-8 to
Act Three, directly mirroring Field's 25%/50%/25% proportions, just
subdivided into smaller units.

The method is rooted in the physical constraints of early cinema: before
continuous reels, screenplays were formatted and shot in sequence-length
(reel-length) units, and film schools "rediscovered" this as a practical
writing technique for taming a blank 110-120-page page count.[^10]

When to reach for it: use the sequence approach as a *drafting* technique:
breaking the blank-page problem of a feature into eight manageable,
15-page mini-movies, each with its own small, solvable dramatic question.
It doesn't compete with plot-point or beat-sheet thinking so much as give a
mid-grain unit of work underneath either.

### (e) Where the systems agree, where they disagree, and which to use when

**Agreement:** all four systems accept the same underlying shape (setup,
escalating complication through a long middle, and a climax/resolution)
and even converge numerically. Field's Plot Point 1 (~page 25-30), Snyder's
Break into Two (page 25), and the sequence approach's Act One/Two boundary
(end of sequence 2, ~page 25-30 at 15 pages/sequence) all land in the same
place. Field's Midpoint (~page 55-60) and Snyder's Midpoint (page 55)
coincide, and Field's Plot Point 2 (~page 85-90) matches Snyder's Break into
Three (page 85). This convergence is not coincidental: Snyder's beat sheet
is explicitly a granular subdivision of Field's paradigm, and Gulino's
sequences are explicitly a subdivision of the same three-act
proportions.[^11]

**Disagreement: granularity and prescriptiveness.** The key axis of
difference is how much each framework tells you to do, and where. Field and
McKee treat three-act structure as an organizing principle: a handful of
general elements attached to broad page ranges, closer to descriptive
theory than a checklist. Snyder converts the same shape into a
prescriptive, minute-by-minute recipe: 15 named beats, each pinned to an
exact page. McKee diverges further still by being explicitly causality-driven
rather than page-driven: no fixed page numbers at all, just the value-shift
chain through scenes and sequences. The sequence approach is a
structural/pacing tool that sits underneath any of the above, giving a
mid-grain unit between McKee's scene-level value shifts and Field's/Snyder's
whole-act thinking.[^12]

**Practical guidance:** reach for Field's paradigm to orient on the macro
shape and diagnose structural problems at a glance; reach for Snyder's beat
sheet as a granular outlining/pacing tool, especially for genre and
studio-style scripts, while watching for the formulaic risk described in
Anti-Patterns; reach for McKee's framework for scene-level diagnostic work
in causally complex or non-formulaic material; reach for the sequence
approach as a drafting technique to break a blank feature-length page count
into workable units.

## Anti-Patterns

**Beat-sheet-by-numbers flatness.** Hitting all 15 Save the Cat beats (or
following Field's page targets) exactly on schedule while the script feels
mechanical and predictable. Diagnose when readers describe a competent
script as somehow lifeless or "seen it before." Fix by treating beat sheets
as a diagnostic checklist applied in revision, not a fill-in-the-blanks
generator; each beat should grow from character choice, not slot a generic
event into a required page position. This is the most frequently cited
critique of Snyder's system specifically, precisely because its precision
makes formula-following most visible on the page.[^6]

**The sagging middle.** Act Two drifts because nothing forces the
protagonist forward. Diagnose by checking whether the Midpoint (Field or
Snyder's sense) actually reverses or raises the stakes; if scenes could be
reordered freely, causality has gone slack. Fix by installing a real
Midpoint turn (false victory or false defeat); in Field's terms, this is
exactly the problem the pinches and the sequence approach were invented to
solve.

**Value-neutral scenes (McKee's "nonevents").** A scene in which nothing
changes for the character: no value shift, positive or negative. Diagnose
scene-by-scene: what value (life/death, love/hate, hope/despair) is true at
the top of the scene, and is it different at the bottom? If not, the scene
has no dramatic function. Fix by either cutting the scene or finding the
turn it should contain.

**Treating one framework as the only correct one.** Applying Snyder's exact
page numbers to a script that is causally complex and non-formulaic (where
McKee's framework would serve better), or applying McKee's scene-level
causal analysis as if it prescribes page counts (it doesn't). Diagnose by
checking whether the framework's native strength (page-precision vs.
causal logic vs. drafting units) matches the actual problem you're solving.
Fix by treating the four frameworks as different lenses at different zoom
levels, per (e), rather than competing systems.
