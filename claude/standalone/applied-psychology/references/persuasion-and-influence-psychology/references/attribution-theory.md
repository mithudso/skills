# Attribution Theory: How People Explain Behavior

The causal stories people tell about *why* — their own actions and others' — and
how those stories drive persuasion, blame, and conflict. Attribution is upstream
of persuasion: change someone's causal attribution and you change what argument
will move them.

## Heider (1958) — the "naive psychologist"
Heider founded the field with the observation that ordinary people act like
amateur scientists, constantly explaining behavior by assigning it to causes. The
master dimension: **internal/dispositional** causes (ability, personality, effort,
attitude) vs **external/situational** causes (luck, task difficulty, social
pressure, circumstance). In ambiguous situations, attributions get skewed by the
perceiver's own wants and needs.

## Jones & Davis (1965) — correspondent inference
For a *single* observation, people infer a disposition that **corresponds** to the
behavior when the behavior is: freely chosen, produces **non-common effects** (few
distinctive outcomes), and is low in **social desirability** (unexpected behavior
is more diagnostic of the actor).

## Kelley's covariation model (1967, 1973)
For **repeated** observations, people behave like statisticians, using three kinds
of information to locate the cause:
- **Consensus** — do *other people* act this way toward the same stimulus?
  (high = points to the stimulus, low = points to the person)
- **Distinctiveness** — does the actor behave this way *only* toward this stimulus?
  (high = stimulus, low = person)
- **Consistency** — does it happen *every time*, across occasions/modalities?
  (high = stable cause; low = circumstance)

Patterns:
- **High** consensus + **high** distinctiveness + **high** consistency → **stimulus** attribution ("the product really is the problem; everyone hits it, only here, every time").
- **Low** consensus + **low** distinctiveness + **high** consistency → **person** attribution ("it's them; nobody else complains, they complain about everything, always").
- High consistency but mixed otherwise → **circumstance** attribution.

When information is incomplete, people fall back on **causal schemata** (e.g.,
discounting: a sufficient external cause discounts an internal one — the engine
behind insufficient-justification dissonance and the overjustification effect).

## The major attribution biases
- **Fundamental attribution error (FAE / correspondence bias; Ross, 1977).**
  Observers **over-weight disposition and under-weight situation** when explaining
  *others'* behavior. "The admin is incompetent" ignores that the admin is
  under-resourced and under-trained.
- **Actor-observer asymmetry (Jones & Nisbett).** We explain **our own** behavior
  **situationally** ("I missed the deadline because the spec changed") but
  **others'** behavior **dispositionally** ("they missed it because they're
  disorganized"). *Caveat:* Malle's (2006) meta-analysis found this asymmetry is
  smaller and more conditional than the textbook version — strongest for negative
  events. Cite it with that nuance.
- **Self-serving bias.** We attribute **our successes** to internal factors
  (skill, effort) and **our failures** to external ones (bad luck, others) — to
  protect self-esteem. The mirror image: in conflict each side credits itself and
  blames the other.

## Why this matters for persuasion and conflict
- **Persuasion depends on the audience's attribution of the source.** If they
  attribute your advocacy to a self-interested motive ("they just want the upsell"),
  they discount it — an attributional version of source credibility (see
  `yale-and-inoculation.md`). Make your motive attributable to *their* benefit.
- **Conflict is an attribution clash.** In a tense renewal, *both sides* run the
  FAE and self-serving bias simultaneously: each reads the other's behavior as
  dispositional bad faith and its own as situationally forced. This is why
  escalations feel like the other party is acting in bad faith — usually they're
  just situationally constrained.
- **De-escalation move:** explicitly supply the **situational** frame for the other
  party's behavior ("they're slammed by an audit," not "they don't care") and own
  your own situational constraints out loud. Reframing the attribution does more to
  defuse a conflict than re-arguing the facts.
- **Attribution and motivation (Weiner).** People also attribute *outcomes* along
  locus (internal/external), **stability**, and **controllability** — which drives
  whether they expect future success and feel efficacy. Relevant when a customer
  blames a failed rollout on an uncontrollable, stable cause ("this product just
  never works here") — surface a controllable, unstable reframe.

## Operator cheat-sheet
1. Diagnose the **causal story** each side is telling before arguing facts.
2. Expect **FAE + self-serving bias** on both sides of any conflict.
3. De-escalate by supplying the **situational** explanation for the other party.
4. Ensure your persuasion can be attributed to the **audience's** benefit, not your
   self-interest, or it gets discounted.
