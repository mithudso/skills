# Neuroimaging of Meth-Related Brain Changes and Recovery

## Core Concepts
- PET imaging quantifies dopamine transporter (DAT) density as a proxy for dopaminergic terminal integrity in the striatum
- Structural MRI (VBM, voxel-based morphometry) detects regional grey matter volume loss across cortical and subcortical regions
- DTI (diffusion tensor imaging) measures white matter microstructural integrity via fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity
- Neuroimaging findings correlate with cognitive, motor, and psychiatric outcomes but show partial dissociation during recovery
- Abstinence duration is the primary predictor of structural and functional neuroimaging normalization

## Mechanisms / Biology
- METH floods the synapse with dopamine via DAT reversal and vesicular release, then causes oxidative stress–driven retrograde degeneration of dopaminergic terminals
- DAT loss in caudate (~35%) and putamen (~21%) reflects axonal terminal injury, not necessarily neuronal soma death — explaining partial reversibility
- Grey matter reduction is most pronounced in prefrontal cortex, insula, anterior cingulate, hippocampus, and striatum — regions governing executive control, interoception, and memory
- White matter injury (reduced FA in corpus callosum, corona radiata, cingulum, frontal deep white matter) reflects demyelination and axonal damage from oxidative cascades
- Neuroinflammation (microglial activation detectable on PET with TSPO ligands) persists beyond acute use and contributes to ongoing structural attrition
- Hypometabolism in orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and anterior cingulate on FDG-PET correlates with impaired inhibitory control and craving vulnerability

## Clinical Picture
- Globally ~27 million people use amphetamines/METH; neuroimaging studies show consistent findings across U.S., Asian, and European cohorts regardless of formulation
- DAT deficits in recently abstinent users average ~24% lower than controls in whole striatum; subregional PET shows caudate (−35%) > putamen (−21%) > ventral striatum (−20%)
- Grey matter loss is detectable after as little as 1–2 years of heavy use; severity correlates with total lifetime use in gram-years
- White matter FA reductions are widespread and associated with slower processing speed, poorer working memory, and elevated impulsivity scores
- Functional MRI shows blunted activation of PFC/ACC during inhibitory control tasks and hyperactivation of dorsal striatum during cue-reactivity paradigms

## Evidence & Treatment
- Seminal J. Neuroscience 2001 (Volkow et al.): DAT recovery of +19% in caudate and +16% in putamen after 12–17 months of protracted abstinence vs. short abstinence (<6 months), confirming partial reversibility
- Despite DAT recovery, motor and memory test scores did not normalize over the same interval, indicating functional lag behind structural recovery
- Voxel-based morphometry 2018 (Frontiers in Psychiatry): long-term abstinence (>2 years) associated with increased grey matter volume in cerebellar and subcortical regions; cortical recovery slower and incomplete
- fMRI neurofeedback targeting dorsolateral PFC is under investigation (Phase II) as a biologically guided adjunct to contingency management
- No pharmacotherapy yet approved specifically targeting meth-induced neuroimaging deficits; bupropion + naltrexone (ADAPT-2) improves treatment response but neuroimaging correlates are not established

## Key Facts
- 24% average DAT reduction in whole striatum of active/recently abstinent METH users vs. controls (PET, Volkow et al.)
- DAT partially recovers (+16–19% by region) after ~14 months abstinence, but functional improvement lags structural recovery
- Grey matter loss is measurable in PFC, insula, hippocampus after chronic use; some regions show recovery after >2 years abstinence
- DTI fractional anisotropy reductions in corpus callosum and frontal WM correlate with executive dysfunction scores
- Neuroinflammation (TSPO-PET microglial activation) persists up to 1 year after last use, even after DAT begins recovering
