# Meth Withdrawal and Protracted Dysphoria

## Core Concepts
- Meth withdrawal is psychologically severe but not medically dangerous (no seizures/autonomic crisis as with alcohol/opioids)
- Three phases: acute crash (12–24 h), withdrawal/anhedonia phase (2–4 weeks), protracted abstinence syndrome (6–24 months)
- DSM-5 criteria require dysphoric mood plus ≥2 of: fatigue, vivid/unpleasant dreams, hypersomnia or insomnia, increased appetite, psychomotor retardation or agitation
- Primary relapse driver is anhedonia and amotivation, not craving for euphoria — first 2 weeks post-crash are the highest-risk window
- Protracted abstinence syndrome (PAS) distinguishes stimulant dependence from other SUDs; functional recovery of dopamine system may require 12–24 months

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Acute crash: massive monoamine (dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine) depletion after supra-physiologic release; vesicular stores exhausted; synthesis cannot keep pace
- Protracted dysphoria: downregulation of dopamine D2/D3 receptors and DAT in striatum and prefrontal cortex; reduced glucose metabolism in nucleus accumbens persists weeks to months, correlating with amotivation
- Neuroimaging (PET): striatal dopamine transporter (DAT) density reduced ~20% at 1 month abstinence; partial recovery after 12–14 months protracted abstinence
- Indirect-pathway spiny projection neurons (iSPNs) in dorsomedial striatum show bidirectional intrinsic excitability changes — increased during acute withdrawal, decreased during protracted abstinence — contributing to dysregulated reward learning
- Prefrontal-striatal functional connectivity disruptions (resting-state fMRI) underlie mood instability and impaired impulse control lasting months after cessation; correlated with depression severity scores

## Clinical Picture
- Acute crash: hypersomnia (up to 18–20 h/day), hyperphagia, profound fatigue; lasts 1–3 days
- Withdrawal peak: severe anhedonia, depressed mood, irritability, anxiety, cognitive slowing; peaks at days 5–7, subsides over 2–4 weeks
- Suicidal ideation occurs in a clinically significant minority during acute withdrawal — requires active monitoring; meth users have 3× higher suicide attempt rates vs general population
- Protracted phase (weeks 4+): episodic craving triggered by cues, persistent mild anhedonia, sleep disruption, cognitive deficits in working memory and executive function
- ~59% of people with meth use disorder relapse within 1 year; anhedonia severity at discharge is a significant predictor of relapse

## Evidence & Treatment
- No FDA-approved medication for meth withdrawal or use disorder (as of 2026)
- ADAPT-2 RCT (NEJM 2021): extended-release naltrexone + bupropion 13.6% response rate vs 2.5% placebo (NNT ~9); only combination with replicated efficacy signal
- Mirtazapine: small RCTs show reduced meth use and blunted withdrawal severity; 2023 Cochrane-level meta-analysis found modest but consistent benefit
- Contingency management (CM): strongest evidence for behavioral treatment; abstinence rates 2–3× higher vs standard care; see meth-contingency-management.md
- Bupropion monotherapy: marginal benefit in low-severity users; insufficient for heavy/dependent users; not recommended as sole agent
- TAAR1 agonists (ulotaront, ralmitaront): emerging mechanism targeting dopamine dysregulation upstream; phase II trials ongoing — see meth-taar1-pharmacology.md

## Key Facts
- Acute crash onset: 12–24 h after last use; peak withdrawal: days 5–7
- DAT recovery: ~20% deficit at 1 month; approaches normalization by 12–14 months abstinence (London et al., PET data)
- Protracted abstinence syndrome duration: up to 24 months for full neurobiological recovery
- ADAPT-2 NNT: ~9 (naltrexone + bupropion combination)
- Relapse rate: ~59% within 12 months; anhedonia at discharge strongest behavioral predictor
