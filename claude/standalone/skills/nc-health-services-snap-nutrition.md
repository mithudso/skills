# NC SNAP-Ed, Food as Medicine, and Nutrition Security

## Overview

North Carolina administers federal food and nutrition assistance programs through the **NC DHHS Division of Child and Family Well-Being**, addressing food insecurity for low-income residents. In 2024–2025, these programs face significant federal restructuring through HR 1 legislation.

## SNAP in North Carolina

### Who Is Eligible
- US citizens and certain qualified non-citizens
- Income ≤130% of Federal Poverty Level (FPL) for gross income; 100% FPL for net income
- Resource limits (some households)
- Applied through NC DHHS ePASS system or county DSS offices

### NC SNAP by the Numbers (2024)
- SNAP benefits in NC do not cover the cost of a **moderately priced meal in any of NC's 100 counties** (per 2024 data) — benefits inadequate relative to food costs
- Administrative costs shared between federal government and state/counties

### Application
- Online: [ePASS](https://epass.nc.gov/)
- In-person: County Department of Social Services
- Phone: NC FAST system

## SNAP-Ed (Nutrition Education)

**SNAP-Ed** provides nutrition education to SNAP-eligible individuals (≤185% FPL):
- Classes on making healthy food choices on a limited budget
- Skills aligned with MyPlate and Dietary Guidelines for Americans
- Physical activity promotion
- Behavior change and cooking demonstrations
- Delivered through local health departments, cooperative extensions, CBOs

**Administered by**: NC DHHS Division of Child and Family Well-Being, with the NC Cooperative Extension and local health departments as key implementing partners.

**Critical 2025 update**: HR 1 (the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act," signed into law July 2025) **eliminated all funding for the SNAP Nutrition and Obesity Prevention Grant Program (SNAP-Ed)**. This represents a major program cut with statewide impact on nutrition education infrastructure.

## NC State Action Plan for Nutrition Security (2023–2024)

NC DHHS released a **State Action Plan for Nutrition Security 2023–2024** covering:
- Cross-program coordination (SNAP, WIC, school meals, CACFP, SFMNP)
- Screening and referral for food insecurity in healthcare settings
- Integration with Healthy Opportunities Pilots (food as medicine services — now suspended July 2025)
- Addressing food deserts in rural NC
- Culturally responsive nutrition services

## Food as Medicine: NC Context

NC Medicaid's **Healthy Opportunities Pilots** (2022–2025) were the primary vehicle for "food as medicine" in NC — medically tailored meals and food boxes covered by Medicaid. With HOP suspended July 2025, the food-as-medicine infrastructure has contracted significantly.

Remaining pathways for food-connected health interventions:
- **WIC** (Women, Infants, Children) — prescription-like food packages for specific nutritional needs
- **Medical nutrition therapy** (covered by Medicaid for diabetes, renal disease, eating disorders)
- **SNAP-Ed** programming (if funding restored or alternative sources secured)
- **Food bank partnerships** with FQHCs and health systems (informal)
- **Produce prescription programs** (local, grant-funded, not systematically Medicaid-covered without HOP)

## Federal Changes Under HR 1 (2025)

HR 1 ("One Big Beautiful Bill Act") enacted July 2025 introduces sweeping SNAP changes affecting NC:
- **SNAP-Ed eliminated** (federal grant program ended)
- **State matching funds requirement** increased — NCDHHS estimates **$420 million annual cost increase** for NC
- **Administrative cost shift**: NCDHHS estimates **$65 million state-level** + **$14 million county-level** increase
- Benefit levels and eligibility: Additional changes under review

These changes represent the most significant federal restructuring of SNAP in decades and place substantial new costs on NC state and county governments.

## SNAP and Health Outcomes

Evidence on SNAP and health:
- SNAP participation associated with reduced food insecurity, hospitalizations, and healthcare costs
- SNAP-Ed participation linked to improved dietary quality among participants
- In NC: food insecurity rates higher in rural counties, eastern NC, and among Black and Latino households
