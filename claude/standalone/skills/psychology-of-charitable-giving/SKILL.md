# Psychology of Charitable Giving

A spoke of the **applied-psychology** hub. Evidence-grounded, replication-honest
reference for the giving-specific empirical effects. Each construct flags effect size,
replication status, and active debates. The general mechanisms (framing, emotion
regulation, nudge theory) live in sibling spokes; this skill owns what is specific to
WHY people give, how much, and to whom.

## 1. Warm Glow and Impure Altruism

### The Andreoni Model (1990)

Andreoni's landmark paper proposed that donors are **impurely altruistic**: they give
partly because the outcome benefits others (pure altruism) and partly because of the
**warm glow** — intrinsic utility from the act of giving itself. Key implications:

- **Crowding-out failure:** If people gave only for outcomes (pure altruism), government
  grants to a cause should perfectly crowd out private donations. Empirically, crowding
  out is only partial — Andreoni & Payne (2011) find substantial but incomplete
  displacement, with a meaningful net-new giving residual attributable to the warm-glow
  component. (The precise cents-per-dollar figure varies by correction for reduced
  fundraising effort; do not cite a specific percentage without checking the paper.)
- **Policy difference:** Subsidies that reduce donors' own contribution cost can
  backfire if the warm glow requires the personal act of giving, not just the outcome.
- **Status quo:** Widely accepted in behavioral economics; tested in natural experiments
  (e.g., National Endowment for the Arts matching grants, UK Gift Aid analysis).

**Replication status:** Robust. The general pattern of partial crowding-out is one of
the most replicated findings in donation economics.

## 2. Identifiable Victim Effect and Singularity Effect

### Core Phenomenon

People give more to **identified, individualized victims** (photo, name, story) than to
statistically equivalent unidentified ones (Small, Loewenstein & Slovic, 2007). The
lab study gave participants $5 and offered donation to Save the Children: identified
child raised $2.83 on average; statistical framing ($1.17); adding statistics to the
story about the identified child also dropped giving, suggesting analytic thinking
suppresses affect-driven generosity.

### Singularity Effect

Kogut & Ritov (2005) found that a single identified victim raised more than an
identified group of victims, even controlling for identifiability. The singularity of
"one" amplifies attention and affect.

**Replication (2024):** Białek et al. (2024) published a close preregistered
replication of Kogut & Ritov Study 2 in *Judgment and Decision Making*, finding
the effect replicated with a smaller magnitude. Key boundary condition: effect found
in individualist cultures, weakened or reversed in collectivist contexts.

**Effect size:** Identifiability effects in this literature are modest — d ≈ 0.20–0.40
across studies (see Evangelidis & Van den Bergh 2013 on fatality-count scaling in aid);
the singularity effect is somewhat smaller and more fragile. Do not overstate:
these are context-dependent effects.

## 3. Compassion Fade, Psychic Numbing, and Pseudoinefficacy

These three constructs form a cluster around the "more victims = less compassion" paradox.

### Psychic Numbing (Slovic 2007)

Slovic's paper "'If I look at the mass I will never act': Psychic numbing and genocide"
(*Judgment and Decision Making*, 2007) demonstrated that emotional response diminishes
as victim number increases, following a psychophysical numbing function. In one study,
participants donated significantly more to one named child than to statistical millions.

**Mechanism:** The affect system responds to changes in state (0 → 1 victim) more
strongly than to large quantities. "The more who die, the less we care" reflects the
logarithmic nature of emotional response, not callousness.

### Compassion Fade

Västfjäll, Slovic, Mayorga & Peters (2014) empirically documented **compassion fade**:
both self-reported and psychophysiological indicators of compassion (skin conductance)
were highest for N=1 child, declined at N=2, and continued declining at N=8. Donors
in their studies gave more to 1 victim than to a group of 8, even when framed identically.

### Pseudoinefficacy

Slovic, Västfjäll & colleagues coined **pseudoinefficacy** (Västfjäll et al. 2015)
to describe the irrational reduction in giving when donors are reminded of the
vastness of the problem they cannot fully solve.
Showing that you can save 4,500 of 11,000 refugees reduced donations relative to simply
showing the 4,500 who CAN be saved — even though saving 4,500 is objectively better
than saving zero.

**Practical implication:** Effective appeals focus on who CAN be helped, not the scale
of the unmet need. "Drop in the bucket" framing is counterproductive.

**Replication status:** Lab effects are well-replicated; field translation is active
area of research with mixed results at scale.

## 4. Empathy–Altruism Hypothesis

### Batson's Core Claim

C. Daniel Batson (1981; monograph 1991) proposed that empathic concern — feeling FOR
another as opposed to feeling WITH them — produces genuinely **altruistic motivation**
whose ultimate goal is the other's welfare, not self-benefit. (The 1981 original
experiments appeared in Batson et al., *JPSP, 40*(2), 290–302; the full theoretical
statement is in the 1991 Erlbaum monograph cited in References.)

**Key experimental design:** High-empathy conditions were created by instructing
participants to imagine the target's feelings. Egoistic accounts predicted people
would only help when helping reduced their own aversive state or when social
evaluation pressure was present. Batson found:

- High-empathy subjects helped even when **escape was easy** (disconfirming
  aversive-arousal reduction)
- High-empathy subjects helped even when **no one would know** (disconfirming
  reputation/social punishment accounts)
- Low-empathy subjects helped only under conditions predicted by egoism

### Egoistic Alternatives

- **Negative-state relief model** (Cialdini et al. 1987): Helping reduces the
  helper's negative mood. Predicts helping only when the negative state is salient
  and relief is achievable by other means. Tested extensively against Batson.
- **Empathy-specific reward seeking:** People might anticipate the positive feeling
  of helping ("empathic joy hypothesis"). Batson's tests find this insufficient.

**Verdict:** Batson's body of work is the strongest evidence for genuine altruism
in humans. But: (a) egoistic interpretations cannot be fully ruled out; (b) most
studies use lab proxies for charitable giving, not real donations. The empathy–altruism
debate is philosophically live (Batson & Shaw 1991); practically, *empathy reliably
increases prosocial behavior* regardless of which account is ultimately correct.

**Replication status:** Meta-analysis (Eisenberg & Miller 1987) found consistent
positive empathy–prosocial correlations. Individual Batson studies use relatively
small samples; recent replication concerns exist but no large-scale failed replication.

## 5. Martyrdom Effect

### Olivola & Shafir (2013)

In five experiments, Olivola and Shafir demonstrated that **anticipated pain and
effort increase willingness to donate** and sponsor others who endure pain for a cause.
Participants asked to choose between a charity picnic and a charity 5-mile run intended
to donate ~$23.87 for the run vs. ~$13.88 for the picnic — nearly double.

**Mechanism:** Effort signals commitment and confers meaning. A painful act for a
cause communicates sincere dedication, amplifying perceived value of the contribution.

**Cause type moderates the effect:** Martyrdom amplifies giving when the cause involves
alleviating suffering. When the cause involves enjoyment/celebration, the easy option
can raise more.

**Extension:** Olivola & Shafir (2013) also found that observing others endure effort
for a cause increases bystander donations — the intuition behind sponsored challenge
fundraising, though field replication at scale remains limited.

**Replication:** Bou Zeineddine & Bagusat (2023, registered report via Peer Community
in Registered Reports) replicated the core martyrdom effect across three studies,
finding it held but with smaller and more variable effect sizes than the original.
Treat as a real but modest and context-conditional effect.

## 6. Overhead Aversion and the Overhead Myth

### Gneezy et al. (2014, Science)

Donors systematically penalize charities with higher overhead (administrative and
fundraising costs), even when those charities achieve greater impact per dollar.
Gneezy, Keenan & Gneezy (2014) ran a field experiment with 40,000 potential donors:

- **Control:** Standard charity appeal.
- **Treatment:** Overhead costs pre-paid by a lead donor; all donations go directly
  to beneficiaries.
- **Result:** Donation rate roughly **doubled** in the treatment condition.

**Mechanism:** Overhead aversion is explained by multiple overlapping accounts:
warm-glow donors want their specific dollars to "reach" beneficiaries; outcome-impact
donors misuse overhead ratio as a proxy for effectiveness; self-signal donors want
to avoid being seen as naive.

### The Overhead Myth

Caviola, Schubert & Greene (2021, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) catalog overhead
aversion as one of several obstacles to effective altruism. Overhead ratio is in fact
a **poor proxy** for effectiveness — many highly effective organizations have above-
average overhead. GiveWell's top charities often have substantial coordination costs.

**Implication:** "Overhead-free" framing (pre-seeded admin funds from a lead donor)
can dramatically increase donation rates. But it does not necessarily redirect donors
to the most impactful causes.

**Replication status:** Field experiment effects are robust; lab manipulations also
show the bias reliably. One of the most actionable findings in donation psychology.

## 7. Moral Identity, Self-Signaling, and In-Group/Parochial Altruism

### Moral Identity

Aquino & Reed (2002) developed the moral identity construct: the degree to which
being a moral person is central to one's self-concept. Two dimensions:

- **Internalization:** Private, self-concept integration.
- **Symbolization:** Public behavioral expression of moral identity.

Research (Aquino et al. 2009; Winterich et al. 2009) finds symbolization more
reliably predicts charitable donations than internalization, suggesting reputation
and self-expression motives partly drive moral-identity-linked giving.

### Self-Signaling

Bodner & Prelec (2003) introduced the **self-signaling framework**: people give
partly to maintain a positive self-image as a generous person. This can be:

- Triggered by public observability (social signaling to others)
- Active even privately (diagnostic value of one's own choices as evidence of
  one's character)

**Warning — moral licensing:** Prior moral acts can license subsequent non-giving
("I already gave"). This is well-documented but contested in strength: meta-analyses
find the licensing effect is small and requires specific conditions (Blanken et al.
2015).

### Parochial Altruism

Giving is strongly in-group biased: people give more to causes affecting their
nation, community, family, or perceived in-group (Bernhard et al. 2006). Geographic
proximity, perceived similarity, and shared identity all increase donation rates.
Buchan, Brewer & Johnson (2011) demonstrate the converse — that global social
identity can reduce parochialism — confirming the baseline parochial default by
showing it requires active identity expansion to overcome.

**Policy implication:** Cause campaigns that make beneficiaries feel closer (through
narrative, shared identity cues) can partially offset in-group bias.

## 8. Prosocial Message Framing and Emotional Appeals

### Gain vs. Loss Framing

Loss framing ("X children will die without your help") vs. gain framing ("your
donation saves X children"). General prospect-theory prediction is loss framing
superiority. In charitable giving, **evidence is mixed** — loss frames can backfire
by inducing helplessness rather than action. Effect sizes are small and context-
dependent; O'Keefe & Jensen's (2007) meta-analysis of gain-loss framing in disease
prevention found no reliable loss advantage (d < 0.20); a subsequent meta-analysis
by Gallagher & Updegraff (2012) across health and prosocial domains found similarly
small and inconsistent framing effects.

### EPPM — Extended Parallel Process Model (Witte 1994)

Fear appeals work through two processes: **danger control** (adaptive behavior
change) and **fear control** (defensive responses that protect the ego rather than
change behavior). Key prediction:
- High threat + high efficacy → danger control → action (e.g., donation)
- High threat + low efficacy → fear control → defensive avoidance

For charitable giving: fear appeals about a crisis work ONLY when donors believe
their contribution will actually make a difference (efficacy). Without efficacy
beliefs, fear can suppress giving.

### Guilt, Hope, Pride, and Anger

Meta-analytic work (Xu & Guo 2018; Septianto, Kemper & Northey 2020) on emotional appeal type:

- **Guilt:** Effective when linked to personal responsibility; direct path to
  donation intention, but risks reactance if perceived as manipulative.
- **Hope:** Effective for inspiring action and increasing openness to a cause,
  particularly when paired with a concrete efficacy message; sadness + hope
  combinations often outperform hope alone in donation contexts (Xu & Guo 2018).
- **Pride:** Can motivate giving as an affirmation of identity ("generous people
  like you give"); weaker effect than guilt in most studies.
- **Anger:** Can mobilize action when a clear antagonist is identified (injustice
  framing); mostly studied in political donation, less in humanitarian causes.

### Narrative Transportation vs. Statistics

A consistent lab finding: **a story about one person raises more donations than
statistics about thousands** (Small, Loewenstein & Slovic 2007). The proposed mechanism is narrative
transportation — immersion in a story suspends analytic processing and activates
emotion.

**But:** Empirical results in field settings are less consistent. Experimental
work (e.g., Feeley, Marshall & Reinhart 2006; Winterbottom et al. 2008) finds
that story-plus-statistics combinations can match or outperform pure narrative,
particularly when audience numeracy is high. Green & Brock (2000) established
narrative transportation theory; subsequent charitable-giving applications show
variable effects depending on victim proximity, specificity, and audience type.

**Analytic mindset warning:** Prompting analytical thinking before an appeal
(including adding statistics to an emotional appeal) consistently suppresses
affect-driven donations. This is the core finding from Small et al. (2007) and
has been replicated.

## 9. Nudge Effects: Replication Status

### Mertens et al. 2022 (PNAS)

Meta-analysis of 447 effect sizes from 212 publications across behavioral domains
(health, food, environment, finance, prosocial/charity, other):

- Overall: Cohen's d = 0.43 (moderate)
- After publication-bias correction: d = 0.31
- **Prosocial domain specifically:** Smaller than food/health; exact corrected
  value varies by correction method.

### The Bayesian Challenge

Van Gestel et al. (2022, same PNAS issue via correspondence) apply Bayesian bias
correction and argue that after accounting for publication bias, **no robust positive
evidence remains** for nudge effectiveness across domains. This is contested — the
answer partly depends on correction method assumptions.

**Bottom line for charitable giving defaults:**
- Default opt-in donation programs show real effects in **deployed field settings**;
  participation rates are typically higher under opt-out than opt-in designs,
  though average gift amounts per donor may be lower — a participation-amount
  trade-off worth assessing before deploying any default.
- Lab default effects tend to overestimate field effects.
- The "default to donate" effect is more robust than generic nudge effect sizes
  suggest when the context matches the implementation.
