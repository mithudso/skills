# NC Mental Health Defenses & Specialized Courts

> **Educational only — NOT legal advice.** For any specific mental health defense or veterans court matter, consult a licensed North Carolina attorney.

North Carolina criminal courts intersect with mental health law in several distinct ways: competency to stand trial, the insanity defense (NGRI), diminished capacity, involuntary commitment after acquittal, and specialized diversion through veterans treatment courts. Each pathway has its own statutory framework, procedural requirements, and strategic considerations.

`verified-as-of: 2026-06-22`

## 1. Competency to Stand Trial (Capacity to Proceed)

### Legal Standard (GS 15A-1001)

No person may be tried, convicted, sentenced, or punished for a crime when — by reason of **mental illness or defect** — they are unable to: [^1]
1. Understand the **nature and object** of the proceedings
2. Comprehend their **own situation** in reference to the proceedings
3. **Assist in their defense** in a rational or reasonable manner

This is a constitutional minimum rooted in *Dusky v. United States* (1960) and *Drope v. Missouri* (1975). "Mental illness or defect" is not limited to diagnosed psychiatric conditions — intellectual disability, dementia, or serious neurological impairment can qualify.

### Raising the Issue (GS 15A-1002)

Capacity may be raised **at any time** by:
- The prosecutor
- The defendant
- Defense counsel
- The court sua sponte

The motion must **detail specific conduct** that leads the moving party to question capacity. Defense counsel has an independent obligation to raise the issue when there is genuine doubt.

### Evaluation Process

NC uses a tiered evaluation system: [^2]

| Case Type | Evaluator | Setting |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Misdemeanors; most felonies | Local Certified Forensic Evaluator (LCFE) — through the LME-MCO | Outpatient |
| Complex felonies; LCFE recommends further evaluation | Central Regional Hospital (CRH), Butner | Inpatient (up to 60 days) |
| Court-ordered second opinion | CRH | Inpatient |

**CRH Forensic Services** conducts approximately 900 evaluations per year; LCFEs handle an additional ~1,700 annually. Evaluations are by licensed doctoral-level psychologists and psychiatrists.

**Report timeline:** GS 15A-1002(b) requires the evaluating facility to submit its report promptly; courts typically schedule capacity hearings within 60–90 days of the evaluation order.

### Capacity Hearing

The court holds a hearing (with defendant present) to determine capacity. Evidence from the evaluator's report is central; both sides may present expert testimony. The court must find the defendant incapable by a **preponderance of the evidence** to halt proceedings.

### Restoration (GS 15A-1003)

If found incapable, the court must order one of:
1. **Commitment to a state psychiatric facility** for restoration treatment
2. **Conditional release** with required outpatient treatment (appropriate for less serious charges or when community treatment is sufficient)

**Restoration programs (2022–2025 pilots):** NC launched six restoration pilot programs:
- Three **detention-based** programs (Mecklenburg, Pitt, Wake counties) — treatment in jail
- Three **community-based** programs (Cumberland, Wake, Mecklenburg counties) — outpatient restoration

Maximum commitment for restoration in felony cases: may not exceed the maximum sentence for the charged offense.

**If restoration fails:** If the defendant is found unlikely to ever regain capacity, the court must dismiss the charges and order civil commitment proceedings under GS 122C-268 (if the defendant remains dangerous).

### Iryna's Law — Impact on Competency Dismissals (SL 2025-93)

Effective December 1, 2025, GS 15A-1008 was amended to make dismissals of charges against defendants found incapable to proceed **ineligible** for expunction under GS 15A-146. This means a violent defendant's dismissed charges remain on record after incapacity dismissal, even if all other criteria for automatic expunction would be met. See [nc-expungement-record-sealing](nc-expungement-record-sealing.md).

## 2. Not Guilty by Reason of Insanity (NGRI)

### Legal Standard — M'Naghten Test

NC uses the **M'Naghten Rule** as its insanity standard — among the most restrictive available. A defendant is not responsible for their acts if, at the time of committing the act, by reason of mental illness or defect, they: [^3]
- Did not know the nature and quality of the act; **or**
- Did not know the act was wrong

**Critical limitation:** Insanity based solely on **voluntary intoxication** (alcohol or drugs) is NOT a valid defense, even if it produced a psychotic state. The mental illness must be independent of or at least not solely caused by voluntary substance use.

### Pleading and Notice Requirements (GS 15A-959)

- Defense must file **written notice** of intent to raise insanity **no later than 20 days** before trial (or such other time as the court orders)
- Notice triggers the State's right to an independent mental health examination
- Defense must simultaneously file notice of any intent to use expert testimony on diminished capacity

**Defendant must consent:** Defense counsel cannot present the insanity defense over the defendant's objection — it is the defendant's right to control this decision. [^4]

### NGRI Verdict and Mandatory Commitment (GS 15A-1321)

A verdict of "not guilty by reason of insanity" is not a simple acquittal. The judge **must** enter a commitment order: [^3]

- If the crime involved **serious physical injury or death**: mandatory commitment to **Central Regional Hospital** (Butner)
- Other crimes: commitment to appropriate state mental health facility

**Initial hearing:** Within 50 days of commitment, a hearing must determine whether continued commitment is warranted.

**Burden for release:** The committed person bears the burden to prove **by a preponderance of the evidence** that they are no longer dangerous to others.

NGRI is uncommon in NC practice — it is rarely raised and succeeds in a small fraction of cases where raised. The risk of indefinite commitment often makes it a less attractive option than competency-related strategies.

## 3. Diminished Capacity

NC recognizes **diminished capacity** (sometimes called "diminished responsibility") as a partial defense — it does not result in acquittal but may negate the specific mental state (mens rea) required for the charged offense.

### How It Works

Expert testimony about a mental disease, defect, or condition may be offered to show the defendant **lacked the specific intent** or other required mental state. For example:
- A charge of first-degree murder requires premeditation and deliberation — a mental condition causing impulsive, unplanned behavior may negate this
- A charge of embezzlement requires specific intent to defraud — a delusional disorder may negate this

### Notice Required (GS 15A-905, 15A-959)

- If the defendant intends to offer expert testimony on diminished capacity, written notice must be filed **within a reasonable time before trial**
- The State may request a court-ordered examination to rebut the defense

### Limitations

- Diminished capacity does NOT negate general intent crimes
- Voluntary intoxication is **not** diminished capacity
- The jury may accept diminished capacity evidence and convict on a lesser offense (e.g., second-degree murder instead of first-degree)

## 4. Involuntary Commitment from Criminal Court (GS 122C-268)

When criminal charges are dismissed because a defendant is found incapable to proceed and unlikely to regain capacity, and the defendant poses a danger to others, the court initiates civil commitment proceedings under GS Chapter 122C:

- The court issues a **temporary custody order** directing law enforcement to take the person into custody
- A commitment hearing under GS 122C-268 is held
- The standard is whether the person is **mentally ill** AND a **danger to self or others**
- Commitment is in a state or private psychiatric facility (not a jail or prison)
- Continued commitment requires periodic judicial review

This pathway is distinct from NGRI commitment (GS 15A-1321) — it is civil, not criminal, and does not require a trial or verdict.

## 5. Veterans Treatment Courts (GS 7A-272)

### Statutory Framework

NC veterans treatment courts (VTCs) are "therapeutic courts" under **GS 7A-272(f)** — defined as courts where a defendant is ordered to participate in activities addressing substance abuse and mental illness as a **condition of probation** or under a **deferred prosecution agreement** (GS 15A-1341). [^5]

The STRONG Veterans Act referenced in the brief is a **federal** statute (signed 2022) addressing VA mental health services — the NC statutory authority for VTCs is GS 7A-272, not a state "STRONG Act."

### Eligibility

- **Any prior military service** — the only NC statutory requirement
- Broader than the federal "veteran" definition: includes those with other-than-honorable discharges (who may seek discharge upgrades through volunteer legal assistance)
- Target population: justice-involved veterans with **PTSD, TBI, substance use disorder, or other service-connected mental health conditions**
- Typically available for nonviolent offenses (counties set eligibility criteria)

### Program Structure

- **Frequency:** Defendants appear in court approximately every other week, providing military-familiar structure and accountability
- **Team approach:** Judge, prosecutor, defense attorney, probation officer, law enforcement, VA liaison, court coordinator
- **Veteran mentor:** Each participant is matched with a veteran mentor
- **Graduated sanctions and rewards:** Non-compliance triggers escalating consequences; milestones earn recognition
- **Services connection:** Courts connect participants to VA healthcare, treatment, housing, and educational benefits

### Outcomes and Disposition

- Successful completion typically results in **dismissal of charges** (under deferred prosecution) or **early termination of probation**
- Failure may result in prosecution proceeding on the original charges

### Operating Courts

VTCs operate primarily near major VA medical centers. Courts have operated in Harnett, Cumberland, Durham, Buncombe counties and others. Funding has come from Governor's Crime Commission grants. [^5]

### Relationship to Drug Treatment Courts

VTCs are a specialty track within NC Recovery Courts (GS 7A Article 62). A veteran with primarily substance abuse issues may participate in either a VTC or a standard adult treatment court, depending on county availability. See [nc-drug-treatment-courts](nc-drug-treatment-courts.md).

## 6. Defense Strategy Overview

| Situation | Recommended Pathway |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Client unable to understand proceedings or assist counsel | Raise capacity — GS 15A-1002 motion; request evaluation |
| Client has serious mental illness AND lacked knowledge of wrongfulness at time of offense | Consider NGRI — GS 15A-959 notice; obtain forensic evaluation |
| Client's mental illness negated specific intent but not general intent | Diminished capacity — expert notice under GS 15A-905 |
| Client is a veteran with PTSD/TBI driving criminal behavior | Veterans Treatment Court — contact VTC coordinator |
| Client incapable and dangerous, charges must be dismissed | Civil commitment — GS 122C-268 petition |

## Key Statutes

- **GS 15A-1001** — No trial when defendant incapable to proceed
- **GS 15A-1002** — Procedures for determining capacity; evaluation; restoration
- **GS 15A-1003** — Commitment for restoration
- **GS 15A-959** — Notice of insanity defense; pretrial determination
- **GS 15A-1321** — NGRI verdict; mandatory commitment
- **GS 15A-905** — Discovery for mental health defenses
- **GS 7A-272** — Veterans treatment courts (therapeutic courts framework)
- **GS 122C-268** — Involuntary commitment procedures (civil)
