# Character Arc Frameworks

A character arc is not a layer of emotional decoration applied to a finished
plot; it is the load-bearing structure that gives the plot's events meaning.
The frameworks below treat the arc as an engineering problem: a character
holds a false belief that once protected them and now limits them, the
external plot exists to apply pressure to that belief until it breaks or
hardens, and the resulting change (or refusal to change) is what makes the
climax feel earned rather than merely resolved. Skip these frameworks and a
book can still have events, but it will read as things happening to a
character rather than a character being changed by what happens — the most
common reason competent plots feel hollow.

## Core Concepts

### 1. The Lie/Want/Need model

Popularized by K. M. Weiland in *Creating Character Arcs*, this model gives
the arc three interlocking parts. The **Lie** is a false belief the
character holds about themselves or the world, often invisible to the
character and sometimes to the reader until it is named late in the story.
The **Want** is the external, visible goal the character pursues, and it is
usually a direct symptom of the Lie — the character wants the thing the Lie
tells them will finally make them safe, validated, or whole. The **Need** is
the Truth that would dissolve the Lie, and it is usually invisible to the
character at the start and sometimes at odds with the Want, which is what
generates internal conflict: pursuing the Want and accepting the Need can
require mutually exclusive choices.[^1]

Worked example: a character believes "if I am ever vulnerable, I will be
abandoned" (the Lie), so she wants career success and total self-sufficiency
(the Want), because independence promises to make abandonment impossible.
What she needs is to accept that vulnerability is the price of connection,
not its opposite (the Need). The plot's job is to keep putting her in
situations where self-sufficiency and connection cannot both be chosen,
forcing the Lie and the Truth into direct competition rather than letting
her hold both indefinitely.

Use this model at the outline stage, before drafting scenes, because the
Lie determines what the Want should be (it must be a plausible symptom of
that specific Lie, not an arbitrary goal) and the Need determines what the
climax has to cost. A Lie/Want/Need triad that doesn't interlock — where the
Want has no logical connection to the Lie, or the Need could be accepted
without giving anything up — produces an arc that resolves on schedule but
doesn't feel to the reader like it was ever really at stake.

### 2. Positive, negative, and flat arc taxonomy

Weiland's typology names three shapes an arc can take, and picking the wrong
one for a given story is a common structural mistake.[^2]

**Positive change arc.** The character overcomes the Lie and accepts the
Truth by the climax, growing in the process. This is the default arc for
most commercial and genre fiction, and the shape readers most readily
recognize.

**Flat (testing) arc.** The character already holds the Truth, essentially
intact, from the opening pages, and the story tests whether that Truth can
survive pressure and whether it can change the world or other characters
around the protagonist rather than the protagonist themselves. A flat arc
is not "no arc" — it fails as flat and reads as static if the Truth is never
seriously tested, or if the surrounding world doesn't visibly shift because
of the character's constancy. Most series leads (a recurring detective, a
franchise hero) run flat arcs across individual installments, since a
character who fully transforms every book has nowhere left to go.

**Negative change arc**, with three named variants that differ in what the
character does with the Truth when it's offered:[^3]

- *Disillusionment*: the character sheds the Lie and reaches a real Truth,
  but the Truth turns out to be bleak or costly rather than redemptive —
  change happens, but it is not uplifting.
- *Fall*: the character is offered the Truth and refuses it, clinging to
  the Lie until it destroys them — the classic tragic structure.
- *Corruption*: the character starts near the Truth (or actively holding
  it) and is gradually persuaded to abandon it for a Lie, ending up worse
  than they began — the villain-origin shape.

Worked example: the same setup — a surgeon whose Lie is "control keeps
people alive" — supports three different books. Positive: she learns to
delegate and a patient survives because she did. Flat: she never abandons
the belief, and the story is about a hospital administration that finally
learns to build systems around her instead of fighting her; the world
changes, not her. Fall: she refuses every offered chance to loosen control
and the cost lands on someone she loves. The external events can stay
similar; the arc type is decided by what the character does with the Truth
when it's offered.

When to use which: pick positive when the story's argument is that growth
is possible and worth the cost; pick flat when the story's argument is
about the cost and reward of conviction under pressure, or when writing a
long-running series lead; pick a negative variant when the story is making
a cautionary argument, and choose the specific variant based on whether the
Truth is reached-but-grim (disillusionment), refused (fall), or actively
traded away (corruption). Naming the intended arc type before drafting
prevents the common failure of writing a positive-arc climax onto a
negative-arc setup, which reads as an unearned tonal reversal.

### 3. Arc-plot interlock

The arc and the plot are not two parallel tracks that happen to finish at
the same time; the external plot's job is to be the specific mechanism that
forces the internal change. Michael Hauge frames this as the difference
between a character's **identity** (the false self built to protect against
re-experiencing an old wound) and their **essence** (who they are
underneath it), with the **outer motivation** — the visible plot goal — as
the vehicle that drags the character toward essence whether they intend it
to or not. The rule Hauge gives is that the closer the character gets to
achieving the outer goal, the more the plot should force them to abandon
the protective identity to get there, so pursuing the Want and approaching
the Need become the same forward motion rather than a subplot bolted onto
the A-plot.[^4]

Worked example: if the Want is "win the case" and the Need is "let someone
else carry the burden," the plot must make winning the case require asking
for help at the exact moment the character least wants to — a courtroom
crisis that can only be solved by trusting a partner. If instead the case
resolves through the character's unilateral brilliance, the plot has let
the character win without confronting the Lie, and the "growth" has to be
asserted in a stray line of dialogue rather than demonstrated in what
happens. The diagnostic question for any major plot beat: does this event
require the character to act on the Lie or act on the Truth? If a beat
could go either way without changing the outcome, that beat isn't doing arc
work yet.

Use this check throughout revision, not just at the climax: every major
plot turn is an opportunity to make the external stakes and the internal
stakes the same choice, and beats that don't move that needle are candidates
for cutting or re-motivating. See
`references/plot-structure-and-story-beats.md` for the beat-by-beat
mechanics this interlock sits on top of.

### 4. The ghost/wound backstory

A Lie needs a plausible origin, or it reads as an arbitrary personality
trait rather than a wound. John Truby coined the term **Ghost** for this: a
specific past event, often shown only in fragments, that still haunts the
character in the present and precedes the story's own inciting
incident.[^4] Weiland's *Creating Character Arcs* vocabulary keeps the
Ghost (the event) distinct from the **Wound**
(the psychological pain that event left behind), treating both as upstream
of the Lie in a causal chain: the Ghost happens, the Wound is the resulting
hurt, and the Lie is the belief the character adopts to keep the Wound from
recurring — a chain compatible with Michael Hauge's parallel use of "wound"
for the past hurt that produces a character's protective identity.[^1][^4]
For craft purposes the terminology matters less than the causal
requirement: whichever label is used, the backstory event has to
specifically explain the content of the Lie, not just gesture at "a
difficult past." The craft requirement is specificity: "she had a hard
childhood" is not a Ghost, but "her mother left without warning the week
she made the swim team, and she has never told anyone the meet was the same
day" is, because it explains both the Lie's content (attachment is
conditional on achievement) and its persistence (the character has built a
coping identity around never needing anyone to stay).

The Ghost does not need to be fully dramatized on the page — a single
well-placed flashback or a character's carefully avoided subject can do
more work than a chapter of backstory — but the writer needs to know it in
detail even when withholding it, because every scene where the character
acts on the Lie should be consistent with that specific originating wound.
When the Ghost is vague, the Lie tends to drift inconsistently across the
manuscript because there's no anchor determining what the character would
and wouldn't do under pressure.

### 5. Arc beat placement within structure

Arc beats are not evenly spaced; they cluster at the same structural
turning points that plot frameworks already name, because the internal and
external stories are meant to be forced together at those junctures.[^6] In
a standard three-act shape: Act One establishes the Lie and the Want inside
the character's ordinary world, often before the character or reader can
name the Lie explicitly; the First Plot Point is the point where the
external plot first makes the Lie untenable to keep ignoring; the Midpoint
typically delivers a partial or false revelation — a moment where the
character glimpses the Truth but isn't yet willing or able to act on it;
the Third Plot Point (often a "dark night of the soul" beat, sometimes
mapped onto the low point in beat-sheet frameworks) is where the Lie
appears to have won, stripping away the character's remaining defenses; and
the Climax is where the character finally chooses between the Want and the
Need under maximum pressure, which is why the choice made there is what
readers experience as the arc's payoff.

Worked example: the surgeon whose Lie is "control keeps people alive"
spends Act One over-scheduling herself alone in the ordinary world; the
First Plot Point is the case that proves solo control has limits; the
Midpoint gives her a glimpse of what delegating could look like, which she
dismisses as too risky; the Third Plot Point is a failure that appears to
confirm delegating was the mistake, stripping her down to the Lie's purest
form; and the Climax forces the same choice one last time, at higher
stakes, where choosing to delegate is what saves the patient.

When to use this placement: treat it as the default scaffolding when
outlining a positive or negative change arc, and use it diagnostically in
revision — if the manuscript's midpoint contains no partial revelation, or
the low point doesn't meaningfully strip the character's defenses, the
arc's structural beats are likely undersupported even if individual scenes
read well in isolation.

This placement is a strong convention, not a fixed law: flat arcs
redistribute this shape (the Truth is present from Act One and the
turning points test rather than deliver it), and negative arcs invert the
climax (the character chooses the Lie, or the Truth arrives too late to
save them). Writers using a different beat-sheet vocabulary (Save the Cat's
"Dark Night of the Soul" beat, the Hero's Journey's "Ordeal") are naming the
same structural slot; see `references/plot-structure-and-story-beats.md`
for those frameworks in full, and treat this section as the layer that maps
internal-arc function onto those same positions rather than a competing
timeline.

### 6. Ensemble and multi-character arc management

Not every character in a cast needs — or can sustain — a fully developed
Lie/Want/Need arc; treating arc depth as binary (full arc or no
characterization at all) causes two common failures: an overstuffed novel
where every named character gets an origin wound and a transformation,
diluting all of them, or a cast where only the protagonist feels like a
person. The working discipline used in ensemble and multi-POV writing is to
assign a small number of full arcs (usually the protagonist and one or two
others whose internal change is structurally necessary to the plot's
resolution) and give the remaining named characters a narrower unit of
characterization: a clear function in the story, a consistent voice, and
optionally a single, smaller arc-like beat (a change in one relationship,
one belief revised) rather than a complete Lie-to-Truth journey.[^5]

Worked example: in a heist-crew ensemble, the planner might carry the book's
one full arc (learning to trust the crew instead of controlling every
variable), while the getaway driver's entire characterization need only be a
consistent voice, a clear skill, and one small moment of loyalty that pays
off a single earlier setup — not a wound, a Want, and a Need of her own. The
diagnostic question when deciding how much arc a character needs: does this
character's internal state change the plot's outcome, or would swapping
them for a competent stand-in leave the ending intact? If the latter, a
full arc is likely wasted effort better spent deepening the arcs that are
load-bearing.

## Anti-Patterns

**The arc-less protagonist.** A character who wants the same thing in the
same way from page one to the last page, with no Lie ever identified or
tested, tends to read as a passive camera moving through events rather than
a person the events happen to. Diagnose by trying to state the character's
Lie in one sentence; if no candidate sentence survives scrutiny, the arc
was never designed. Fix at the outline stage using the Lie/Want/Need model
in Concept 1, not by patching in a reflective paragraph near the end.

**Arc that contradicts plot events.** The character is told to have grown,
but the plot's events didn't require or reward that growth — the climax
could resolve identically whether or not the internal change occurred. This
is the failure mode Concept 3 exists to prevent: diagnose it by asking
whether the climax's outcome depends on the character acting on the Truth
rather than the Lie, and fix it by re-engineering the climax so the two are
inseparable, not by adding an epilogue that asserts the change happened.

**Unearned or sudden change.** A reversal that arrives in a single clean
scene with no accumulated pressure reads as a plot update rather than a
psychological event, whether the belief being reversed is small (a single
scene's opinion) or the book's central Lie. This file addresses only the
arc-length version of that failure — insufficient structural pressure across
acts, per Concepts 2 and 5; the scene-level mechanics of how a single belief
reversal should be dramatized (dissonance, resistance, cost) are covered in
full in `references/character-psychology-portrayal.md` and are not repeated
here.

**Every-character-gets-a-full-arc bloat.** Giving every named character a
Ghost, a Lie, a Want, and a Need regardless of their structural weight
dilutes the arcs that matter and inflates the manuscript with
characterization the plot never uses. Diagnose with the swap test in
Concept 6 (would removing this character's internal journey change the
ending?) and fix by demoting non-load-bearing characters to function, voice,
and at most one small arc-like beat.
