# The Yale Approach & Inoculation Theory

The "who says what to whom" engineering of a persuasive message, plus how to make
an attitude **resistant** to future attack.

## Yale attitude-change approach (Hovland, Janis & Kelley, 1950s)
The original systematic research program on persuasive communication, born from
WWII propaganda/morale studies. Frame: **"who (source) says what (message) to whom
(audience)"**, through which channel.

### The yielding sequence
Persuasion proceeds through stages — **attention → comprehension → acceptance
(yielding) → retention** — and a message must clear each. A brilliant argument that
isn't attended to or comprehended never reaches yielding. (McGuire later expanded
this into his information-processing chain.)

### The four clusters of variables
- **Source.** **Credibility = expertise × trustworthiness.** High-credibility
  sources persuade more *initially*. Attractiveness and similarity add peripheral
  pull (cf. ELM peripheral cues).
- **Message.**
  - **One-sided vs two-sided.** **Two-sided** messages (present and refute the
    opposing view) beat one-sided for **informed audiences** and those **initially
    opposed**, and they confer **resistance** to later counter-persuasion. One-sided
    works for already-agreeing, less-informed audiences.
  - **Fear appeals.** Moderate fear can work, but **only paired with an efficacy
    path** (a clear, doable response). Fear without efficacy triggers denial/avoidance
    (later formalized as the Extended Parallel Process Model).
  - **Order effects** (primacy vs recency), explicit vs implicit conclusions, and
    quantity/quality of arguments.
- **Channel.** Medium effects (video > audio > text for likeable/credible sources;
  text aids comprehension of complex arguments).
- **Audience.** Prior attitudes, intelligence, self-esteem, and involvement moderate
  everything (this is where the Yale program hands off to ELM/HSM).

### The sleeper effect
A counter-intuitive durability finding: a message from a **low-credibility source**
(or accompanied by a **discounting cue**) can show **increased** persuasive impact
**over time**. Mechanism: the **dissociation** of the message from its discounting
cue — memory for the cue decays faster than memory for the content, so the initially
suppressed message "wakes up." Practical caution: a message you discounted in the
room can resurface later detached from the reason you dismissed it. Don't assume one
rebuttal permanently kills an idea.

## Inoculation theory (McGuire, 1961/1964)
The biological metaphor: just as a weakened virus primes the immune system, a
**weakened dose of a counterargument plus refutation** primes an attitude to resist
a later, stronger attack.

### The two active ingredients
1. **Threat (forewarning).** Telling the person their attitude *will be attacked*
   creates a sense of vulnerability that **motivates** them to defend it. Threat is
   the motivational trigger — without it, inoculation doesn't take.
2. **Refutational preemption.** Pre-exposing them to the counterarguments **and
   modeling the rebuttals** gives them practiced defenses (counterarguing material)
   to deploy when the real attack lands.

Inoculation builds **broad** resistance — it works even against counterarguments not
specifically pre-refuted (a generalized "immunity"). It's most valuable for
protecting attitudes the person already holds but hasn't had to defend (analogous to
"germ-free" attitudes that have never been challenged and are therefore fragile).

### Modern prebunking and its honest limits
Inoculation is the basis of **prebunking** against misinformation (Roozenbeek & van
der Linden; the *Bad News* game; short "prebunk" videos). Evidence base:
- Banas & Rains's (2010) meta-analysis found inoculation produces **reliable
  resistance** to persuasion.
- Recent field work shows prebunking can improve misinformation discernment across
  cultures.
- **Caveat (Pennycook et al., 2024):** psychological inoculation (and accuracy
  nudges) **alone** did **not** reliably help people separate *manipulative* from
  *non-manipulative* content in some studies — effects can reflect a general skepticism
  shift rather than precise discernment, and they **decay** without boosters. Present
  inoculation as reliable-but-not-magic, and plan **booster** exposures.

## Operator translation (durability and pre-arming)
- **Lead with credibility, but mind the sleeper effect** — and remember initial
  discounting may not last.
- **Use two-sided messages** for informed or skeptical stakeholders: name the
  counterargument and refute it. It persuades the skeptical audience *and* hardens the
  resulting attitude.
- **Inoculate your champion before a hostile internal review.** Before they walk into
  the room with skeptics, (1) forewarn them which objections they'll hear (threat) and
  (2) rehearse the rebuttals (refutational preemption). This is the single most
  practical use of the theory in change management — it's why a champion who has
  pre-argued the objections holds the line and an un-prepped one folds.
- **Pair fear/risk framing with a concrete, doable response** or it backfires.
- **Plan boosters.** Resistance decays; a one-time inoculation fades.
