# Screening for Excessive Daytime Sleepiness in Addiction Treatment Settings

## Core Concepts

- **Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS)** is pathological sleepiness during waking hours not explained by inadequate nocturnal sleep; distinct from fatigue or low motivation
- **Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)**: 8-item self-report (0–24); asks likelihood of dozing in 8 common situations; >10 is the standard clinical cutoff for EDS warranting evaluation
- **Swiss Narcolepsy Scale (SNS)**: 5-item instrument targeting cataplexy and sleepiness symptoms; SNS ≤0 has 89% sensitivity / 88% specificity for hypocretin-deficient narcolepsy with cataplexy — substantially more specific than ESS alone (ESS specificity ~54% for narcolepsy)
- **Stanford Sleepiness Scale (SSS)** and **Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (KSS)**: single-item moment-in-time ratings (SSS 1–7; KSS 1–10); capture acute sleepiness fluctuations rather than habitual EDS — useful for within-session state monitoring but not diagnostic
- SUD populations have markedly elevated rates of sleep disorders (OSA, narcolepsy, insomnia, PLMD) that are systematically under-identified because EDS overlaps with intoxication, withdrawal, PAWS, and medication side effects

## Mechanisms / Neurobiology

- Opioids suppress REM and slow-wave sleep acutely; chronic use upregulates adenosine signaling, increasing homeostatic sleep drive and EDS even during abstinence
- Stimulant withdrawal (amphetamine, cocaine) produces hypersomnia via dopamine and norepinephrine depletion; ESS scores peak at 1–2 weeks post-cessation and may falsely elevate above 10
- Alcohol disrupts sleep architecture chronically; withdrawal rebound REM is hyperintense, yet residual EDS persists ≥6 months into sobriety
- Hypocretin (orexin) neurons are disrupted by substance exposure and in narcolepsy type 1; overlap creates diagnostic ambiguity — stimulant craving in a patient with undiagnosed narcolepsy may represent self-medication of hypocretin deficiency
- OSA prevalence in SUD populations reaches 35–50% (vs ~15–30% general adults), compounding EDS through nocturnal hypoxia and sleep fragmentation

## Clinical Picture

- ESS >10 prevalence in general adult population: ~30%; in opioid-dependent patients in treatment: estimated 40–60% depending on study; cutoff inadequacy debate centers on the high false-positive rate (low specificity) in clinical subgroups
- ESS ≥16 signals severe EDS and sharply increases pre-test probability for narcolepsy, OSA, or idiopathic hypersomnia — polysomnography + MSLT referral typically indicated at this threshold
- Narcolepsy type 1 (with cataplexy) prevalence: ~0.02–0.05% general population; among patients presenting with SUD and EDS, undetected narcolepsy is a meaningful missed diagnosis, particularly when stimulants produced dramatic symptom relief
- SNS adds cataplexy detection (sudden muscle weakness triggered by emotion) — a symptom patients rarely volunteer but that nearly confirms NT1 when present; clinicians should ask directly

## Evidence & Treatment

- No RCTs validate a systematic EDS screening protocol in addiction treatment programs; current evidence is observational and expert-consensus based
- ESS >10 in a patient in early SUD recovery should prompt: (1) sleep hygiene assessment, (2) OSA screening (STOP-BANG), (3) if persisting beyond 4–6 weeks of stable abstinence, formal sleep study referral
- SNS is recommended when ESS is elevated AND stimulant self-medication history or emotional cataplexy-like episodes are present — a positive SNS (score ≤0) warrants PSG/MSLT
- Integrated sleep-addiction clinics improve engagement: addressing EDS reduces relapse risk (sleepiness is a relapse trigger), improves cognitive rehabilitation, and treats an independent disorder driving self-medication
- Barriers include provider unfamiliarity with sleep instruments, stigma around requesting sleep studies for patients with SUD, insurance pre-authorization hurdles, and the diagnostic noise of PAWS

## Key Facts

- ESS cutoff >10 = EDS warranting evaluation; ≥16 = severe EDS with strong indication for sleep study
- SNS specificity for narcolepsy with cataplexy: 88% vs ESS specificity of only 54%
- OSA prevalence in SUD treatment: ~35–50% (roughly double the general population)
- EDS peak in stimulant withdrawal: approximately weeks 1–2 post-cessation; re-assess at 6+ weeks before attributing ESS elevation to a primary sleep disorder
- KSS/SSS are state measures (single moment); ESS measures habitual propensity — use ESS for screening, KSS/SSS for session-level monitoring
