# CBT for Substance Use Disorders

> Provenance: health/nat spoke — `/dr nat-cbt-sud` 2026-06-22. Distinct from Matrix Model (nat-matrix-model), which is a multi-component program incorporating CBT as one element.

CBT for SUD is the most extensively researched psychosocial treatment for substance use disorders. Carroll's 1998 NIDA-published manual and Marlatt & Gordon's (1985) relapse prevention model are the twin foundations. The core premise: substance use is a learned behavior that can be modified through skill acquisition, cognitive restructuring, and systematic cue management.

---

## Core Model

**Learning theory basis:** SUD develops through classical conditioning (cue–craving associations) and operant conditioning (substance use reinforced by relief, pleasure, social reward). Treatment targets extinction of conditioned responses and counterconditioning via new behavioral repertoires.

**Two-track intervention:**
- *Cognitive track* — identify and challenge drug-related beliefs ("I can't cope without meth," "One hit won't hurt," "I've already relapsed, might as well continue")
- *Behavioral track* — skill-building, behavioral rehearsal, structured avoidance and coping planning

Relapse is framed as a learned skill failure, not a moral failure — this reframing is itself therapeutic and reduces shame-driven dropout.

---

## Functional Analysis (FA)

The foundational CBT-SUD technique. Structure: **Antecedents → Behavior → Consequences**.

**Antecedents:**
- *Internal:* emotions (boredom, anxiety, dysphoria), thoughts, cravings, physical states
- *External:* people (using peers, dealers), places (former use locations), things (paraphernalia, cash, smell)

**Behavior:** drug use episode (dose, route, context)

**Consequences:**
- *Short-term:* relief, euphoria, social bonding, energy, sexual disinhibition
- *Long-term:* occupational loss, relationship damage, health consequences, legal exposure

FA reveals the *function* the substance serves for that individual — the specific need being met. This determines what must change. For methamphetamine, common functions include: energy/productivity augmentation for work demands, social confidence in anxiety-driven individuals, escape from trauma or negative affect, and relief from orexin-deficiency hypersomnolence (undiagnosed narcolepsy/IH in a subset). [^1][^3]

## High-Risk Situation Identification

Marlatt & Gordon's taxonomy identifies five primary categories accounting for the large majority of relapse episodes [^2]:

1. **Negative emotional states** — anger, anxiety, depression, loneliness (>50% of relapses in multiple studies)
2. **Positive emotional states** — celebrations, excitement, parties
3. **Interpersonal conflict** — arguments with partners, family, employers
4. **Social pressure** — direct offers, peer pressure, presence of using peers
5. **Craving/cue exposure** — conditioned responses to drug-associated stimuli (objects, smells, locations)

**Clinical application:** Build a personalized high-risk hierarchy ranked by subjective risk weight. For each situation, develop two responses: (a) avoidance strategy (early recovery, when coping capacity is low) and (b) active coping plan (for unavoidable exposure). The hierarchy is revised as recovery progresses.

## Coping Skills Training

Carroll's manual organizes skill-building into domains, each practiced via *behavioral rehearsal* (in-session role-play) before real-world application:

- **Drug refusal skills** — specific scripts for declining offers from known and unknown sources; assertive versus aggressive framing
- **Drug avoidance strategies** — route planning, contact management, environmental restructuring
- **Cognitive coping** — STOP–Think–Act sequence to interrupt automatic drug-seeking cognition
- **Distress tolerance** — integrates DBT-adjacent skills (urge surfing, TIPP, radical acceptance) for acute craving management
- **Interpersonal skills** — assertiveness, conflict resolution, prosocial activity expansion
- **Problem-solving** — structured D–E–F–I–N–E approach to life stressors that would otherwise precipitate use
- **Anger management** — de-escalation, emotion labeling, time-out protocols

All skills are first modeled by the therapist, then rehearsed by the client, then assigned as between-session practice with structured self-monitoring forms. [^1][^4]

## Relapse Prevention: The Marlatt–Gordon Model

**Immediate determinants of relapse:**
- Encounter with high-risk situation → if adequate coping response → increased self-efficacy → decreased relapse probability
- If *no* coping response → decreased self-efficacy + positive outcome expectancy for substance → lapse

**Abstinence Violation Effect (AVE):** The critical lapse-to-relapse amplifier. After a slip, the person attributes failure to a stable, global personal defect ("I'm an addict, I have no willpower") → guilt + reduced self-efficacy → continued use as the only available coping response. CBT directly targets AVE via:
- Cognitive restructuring: "a slip is a learning event, not a relapse"
- Lapse vs. relapse distinction (explicit psychoeducation)
- Emergency coping card (pre-written response to the first slip)
- 24-hour contact protocol with therapist or sponsor [^2]

**Covert antecedents:** Lifestyle imbalance ("should vs. want" ratio), apparently irrelevant decisions (AIDs — small choices that cumulatively increase exposure risk), and urge/craving conditioning are addressed through lifestyle monitoring assignments and urge-surfing practice.

## Evidence Base

**Stimulant/meth-specific RCTs:**
- Rawson et al. (2004) Matrix Model RCT (N=978 across 8 sites): CBT-integrated outpatient treatment produced 27% higher treatment completion and 31% greater meth-free urines versus TAU; CM component showed additive effects during active treatment phase [^5]
- Carroll & Onken (2005) review: CBT established efficacy across SUD; for stimulants, CBT + CM is the most effective combination; individual + group CBT format outperforms either alone

**Meta-analytic evidence:**
- Frontera et al. (2025) meta-analysis (8–9 RCTs, 849 participants): standalone CBT vs. minimal intervention — OR = 2.88 (95% CI 1.08–7.70) for stimulant abstinence; meth/amphetamine subgroup OR = 9.14 (interpret cautiously — small k, zero-event control trials inflate estimate); evidence quality rated *low* [^4]
- Magill et al. (2019) meta-analysis (30 RCTs): CBT vs. minimal/waitlist — moderate effect (g = 0.58); CBT vs. TAU/supportive counseling — small effect (g = 0.18); CBT vs. another specific therapy — near-zero effects; suggests CBT's advantage lies primarily over no-treatment conditions [^6]

**Key takeaway:** CBT is superior to no treatment or minimal intervention; broadly equivalent to other active psychosocial treatments when compared head-to-head. The additive combination with contingency management is the best-supported approach for meth SUD.

## Adaptations for Meth-Related Cognitive Impairment

Methamphetamine produces significant deficits in prefrontal cortex function, hippocampal memory consolidation, and executive control — precisely the cognitive systems CBT requires. [^7] This creates a treatment paradox: patients most in need of CBT have diminished capacity to engage with its most cognitively demanding components.

**Impairments affecting CBT engagement:**
- Working memory deficits → cannot retain session content, fail to complete thought-monitoring homework
- Reduced cognitive flexibility → difficulty shifting maladaptive patterns; rigid thinking persists
- Impaired inhibitory control → automatic drug-seeking behaviors resist cognitive interruption
- Metacognitive deficits → reduced ability to observe and report own thought processes (required for thought records)

**Recommended adaptations (2–6 months post-cessation):**
1. **Behavioral-first sequencing** — lead with behavioral skills (avoidance planning, refusal scripts) before introducing cognitive restructuring techniques; behavioral rehearsal relies less on metacognition
2. **Simplified worksheets** — reduce written homework to 3-field formats (trigger / feeling / what I did); defer full thought records until months 3–6
3. **Repetition and overlearning** — repeat core concepts across multiple sessions; do not assume retention from prior sessions
4. **Therapist as external scaffold** — therapist actively structures thinking rather than expecting client to self-monitor independently
5. **Shorter sessions** — 45 minutes may be more effective than 60 for clients with significant attentional impairment
6. **Pharmacological support** — where possible, coordinate with prescribers on pro-cognitive support (adjunct agents targeting dopamine/glutamate) to restore cognitive substrate for CBT engagement [^7]
7. **Cognitive rehabilitation first** — for severe impairment, consider structured cognitive remediation program before or concurrent with CBT (cognitive rehabilitation → CBT sequencing)

---

## Limitations and Boundary Conditions

- Effect sizes shrink substantially vs. active comparators (another specific therapy ≈ equivalent to CBT)
- Durability concerns: meth-SUD studies show effects often lost at 6-month follow-up without ongoing contact
- Evidence base for meth specifically is smaller than for alcohol or cocaine; most stimulant RCTs mix cocaine and meth samples
- No RCTs yet for newer CBT variants (mindfulness-based CBT, schema therapy) specifically in meth SUD
- Telephone-delivered CBT (tCBT) shows efficacy for meth users in rural/remote settings, expanding access beyond clinic-based delivery
