# Medicaid DSH, UPL, and Provider Financing

> **Educational only — NOT legal or financial advice.** Federal rules change frequently; verify with CMS or state Medicaid agency.

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22** *(compiled from training knowledge; key federal citations stable; verify current status of ACA DSH reductions, directed payment preprints, and NC-specific amounts with CMS/NC DHHS)*

---

## 1. Why Provider Financing Matters

Medicaid pays providers below private-insurer rates, creating financial pressure on safety-net hospitals and clinics that serve high proportions of Medicaid and uninsured patients. To compensate — and to generate the state match that draws down federal FMAP dollars — states have developed a complex set of supplemental financing mechanisms: DSH, UPL, provider taxes, and (under managed care) directed payments.

---

## 2. Disproportionate Share Hospital (DSH) Payments

### What DSH Is
DSH payments are Medicaid supplemental payments to hospitals that **serve a disproportionate share of low-income patients** — specifically those with high Medicaid inpatient utilization days or high uncompensated care costs. Authorized under **Section 1923 of the Social Security Act**.

### Federal DSH Allotments
- CMS distributes annual **DSH allotments** to each state, capping the total federal match for DSH payments
- Allotment formula based on historical state DSH spending + adjustments
- States cannot receive federal match for DSH spending above the allotment
- **Hospital-specific DSH limit**: a single hospital's DSH payment cannot exceed its uncompensated care costs (costs of care for Medicaid patients above Medicaid payment + uninsured patients)

### DSH Audits
- States must conduct **annual DSH audits** of every DSH-receiving hospital
- Hospitals that received DSH above their specific limit must repay the excess
- CMS has strengthened audit requirements; states face disallowances for non-compliance

### ACA DSH Reductions
The ACA assumed Medicaid expansion + marketplace coverage would reduce uncompensated care, so it mandated **DSH reductions** — repeatedly delayed by Congress. As of 2026, reductions remain substantially postponed but not permanently repealed. Non-expansion states argue reductions are unfair (their uncompensated care burden didn't drop).

## 3. Upper Payment Limits (UPL)

### What UPL Is
The UPL rule caps total Medicaid payments to a class of providers (hospitals, nursing facilities, physicians) at **what Medicare would pay** for the same services. States cannot receive federal match for payments above the UPL.

### FFS vs. Managed Care UPL
- Under **fee-for-service (FFS)**: UPL applies directly to state plan payments
- Under **managed care**: UPL-like limits apply differently — managed care plans pay providers via their negotiated rates; states cannot make supplemental payments outside the capitation rate except through directed/pass-through payments under specific CMS rules

### Historical Abuse
In the 1990s–2000s, states exploited UPL by cycling IGT money through provider payments at near-Medicare rates, drawing federal match on essentially state-funded payments with little net additional benefit to providers. CMS cracked down with tighter rules starting 2001.

## 4. Provider Taxes and IGT (Intergovernmental Transfers)

### Provider Taxes
States may impose taxes on Medicaid-participating providers (hospitals, nursing homes, MCOs, pharmacies, etc.) and use the tax revenue to fund the **state share of Medicaid**. The tax revenue attracts federal FMAP match, effectively amplifying state spending.

**Federal rules (Section 1903(w)):**
- Provider tax must be **broad-based** (applied uniformly across the class)
- Must not **hold harmless** providers (state cannot guarantee providers are no worse off after tax + payment)
- Tax rate cannot exceed **6% of net patient revenues** (the "safe harbor" threshold)
- CMS evaluates whether taxes functionally create a hold-harmless arrangement

**NC context:** NC uses hospital taxes and nursing facility taxes as part of its Medicaid financing strategy.

### IGT (Intergovernmental Transfers)
Public hospitals (government-owned) can transfer funds to the state to serve as the non-federal share of Medicaid payments. This allows states to draw down federal match without using state general fund money. IGT is legal but subject to scrutiny to ensure it doesn't create a circular flow (provider gets transfer back as payment).

## 5. Directed and Supplemental Payments Under Managed Care

### The Shift to Managed Care and the Financing Gap
When states move Medicaid from FFS to managed care, **traditional UPL/DSH supplemental payments are harder to pass through** because MCOs pay providers directly. CMS issued new regulations (2016, 2020, 2023 iterations) creating:

### Directed Payments (42 CFR § 438.6(c))
- State requires MCOs to pay specific providers at **minimum (or exact) rates** set by the state
- Allows supplemental payments to flow through the managed care capitation rate
- Subject to CMS approval via "directed payment preprint"
- Most common: hospital directed payments at Medicare-equivalent rates

### Pass-Through Payments (Being Phased Out)
Legacy supplemental payments that passed through MCO contracts directly to providers — grandfathered through transition but phased out by 2027 under 2016 rule.

### Aggregate UPL for Managed Care
States must demonstrate that combined capitation + directed payments don't exceed the FFS UPL for the provider class — the "aggregate upper payment limit" test.

## 6. NC Provider Financing Context

**NC DSH:** NC operates DSH for qualifying hospitals including safety-net hospitals in urban areas and rural critical access hospitals. Post-expansion (Dec 2023), NC's uncompensated care burden has decreased, making DSH calculations more complex.

**NC provider taxes:** NC levies assessments on hospitals and nursing facilities that are structured to comply with federal hold-harmless restrictions.

**NC managed care directed payments:** NC uses directed payment arrangements under NC Medicaid managed care contracts (Standard Plans and Tailored Plans) to ensure adequate provider rates for safety-net hospitals and behavioral health providers.

## 7. Policy Implications

| Mechanism | State incentive | Federal concern |
|---|---|---|
| DSH | Maximize allotment draw-down for safety-net hospitals | Ensure payments reflect actual uncompensated care |
| Provider taxes | Amplify federal match via provider assessment | Prevent circular hold-harmless arrangements |
| IGT | Public hospitals fund state match | Ensure real public cost, not paper transfer |
| Directed payments | Maintain MCO rate adequacy for key providers | Prevent above-UPL payments, MCO gaming |

**Block grant debate:** Critics of current FMAP/open-ended entitlement structure argue DSH, provider taxes, and directed payments allow states to "game" federal matching. Block grant or per-capita cap proposals would eliminate the match incentive but risk reducing total Medicaid funding.
