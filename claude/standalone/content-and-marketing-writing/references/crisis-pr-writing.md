# Crisis PR Writing

## Overview

Crisis PR writing is the discipline of communicating publicly during a **reputation crisis** that is not (or is no longer) a live operational incident. Examples:

- An executive misconduct allegation surfaces
- A data breach beyond the operational window — now a public-disclosure problem
- A product recall or safety advisory
- A supplier or partner exposure (forced labor, sanctions, contamination)
- A viral social-media controversy
- An advocacy/regulator action against the company
- An employee-conduct video goes viral

The job is different from incident-comms. Incident-comms says "the system is down, we're working on it" to existing customers and is operational in tone. Crisis PR speaks to **the public, the press, regulators, employees, customers, and shareholders simultaneously** about **trust** — and once trust is lost it does not come back through technical updates.

The canonical reference response is Johnson & Johnson's 1982 Tylenol cyanide crisis: rapid acknowledgement, decisive action (a full 31-million-bottle recall), transparent media communication, a 1-800 line for the public and a separate dedicated line for press, and a visible product redesign (tamper-evident packaging) that became an industry standard. The pattern matters more than the specifics: **acknowledge fast → explain what you know → commit to specific action → follow through publicly.**

## Core concepts

### 1. The acknowledge / explain / commit / follow-through arc

Every crisis statement, regardless of length or audience, moves through four phases. The phases are sequential — skipping or reordering them creates the "non-apology apology" or the "tone-deaf statement" pattern.

| Phase | Job | Length | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge | Name the event. State that it happened. Express concern for the people affected. | 1–2 sentences | "Earlier today, an unauthorized party accessed a database containing customer account information. We deeply regret the impact this has on the people who trust us." |
| Explain | State what you know and, importantly, what you don't yet know. Avoid speculation. | 1–3 sentences | "Our security team detected the access at 4:17 a.m. ET and contained it within 90 minutes. We do not yet know the full scope of records affected, and we have engaged independent forensic investigators." |
| Commit | Name the specific actions you are taking and the timeline. | 2–4 sentences | "We have notified law enforcement and regulators. We are providing affected customers with 24 months of credit monitoring at no cost. We will publish a verified scope assessment within 14 days." |
| Follow-through | Promise — and then deliver — a visible status update. | 1 sentence | "We will issue an update by Friday, November 15, at 5 p.m. ET, and weekly thereafter until the investigation closes." |

**The four phases work across formats**: an 80-word holding statement, a CEO-signed open letter, a regulator briefing, and a multi-day media tour all use the same arc; the difference is depth.

### 2. The 60-minute holding statement

Within 60 minutes of a crisis becoming public, you owe a statement. The holding statement is **not the full statement** — it's a high-trust placeholder that proves the organization is engaged, listening, and not absent.

**A good holding statement**:

- Acknowledges that something happened (or that there are reports of something — if facts are still emerging)
- Expresses concern for anyone affected
- States you are investigating
- Promises a substantive update by a specific time
- Names a point of contact (or the company's press email)

**Skeleton**:

> "We are aware of [event/reports of event]. Our team is actively [investigating / responding / assessing the situation]. The safety/wellbeing of [affected stakeholders] is our top priority. We will share a detailed update by [specific date/time]. For media inquiries, contact press@company.com."

**Hard rules**:

- Never speculate. "We don't know yet" beats "we believe."
- Never minimize. "An incident has been reported" beats "minor disruption."
- Never blame externally on day one — even when external blame is true. It reads as evasion.
- Always commit to a specific next update time. Vague "we'll share more when we have it" reads as stalling.

### 3. The non-apology apology (anti-pattern)

The single most damaging error in crisis writing is the conditional or deflecting apology. Examples to refuse:

| Non-apology | Why it fails | Correct form |
|---|---|---|
| "We're sorry if anyone was offended." | Conditional ("if") + locates the problem in the audience ("was offended") not the action | "We're sorry. The video posted Tuesday does not reflect our values, and we should not have produced it." |
| "Mistakes were made." | Passive voice removes the actor and the responsibility | "We made a mistake. Our customer-service team gave incorrect information about refunds for three weeks." |
| "We regret that some customers had a negative experience." | Vague + frames the customer's reaction as the issue, not the company's action | "We failed our customers. For three weeks, we charged $40 for a service that should have been free under our published terms." |
| "While we understand the concerns raised, we stand by our decision." | The "while we understand" pattern is dismissive boilerplate | If you stand by it, explain the reasoning. If you don't, change the decision. Don't pretend both. |

**Test your apology**: read it aloud. If it contains "if," "unfortunate," "any," "regret," or "concerns raised" — and does not contain a specific action — it is not an apology. It is a deflection.

### 4. SCCT response posture (Situational Crisis Communication Theory)

Coombs' SCCT framework helps choose a posture based on **how responsible the organization is for the crisis**. Three clusters; pick one:

| Cluster | When to use | Postures |
|---|---|---|
| Victim cluster | The organization is also a victim (natural disaster, malicious external attack, rumor, product tampering) | Inform; express sympathy; emphasize remediation |
| Accidental cluster | Unintentional crisis (technical-error accident, mega-damage from a process failure) | Explain the cause; demonstrate corrective action; express regret |
| Preventable cluster | The organization knew or should have known (human-error accident, organizational misdeed, executive misconduct) | Full apology; full responsibility; visible corrective action; leadership consequence |

**Mismatch is the most expensive crisis-PR error**. Treating a preventable-cluster crisis as a victim-cluster crisis (e.g., blaming an external party for what was internal negligence) creates the secondary crisis: "first they did it, then they lied about it." The cover-up always becomes the bigger story.

### 5. Public-facing vs regulator-facing voice

The same facts often need to be communicated to three different audiences at near-simultaneous timing. The voices are not interchangeable.

| Audience | Voice | What they need |
|---|---|---|
| Public/press | Plain language, human, third-person company plus first-person CEO/leader when an apology is involved | Acknowledgement, empathy, commitment, what they should do |
| Regulators | Precise, factual, dated, dispassionate, with explicit references to relevant statutes, codes, or prior filings | Compliance posture, timeline of detection and response, remediation commitments, contact for follow-up |
| Customers | Direct, specific, action-oriented ("here is what we are doing for you," "here is what you should do") | What's happening to me, what I should do, when I will hear next |
| Employees | Honest about what's known and unknown, with explicit guidance on what to say if asked | Internal clarity before external clarity — never let employees learn it from Twitter |

**Sequencing rule**: employees get a brief before the public statement is released, even if it's a 30-minute window. Customers and regulators are notified per legal counsel's guidance, but never after the public press cycle.

### 6. The CEO signature decision

CEO signing escalates and humanizes. It also raises the stakes. Decision matrix:

- **Yes — CEO signs**: preventable-cluster crises, executive-conduct issues, fatal product failures, large-scale customer-impacting breaches, anything where the CEO will inevitably be quoted by the press anyway.
- **No — Communications/Legal signs (or no signature)**: routine product advisories, low-impact compliance notices, partner/supplier-exposure statements where the CEO has no clear personal connection, ongoing investigations where speculation is dangerous.
- **Hybrid**: company statement on letterhead with a CEO-attributed quote inside it.

Once the CEO signs the first statement, they own the arc. Switching signatures mid-crisis reads as the leader pulling away.

### 7. The legal/comms handoff

Crisis PR happens at the seam between Legal and Communications. The handoff rules:

- Legal sets the **outer limit** of what can be said (admissions risk, regulatory pre-notification, evidentiary issues, ongoing law-enforcement coordination).
- Comms sets the **floor** of what must be said (silence is itself a statement; reputation damage is a real and measurable harm).
- The CEO/executive owner picks a point between the two.
- Both groups review every public statement before release. Legal looks for admissions and binding language; Comms looks for tone, evasion, and non-apology patterns.
- The phrase "no comment" is **never** the answer in modern crisis PR. Acceptable variants: "We don't comment on pending litigation, but here is what we can say...," "We're still assessing the facts and will share more by [specific time]."

### 8. Versioning discipline

Crisis statements have a high revision rate and a high copy-paste rate. Track them.

- Every statement gets a version number, a timestamp, and an author.
- When facts change, supersede the prior statement publicly. Don't quietly edit a press page.
- The "we are revising our earlier statement to correct a factual error" admission is uncomfortable but recoverable. The "they quietly changed the page" discovery is not.

### 9. Channels and cadence

Crisis statements flow through specific channels in a specific order:

1. **Holding statement (T+0 to T+60 min)**: Posted on company press page, sent to wire if material, given to journalists actively pursuing the story.
2. **Substantive statement (T+2h to T+12h)**: Press release on owned channel; emailed to affected customers (if applicable); coordinated with regulator notifications.
3. **Update cadence (T+24h, T+72h, then weekly until close)**: Posted in a single "incident log" page so press can see the timeline; emailed to affected customers if material change.
4. **CEO presence (T+24h to T+72h, depending on severity)**: Open letter, video statement, or media availability for preventable-cluster crises.

The "incident log" page — a single URL with chronologically ordered, dated, versioned updates — is the highest-trust channel pattern in modern crisis PR. Use it.

### 10. The Tylenol benchmark

Reference response by which all major crises are judged. Specifics worth memorizing:

- Within hours of the first deaths, J&J halted all Tylenol advertising and issued nationwide warnings.
- 31 million bottles recalled at a cost of ~$100M (in 1982 dollars).
- 450,000 messages to healthcare facilities and stakeholders.
- A 1-800 line for the public and a separate pre-recorded media line.
- Visible product redesign (tamper-evident triple-seal packaging) within months.
- The CEO (James Burke) was the public face throughout.

The Tylenol takeaway: when a preventable-or-victim-cluster crisis hits, the cost of decisive action is always less than the cost of perceived inaction.

## Templates and examples

### Holding statement (60-minute) — data breach

> **Statement — November 14, 2026, 9:42 a.m. ET**
>
> Acme Corp is aware of reports that customer account information may have been accessed without authorization. Our security team is actively investigating and has engaged independent forensic experts. The protection of customer data is a responsibility we take seriously, and we deeply regret any concern this causes.
>
> We will share a substantive update by 5:00 p.m. ET today, November 14. Affected customers, if any, will be contacted directly with specific guidance.
>
> Customers with immediate questions can contact security@acme.com. Press inquiries: press@acme.com.
>
> — Acme Corp Communications

### Substantive statement — product recall (preventable cluster)

> **Acme Corp Statement on Voluntary Recall of Model X-1 Power Bank**
> **November 14, 2026 — San Francisco**
>
> We are voluntarily recalling all Acme Model X-1 Power Banks sold between June 2025 and October 2026 because of a battery defect that may cause overheating and, in rare cases, fire. Three injuries have been reported, and our hearts are with the people affected.
>
> The defect originates in a manufacturing process change introduced in May 2025 that was not adequately verified before shipping. The responsibility for that failure is ours.
>
> Effective today:
>
> - All Model X-1 sales are suspended.
> - Every customer who purchased a Model X-1 will receive a full refund, plus a $50 credit, regardless of receipt.
> - Return shipping is prepaid; instructions are at acme.com/recall.
> - We have notified the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission and equivalent regulators in the 14 other markets where the product was sold.
> - An independent safety review of our manufacturing-change process is underway, led by [named firm], with public findings expected by January 31, 2027.
>
> We will issue a status update on November 21, and weekly thereafter, until every affected unit is returned and the safety review is complete.
>
> Customer support: 1-800-555-RECALL or recall@acme.com.
> Press: press@acme.com.
>
> — Jane Doe, Chief Executive Officer

### Executive-misconduct statement

> **Statement from Acme Corp — November 14, 2026**
>
> Earlier this week, the Acme Corp Board of Directors received credible reports that [Executive Name] engaged in conduct inconsistent with our code of ethics. The Board immediately retained outside counsel to investigate.
>
> Effective today, [Executive Name] is on leave pending the outcome of the investigation, and [Interim Leader, Title] is assuming the role on an interim basis. The Board will accept and act on the investigators' findings, including consequences up to and including termination.
>
> Conduct that violates our values harms our colleagues, our customers, and the trust we work to earn. We are committed to acting on the facts, not on what is convenient.
>
> The Board will provide a public update by December 15, 2026. Until then, we will not comment on personnel specifics, as a matter of fairness to all involved and consistent with employment law.
>
> Press inquiries: press@acme.com.
>
> — The Board of Directors, Acme Corp

### Social-media controversy response

> **A note from Acme — November 14, 2026**
>
> The post we published Tuesday on our social channels was wrong. It made a joke at the expense of [affected group] that we should not have made.
>
> We are sorry. The post has been removed. The two team members who approved it are no longer involved in our social media program, and the manager who approved the campaign has been reassigned. We are conducting a review of our content-approval process and will publish what we change by November 30.
>
> To the people we hurt: thank you for telling us. We are listening.
>
> — Jane Doe, CEO

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Conditional apology ("sorry if anyone was offended") | Locates fault in the audience, not the actor | "We're sorry. We did X. We should not have." |
| Passive voice ("mistakes were made") | Hides the actor; reads as accountability evasion | First person plural: "We made a mistake." |
| Going dark | Silence is read as either guilt, panic, or both | Even a 60-second holding statement beats two hours of silence |
| Treating a preventable-cluster crisis as a victim-cluster crisis | The cover-up always becomes the bigger story | Match SCCT posture to the actual responsibility |
| Quietly editing the press page | Discovery destroys trust permanently | Version statements; supersede openly |
| "No comment" | Reads as guilt + disrespect to the public | "We are still assessing; we will share more by [time]" |
| Speculating in the first 60 minutes | Speculation that turns out wrong becomes the next crisis | "We do not yet know" — and mean it |
| Blaming a third party on day one | Reads as evasion, even when true | Establish your own accountability first; assign external responsibility (if any) when facts are confirmed |
| Switching the signature mid-crisis | Reads as the leader retreating | Commit to a signatory at T+0 |
| Long delays between updates | Trust degrades exponentially with silence | Commit to and meet a public cadence |
| Crisis comms written by Legal alone | Reads as a court filing, not a human statement | Legal sets outer limits; Comms drafts; CEO owns |
| Reusing the same statement when facts have changed | Materially false within hours | Update; supersede; version |

## Decision heuristics

**Holding statement vs full statement at T+0**

- Less than 60 minutes since public exposure → holding statement is sufficient and expected
- More than 4 hours since public exposure and no holding statement issued → the silence is now the story; release a holding statement that acknowledges the delay
- Facts substantially known, all stakeholders ready → release the substantive statement and skip the holding step

**Apology vs explanation vs sympathy**

- Preventable cluster (organizational fault) → apology + corrective action + consequence
- Accidental cluster → explanation + corrective action + regret
- Victim cluster → sympathy + remediation + cooperation with authorities

**CEO speaks vs Comms speaks**

- Human harm + organizational fault → CEO
- Human harm + no organizational fault → CEO statement of sympathy + Comms operational updates
- No human harm, technical/process issue → Comms or relevant business leader

**When to bring in outside counsel**

- Any allegation that could constitute a securities-disclosure trigger
- Any human-injury or fatality
- Any law-enforcement contact (proactive or reactive)
- Any regulator inquiry
- Any executive-misconduct allegation
- Any threatened or filed litigation

**When the statement is "done"**

- The four-phase arc is complete (acknowledge / explain / commit / follow-through)
- Legal has cleared admissions risk
- The named action is specific (a date, a number, a person, a verifiable change)
- The next update time is committed in writing
- The point of contact is a working email or phone

## Cross-skill collisions and routing

- **operational outage updates during a live incident** → `incident-comms` (this skill assumes the operational incident has been declared or has moved into the reputational/disclosure phase)
- **binding legal notice with contractual or statutory effect** → `legal-adjacent-writing`
- **standard news/wire press release with no crisis** → `press-release-writing`
- **internal-only executive announcement** → `executive-comms`
- **post-incident technical postmortem for engineers** → `postmortem-writing`
- **policy or regulatory writing that establishes a new rule, not a response** → `policy-and-governance-writing`
