# Learning Science — Reading Notes

Organized by **concept, not by source**, so that as you read more, a new source on
the same idea lands on the note that already exists and they compound (Matuschak's
evergreen-notes idea, [below](#evergreen-notes--concept-oriented-not-source-oriented)).
Studies are folded in as **evidence under the concept they support** rather than kept
as separate per-book notes.

**How to add to this as you read more:**
- Find the concept the new material speaks to and add it *there* (in your own words —
  see [Smart notes](#smart-notes--writing-is-thinking)). Sources then stack on one note.
- If it's a genuinely new concept, start a new note under the right section and link it.
- Open questions are kept as questions, not resolved.

## The principle behind it all

### Desirable difficulties
Bjork's umbrella idea: **harder encoding = stronger retention.** Making learning feel
harder in the moment tends to make it stick better later. This is the parent principle
under which several of the techniques below sit — retrieval practice, spacing, and
interleaving all *feel* worse while you do them but produce more durable learning.

- **Source:** Bjork, via *Make It Stick* (Brown et al.)
- **Instances:** [Retrieval practice](#retrieval-practice-the-testing-effect),
  [Spacing](#spacing--distributed-practice), [Interleaving](#interleaving),
  [The generation effect](#the-generation-effect)
- **Why it lasts:** [Storage strength vs. retrieval strength](#storage-strength-vs-retrieval-strength)

## Techniques that exploit desirable difficulty

### Retrieval practice (the testing effect)
Testing yourself beats rereading. The act of *recalling* strengthens memory more than
re-exposure does. (A [desirable difficulty](#desirable-difficulties).)

- **Source:** *Make It Stick* (Brown et al.)
- **Evidence:** Karpicke & Blunt 2011 (*Science*) — retrieval practice beat elaborative
  studying (concept mapping) for learning science texts, **even when students predicted
  the opposite.**
- **Related:** rated *high utility* by [Dunlosky 2013](#dunlosky-2013--what-works-what-doesnt);
  connected to [the generation effect](#the-generation-effect); operationalized by
  [spaced-repetition systems](#spaced-repetition-systems-sm-2-anki-leitner).

### Spacing / distributed practice
Spacing out practice **feels worse but works better.** Massed practice (cramming) gives
fast gains that evaporate. (A [desirable difficulty](#desirable-difficulties).)

- **Source:** *Make It Stick* (Brown et al.)
- **Evidence:** Cepeda et al. meta-analysis — the optimal spacing gap scales with the
  retention interval: **"cram for tomorrow, space for forever."**
- **Related:** rated *high utility* by [Dunlosky 2013](#dunlosky-2013--what-works-what-doesnt);
  operationalized by [spaced-repetition systems](#spaced-repetition-systems-sm-2-anki-leitner).

### Interleaving
Mixing different problem types beats blocking (practicing one type at a time) — **even
though blocked practice feels easier.** (A [desirable difficulty](#desirable-difficulties).)

- **Source:** *Make It Stick* (Brown et al.)

### The generation effect
Attempting an answer *before* being shown it helps — **even when you fail** at the
attempt. (A [desirable difficulty](#desirable-difficulties).)

- **Source:** *Make It Stick* (Brown et al.)
- **Open question:** connects to the Karpicke finding on
  [retrieval practice](#retrieval-practice-the-testing-effect)?

## A different lever: encoding

### Elaboration
Elaboration = **connecting new information to what you already know.** That connecting
is what makes it stick. (This is an *encoding* mechanism — building links to prior
knowledge — rather than an instance of [desirable difficulty](#desirable-difficulties).)

- **Source:** Ahrens, *How to Take Smart Notes* (*Make It Stick* also touches on it)
- **Related:** Karpicke & Blunt's study compared retrieval practice *against*
  elaborative studying (concept mapping) — see
  [Retrieval practice](#retrieval-practice-the-testing-effect).

## The model that explains why the gains last

### Storage strength vs. retrieval strength
Bjork (a different paper?) distinguishes **"storage strength"** from **"retrieval
strength."** Forgetting is decay of *retrieval* strength; *storage* persists. That's why
relearning something is faster than learning it the first time. This is the model that
explains why the [desirable-difficulty](#desirable-difficulties) techniques above produce
durable learning.

- **Source:** Bjork (different paper from the desirable-difficulties one? — unverified)

## The evidence at a glance

### Dunlosky 2013 — what works, what doesn't
A review that rated study techniques by utility:
- **High utility:** practice testing ([retrieval practice](#retrieval-practice-the-testing-effect))
  + distributed practice ([spacing](#spacing--distributed-practice)).
- **Low utility:** highlighting and rereading.
- **Middling:** summarization.

- **Source:** Dunlosky 2013 review

## Putting it into practice

### Spaced-repetition systems (SM-2, Anki, Leitner)
Tools that operationalize [retrieval practice](#retrieval-practice-the-testing-effect)
and [spacing](#spacing--distributed-practice):
- Anki uses the **SM-2** algorithm — **intervals expand on success.**
- **Leitner boxes** = the analog version of the same idea.

- **Source:** (personal note / "random")

### Smart notes / writing is thinking
- **Writing IS thinking**, not the record of it.
- Notes should be **in your own words** — copying quotes bypasses understanding.
- (See also [Elaboration](#elaboration), which is the mechanism this relies on.)

- **Source:** Ahrens, *How to Take Smart Notes*

### Evergreen notes / concept-oriented, not source-oriented
Notes should be **concept-oriented, not source-oriented.** A second source on the same
concept lands on the existing note and they **compound.** Give notes titles like APIs
(stable, reusable handles). This is the organizing principle this whole document follows.

- **Source:** Matuschak
