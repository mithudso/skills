# Cover letter writing

## Overview

The cover letter is a **narrative artifact** — it should read like a focused, one-page argument for why this human should be in this role at this company. It is structurally different from the resume, which is a scannable list of structured facts. The cover letter is where the candidate's voice, judgment, and pattern-matching shows up. A great resume gets the recruiter to open the cover letter; a great cover letter gets the recruiter to schedule the call.

This skill assumes you are drafting or revising a cover letter for a specific application, not a generic template. **Customization per application is non-negotiable.** A cover letter that could be sent to any company is a cover letter that won't win any.

## Core concepts

### 1. The three-part skeleton: why this company / why this role / why me

Every effective cover letter answers three questions in order:

1. **Why this company.** Show evidence you've done your homework: a product they shipped, a value they've published, a mission you align with, a person you spoke to in informational interviews. Generic flattery ("I'm impressed by your innovative culture") fails this test. Specific references ("Your team's decision to open-source the X library in 2025 caught my attention because I've been working on a similar problem at Y") passes.
2. **Why this role.** Connect the specific responsibilities in the job description to specific skills or experiences you have. Reuse 2-3 keywords from the JD.
3. **Why me.** Demonstrate — through evidence and stories — that you can do the job. This is the longest section. Don't list skills; show outcomes.

The skeleton can be expressed in 3-4 short paragraphs or compressed into 2 longer ones, but the three questions must all get answered.

### 2. Opener discipline: avoid the "I am writing to apply" cliché

The first sentence is the only one a busy reader is guaranteed to read. Burn it on a cliché and you've spent your one bullet.

**Weak openers (cliché, signal nothing):**
- "I am writing to apply for the X position at Y."
- "Please accept this letter as my application for X."
- "My name is X and I am a [degree/title]."

**Stronger openers (specific, signal research or stake):**
- "I noticed your team published the design for the X system last month — the tradeoffs you made on Y are the same ones I worked through at Z."
- "When I read that [Company] is expanding into [market], I recognized the inflection point — I led the same transition at [prior company] in 2024."
- "[Mutual contact] mentioned you're looking for someone who can [specific thing]. That's been my full-time focus for the past three years."

**Note:** Alison Green of Ask a Manager argues that simple openers like "I'm writing to apply for your X position" are acceptable and that creative openers can misfire. Use a strong specific opener if you can write one well; default to a simple direct opener if the alternative is forced or gimmicky. The cliché to avoid is the **dead** opener — the one that signals zero research and zero stake.

### 3. Show evidence, don't list skills

The single most common failure mode of cover letters is the "I am a great communicator and a team player" sentence. Asserting a quality is not the same as demonstrating one.

**Weak:** "I have strong project management skills and am highly organized."

**Stronger:** "Last year I shipped the X migration on a six-engineer team across three time zones. The original estimate was four months; we delivered in five because I caught a dependency on the Y service that wasn't in the original scope and re-sequenced the work."

The cover letter should read like the candidate is **walking the reader through one or two specific stories** that demonstrate the qualities the JD asks for. Stories beat adjectives.

### 4. Length conventions: one page, 3-4 short paragraphs, 250-350 words

- **One page maximum**, no exceptions. A two-page cover letter signals poor editing.
- **250-350 words** is the standard target.
- **3-4 short paragraphs**, block-formatted (flush left, no indent, blank line between paragraphs).
- If the letter runs over one page in 11pt with normal margins, cut paragraphs, not margins.

Engineering roles, internal applications, and entry-level applications often work well at the shorter end (~250 words). Senior executive, academic, or consulting roles may stretch to 350-400 words but rarely longer.

### 5. Customization per application is non-negotiable

A reused cover letter is detectable in the first paragraph. Recruiters who read 50 letters a week notice when they're reading the same one.

**At a minimum, customize:**
- Company name and the "why this company" paragraph (this is the most-skipped customization)
- 2-3 keywords pulled from the job description
- The job title in the opener
- The "why this role" connection — which specific responsibility from the JD you're matching

A practical workflow: write a **base letter** that holds your strongest 2-3 stories, then write a **fresh first paragraph** and a **fresh closing paragraph** for each application, and edit the middle to match the JD's keywords.

### 6. Executive summary placement: top vs bottom

Two valid structural patterns:

- **Executive-summary-at-top** (preferred for most professional roles): The opener and first paragraph state the thesis — who you are and why you're the right fit — then the body provides evidence. This is the BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front) pattern. Best when recruiters skim.
- **Narrative-arc** (more common for academic, creative, or career-change letters): The opener sets up a story or motivation, the body builds context, the closing crystallizes the fit. Risk: the reader bails before the payoff.

**Default to executive-summary-at-top.** Use narrative-arc only when the story itself is the differentiator (career change, unusual background, mission-driven role).

### 7. Signature and sign-off conventions

- **Salutation:** "Dear [Name]," when you know the hiring manager's name. "Dear [Team] Hiring Team," is acceptable when you don't. Avoid "To Whom It May Concern" and "Dear Sir/Madam."
- **Sign-off:** "Sincerely," is the universal safe choice. "Best regards," is a slightly warmer alternative. Avoid "Cheers," "Thanks," and "Warmly," for cover letters — these read informal.
- **Capitalization:** Only the first word of the closing phrase is capitalized ("Best regards," not "Best Regards,").
- **Digital submissions:** A typed name below the sign-off is sufficient. A scanned handwritten signature is not required for PDF or email submissions and often looks dated.
- **Header:** Your name, email, phone, city/state, and (optional) LinkedIn URL at the top. The recipient's name and company beneath, then the date. Many digital-only applications skip the postal-address block — that's fine.

### 8. The "I" parade and sentence-opening discipline

Count how many of your sentences open with "I." If more than half do, the letter reads self-absorbed. Vary openings: lead with the company, the role, the project, the result, or a subordinate clause.

**Weak (I parade):** "I led the migration. I designed the rollback plan. I shipped on schedule. I then mentored two new engineers on the system."

**Stronger:** "Leading the migration, I designed a rollback plan that the team still uses. The work shipped on schedule, and afterward two new engineers came onto the system — I mentored both through their first quarter."

## Templates and examples

### Template: three-paragraph cover letter (executive-summary-at-top)

```
[Your name]
[Email] | [Phone] | [City, State] | [LinkedIn]

[Date]

[Hiring manager name, if known]
[Title]
[Company]

Dear [Name],

[Opener — 1-2 sentences. Specific reference to the company or role. End with a one-line
thesis: who you are, the role you're applying for, and why you fit.]

[Body paragraph 1 — 3-5 sentences. The strongest evidence story: a specific project,
the role you played, the measurable outcome, and the skill it demonstrates. Connect
explicitly to a responsibility in the JD.]

[Body paragraph 2 — 3-5 sentences. Why this company specifically. Reference a product,
value, recent news, or mission. Connect to your own trajectory or interest. This is
where "why this company" lives.]

[Closing — 2-3 sentences. Restate the fit. Invite a conversation. Avoid "I look forward
to hearing from you" — try "I'd welcome the chance to discuss how I can help with X" or
"I'd be glad to walk through how I approached Y in more detail."]

Sincerely,

[Your name]
```

### Example: software engineer cover letter (~300 words)

```
Dear Maria,

I read the writeup of your team's migration from Redis to KeyDB last quarter — the
section on hot-key fan-out matched a problem I spent six months on at Acme. I'm
applying for the Senior Backend Engineer role on the Platform team, and I think the
work you're describing is exactly the kind I want to do next.

At Acme, I led the redesign of our session-store layer for a system that served
8M sessions/day. The original architecture had a fan-out problem on three hot keys
that caused tail latencies above 800ms during traffic spikes. I designed and shipped
a sharded-key strategy with a local read-through cache; p99 dropped to 120ms and the
on-call paged 70% less for the next two quarters. I learned more about cache-coherence
edge cases on that project than in the four years before it.

What draws me to Beta specifically is the open-source posture — the fact that your
KeyDB work landed upstream rather than staying internal. I've contributed to the
Redis-py client and to a smaller pub-sub library, and the teams I've worked best on
have all treated open-source contribution as part of the job rather than a side
hobby. Your team's published RFC process is the most rigorous I've seen outside
of an IETF working group.

I'd welcome the chance to walk through the sharded-key design and the tradeoffs we
considered against consistent-hashing alternatives. I'm available for a call any
weekday afternoon Pacific time.

Sincerely,

Mitchell Hudson
```

### Example: career-change cover letter opener (narrative-arc)

```
Dear Dr. Chen,

For seven years I built distributed systems. I was good at it, and I liked the work
— until I spent a summer volunteering at a clinical-trial data audit and realized
that the same tools I'd been using to scale ad-tech could close a real gap in
medical-records integrity. That summer is why I'm applying for the Data Engineering
role on your clinical-informatics team, and why I spent the past nine months
completing a certificate in health data standards (HL7 FHIR, OMOP CDM) on top of
my engineering work.
```

### Sample paragraph: show, don't list

**Don't:** "I am a strong communicator, a fast learner, and a team player who thrives in ambiguous environments."

**Do:** "When my team's product manager went on leave three weeks before launch, I picked up the customer-facing communication and wrote the launch FAQ, the support runbook, and the rollback procedure. Two engineers told me afterward they hadn't realized how much of the role they could absorb until they saw what was needed."

## Anti-patterns

1. **The cliché opener.** "I am writing to apply for the X position." Acceptable as a fallback but a wasted bullet if you can do better.
2. **The reused letter.** Generic flattery, no company name in the first paragraph, no JD-keyword echo. Detectable in 30 seconds.
3. **The skill list.** "Strong communicator, fast learner, team player." Adjectives without evidence.
4. **The I parade.** Every sentence opens with "I." Reads self-absorbed and lazy.
5. **The resume rehash.** Repeating the resume's bullets in paragraph form. The cover letter must add context or interpretation that the resume can't.
6. **The two-page letter.** Always cut to one page.
7. **The "To Whom It May Concern" / "Dear Sir or Madam."** Signals no effort to find the hiring manager. Search LinkedIn first.
8. **The desperation closer.** "I really need this job," "I would do anything to work here," or "Please consider me — I'm a hard worker." These signal weakness, not enthusiasm.
9. **The grade-school formalism.** "Enclosed please find my resume." Modern letters use plain language.
10. **The misaligned salary or "current title" mention.** Don't mention salary expectations or current compensation unless asked. Cover letter is not the place.
11. **The typo in the company name.** Auto-disqualifying. Read the company name aloud before sending.
12. **The 18-month-old cover letter date.** Update the date for every application.

## Decision heuristics

- **Is the opener specific to this company?** If the first sentence could be sent to any company, rewrite it.
- **Does the letter answer all three questions (why this company / why this role / why me)?** If one is missing, the letter is incomplete.
- **Can I point to evidence behind every claim?** If a skill is asserted without a story, replace it or cut it.
- **Is the letter under one page?** If not, cut the weakest paragraph.
- **Have I read it aloud?** Awkward phrasings and the I-parade reveal themselves in spoken voice.
- **Does the closing invite a specific next step?** If it ends with "I look forward to hearing from you," reach for something more concrete.
- **Have I checked the company name, hiring manager name, and role title for typos?** Three times.

## When to use a cover letter at all

Cover letters are not always required. HBR research (2025) shows that even when optional, including a thoughtful cover letter still measurably increases callback rates. Always include one when:

- The application form has a cover letter field (even marked optional).
- You're a career-changer, returning from a gap, or applying for a stretch role — the resume alone won't make the case.
- You have a referral or specific company connection to mention.
- The role is at a small company or startup where hiring managers read every application.

Skip the cover letter only when the application explicitly says "no cover letter" or "cover letter not required and will not be reviewed."
