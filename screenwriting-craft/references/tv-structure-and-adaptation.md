# TV Structure & Adapting Prose to Screenplay

Television structure diverges from feature structure because it is built
around a delivery mechanism (broadcast ad breaks, or their absence) and a
premise that must generate episodes indefinitely rather than resolve once.
Adaptation is a distinct craft problem: taking a work built for a different
medium's strengths (prose interiority, discursive structure) and rebuilding
it for the externalization constraint (see
`references/screenplay-format-and-externalization.md`).

## Core Concepts

### (a) Act breaks and the network commercial-break structure

Traditional broadcast television is structurally organized around ad-slot
economics, not pure storytelling logic. A one-hour drama script typically
runs: Teaser (2-5 pages), then Act One through Act Three or Four, sometimes
a fifth act or a Tag; historically four acts allowed commercial insertion
roughly every 13 minutes, and many contemporary hour dramas now use five
acts. Half-hour comedies typically run a cold open into three or four acts
(single-camera vs. multi-camera affects the count), landing at 22-36
pages.[^1] The defining mechanical unit is the **act-out**: each act is
built to end on a hook, cliffhanger, or twist strong enough to survive the
commercial interruption and pull the viewer back, with the first and
strongest act-out conventionally placed at the end of Act One.[^2]

**Streaming and premium cable** are commonly described as more feature-like
and continuous, since there is no contractual requirement to break for ads.
This is the simplified, traditional framing, and it is already eroding:
ad-supported streaming tiers are pulling act-break-style structuring back
into practice, and some streaming/cable writers still draft with act breaks
marked internally for pacing (and potential future ad insertion), then
compress or remove them if the platform doesn't require them. The more
accurate claim: premium/no-ad platforms remove the *requirement* for
act-outs, not necessarily the practice of structuring in act-like
movements; many streaming dramas still use internal "part" breaks that
function dramatically like acts without serving commercial timing.[^3]

### (b) The pilot's "world" and "engine"

Pilot craft literature consistently frames a successful TV pilot as needing
to accomplish two jobs at once: establish the show's **world** (setting,
tone, thematic focus, scope; TV shows are described as world-driven,
unlike feature films, which are concept-driven) and establish the
**engine**: a self-sustaining, repeatable conflict-generating mechanism
that can produce new episodes indefinitely, distinct from a feature's
one-and-done arc.[^4]

The clearest, most citable articulation of "engine" comes from screenwriter
Erik Bork (*Band of Brothers*), who defines it as an ongoing source of
problematic situations for the characters that creates new story in every
episode. Bork frames the pilot's job as demonstrating, concretely, how that
engine works: showing who these people and problems/goals are via one
illustrative episode-length problem, proving the premise can sustain
multiple seasons rather than just prologuing a larger story.[^5]

No formal originator was found for "world + engine" as a single coined
term; no single screenwriting educator or showrunner is credited with
pairing these two words into one named framework. "World" and "(story)
engine" are two independently established concepts in TV development craft
that are routinely discussed together as the pilot's dual mandate; the
"engine" half is the more traceable of the two. Treat "world + engine" as a
widely diffused convention rather than a coined term with a single
source.[^5]

### (c) Episodic vs. serialized vs. hybrid structure

**Episodic (procedural)** structure: self-contained installments with low
serial dependence, a "case of the week" format resolved within the
episode, favoring syndication and out-of-order viewing; scenes and acts are
built to open and close a discrete problem within the episode's runtime.

**Serialized** structure: a continuous, arc-driven narrative that carries
unresolved threads across episodes and seasons; act breaks and scenes are
constructed to advance ongoing character and plot arcs rather than resolve
independently, rewarding sequential viewing.

**Hybrid** shows (the majority of contemporary hour dramas) combine an
episodic A-story (a case or mission of the week) with a serialized B-story
or season arc. This affects act construction directly: procedural act-outs
tend to escalate the case of the week, while the serialized thread's beats
are often seeded at act transitions to keep season-long stakes present
without stalling the episodic engine.[^6]

### (d) Other screen-specific TV conventions

A **teaser** or **cold open** is a pre-title-sequence scene that either
hooks the audience into the episode's central conflict or mystery (teaser,
common in procedurals and drama) or plays a self-contained button or joke
before the titles (cold open, common in comedy); the terms are often used
interchangeably in craft literature, though "teaser" trends toward drama
usage and "cold open" toward comedy.[^7] Runtime norms: hour-long drama
scripts run roughly 45-60+ pages (accounting for a roughly 42-48-minute
network hour after ads, longer for premium/streaming hours); half-hour
comedy scripts run 22-36 pages. Craft guides consistently teach placing the
strongest hook at the end of Act One specifically, with escalating (not
necessarily equal) hooks through subsequent act-outs, and the final act or
tag resolving the primary episodic conflict while planting a hook for the
next episode.

### (e) The core adaptation challenge: externalizing interiority

The single most repeated claim across adaptation-craft sources: prose gives
direct, privileged access to a character's internal thought (interior
monologue, extended introspection, authorial narration of feeling), and the
camera cannot show thought; it can only show what is externally visible or
audible. This is described as the defining translation problem of
adaptation, not a peripheral one, and is the same externalization
constraint covered in `references/screenplay-format-and-externalization.md`,
applied specifically to converting an existing prose work.[^8]

### (f) Common adaptation techniques

1. **Cutting and combining subplots and characters.** Adaptation requires
   aggressive compression, commonly cited as roughly a 300-page novel
   compressing to a 100-120-page screenplay. A governing test: does this
   serve the protagonist's arc? If not, it goes. A concrete technique for
   character reduction: when a novel has many minor antagonists or
   supporting players, roll them into fewer, stronger composite
   characters.[^9]
2. **Converting internal monologue into dialogue, action, or visual
   metaphor.** The craft consensus is that the solution is not direct
   translation (dumping monologue into voice-over) but finding the external
   behavioral or visual equivalent of the internal beat: the same
   techniques covered in `references/screenplay-format-and-externalization.md`
   (subtext, physical action as emotional proxy, visual correlative).
   Narration/voice-over is treated as a last resort, usable but risky.
3. **Restructuring non-linear or discursive prose into visual scene
   structure.** Because novels aren't obligated to follow three-act
   causality, adapters typically rebuild the story around a clear inciting
   incident, midpoint shift, and climax that the source material may not
   have organized explicitly; the adapter is not copying the source's
   structure but creating one.[^10]
4. **Finding the dramatic spine.** The "spine" is the drive behind the
   protagonist's actions: what propels the protagonist forward. Applied to
   adaptation, this becomes a filtering question: what is this story really
   about? Practical version: establish point of view first (which character
   has the most dramatic experience), then find one ordeal that epitomizes
   the character's personality, conflict, or growth, illustrated by the
   film adaptation of *Primal Fear*, which narrows the novel's scope to the
   protagonist's most dramatically concentrated period, cutting his
   backstory years entirely.[^11]

### (g) Fidelity vs. transformation

Professional craft opinion is unusually one-sided: strict faithfulness to
source material is broadly treated as bad practice, not a virtue. An
entirely faithful adaptation is described as useless, since reading is a
highly individual act of cognition and interpretation; no film can match
every reader's internal version of the book, and demanding it dismisses the
adapter's own legitimate creative response. One argued alternative standard
is to prioritize tone and mood over plot and dialogue adherence, citing
Sofia Coppola's *The Virgin Suicides* as a film that captures a source
novel's atmosphere through music and visual mood rather than plot fidelity,
treating the resulting film as an artwork in its own right.[^12]

Linda Seger's *The Art of Adaptation: Turning Fact and Fiction into Film*
(1992) is the standard-bearer craft text in this space, still used in
university screenwriting and adaptation courses. Secondary summaries
describe her core framing as producing a "second original": a new work
honoring the source's essence while being reinvented for a visual,
action-driven medium rather than one built on words, interiority, or fluid
literary time. (This "second original" characterization is drawn from
summaries of Seger's approach rather than a verified direct quote from the
primary 1992 text; treat it as a reasonable paraphrase, not a citation of
her exact words.)[^13]

### (h) Identifying and converting unfilmable scenes

The consistent test offered across sources: ask "can this be shown?" If a
book moment exists purely in a character's internal thought with no visual
or behavioral counterpart, it's a candidate for either transformation into
an external dramatization (behavior, image, confrontation, action) or
outright cutting if it doesn't serve the protagonist's spine. "Unfilmable"
scenes are typically those relying on literary devices with no direct
screen equivalent: metaphors that must become visual motifs, internal
monologues that must become dramatic confrontations.[^14]

## Anti-Patterns

**Forcing act-outs onto a no-ad platform out of habit.** Writing rigid,
commercial-style cliffhangers into a streaming script with no ad breaks,
purely out of formatting habit. Diagnose by asking whether the platform
actually requires the break; if not, use the act-like movement only where
it serves pacing, not as a mechanical requirement.

**A pilot that builds a world with no engine.** A pilot that richly
establishes setting, tone, and characters but gives no indication of a
repeatable, ongoing source of conflict, leaving no visible answer to "what
does episode two look like?" Diagnose by asking whether the premise could
generate a plausible next episode's problem without a full re-pitch. Fix by
identifying the standing conflict-generating mechanism and demonstrating it
concretely within the pilot, per Bork's "engine" definition.

**Faithful-translation adaptation.** Attempting to preserve every scene,
subplot, and line of a source novel rather than rebuilding it for the
screen. Diagnose by checking runtime math: a page-for-page translation of a
300-page novel produces an unproducible three-hour-plus script. Fix by
applying the dramatic-spine filter: cut or combine anything that doesn't
serve the protagonist's arc, and treat fidelity to tone/theme, not plot
detail, as the goal.

**Direct-narration adaptation of interior monologue.** Converting a novel's
internal monologue into unbroken voice-over rather than finding its
external, behavioral, or visual equivalent. Diagnose by checking whether
the voice-over restates something the visuals could show. Fix using the
externalization techniques in
`references/screenplay-format-and-externalization.md`.
