## 1. Overview — NC's Regulatory Landscape for Recovery Housing

**Recovery housing** (also called sober living, recovery residences, or halfway houses) occupies a distinctive regulatory space in NC:

- NC does NOT currently require state licensure for basic recovery housing / sober living homes
- Voluntary certification is available through **NCARR** (NC Association of Recovery Residences), the state affiliate of the **National Alliance for Recovery Residences (NARR)**
- Federal Fair Housing Act (FHA) protections significantly limit local governments' ability to restrict recovery housing
- Once a recovery residence crosses certain clinical service thresholds, it may become a **licensed facility** under NC DHSR MHLCS (see Section 9)

---

## 2. No State Licensure Requirement (as of 2026)

**North Carolina does not require state licensure for basic sober living homes / recovery residences.** This distinguishes recovery housing from SUD treatment facilities.[^1]

Key distinctions that determine whether licensure is required:
- A recovery residence that provides **SUD treatment services** (therapy, clinical assessments, medication dispensing) is a **licensable facility** under GS 122C — and must hold a DHSR MHLCS license
- A recovery residence that provides **peer support, structured living, and sobriety requirements** without clinical treatment services generally does NOT require state licensure

**Important:** Even without state licensure requirements, recovery residences must comply with:
- Federal Fair Housing Act (FHA) reasonable accommodation requirements
- Local building codes, fire codes, and occupancy standards
- Any applicable local municipality licensing or registration requirements

---

## 3. NCARR — NC Association of Recovery Residences

The **North Carolina Association of Recovery Residences (NCARR)** is the official state affiliate of **NARR (National Alliance for Recovery Residences)** in NC.[^1][^2]

**What NCARR does:**
- Certifies recovery residences throughout NC against NARR standards
- Provides training and technical assistance to recovery residence operators
- Maintains a directory of NARR-certified recovery residences in NC
- Advocates for recovery housing policy in NC

**Why NCARR certification matters:**
- **Government funding:** Most opioid settlement fund programs, SAMHSA grants, and Medicaid-aligned recovery housing supports require or strongly prefer NARR-certified homes
- **Referrals:** LME-MCOs, treatment programs, and drug treatment courts often refer only to NCARR-certified residences
- **Consumer trust:** Certification signals that the residence meets minimum safety, ethics, and recovery-support standards

---

## 4. NARR Certification Levels

NARR uses a **4-level certification model** based on the intensity of support provided:[^2]

| Level | Type | Key Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 1** | Peer-run | Democratically run; residents manage the home; no paid staff; Oxford House is the paradigmatic Level 1 |
| **Level 2** | Monitored | House manager present; structured environment; house rules enforced; most common type |
| **Level 3** | Supervised | On-site paid supervisor; clinical services accessed off-site; structured programming |
| **Level 4** | Service provider | Integrates clinical services on-site; adjacent to licensing territory |

Note: Level 4 residences that provide on-site clinical SUD services may cross the threshold into DHSR MHLCS licensing territory under GS 122C (see Section 9).

NCARR certifies residences at all four levels against the applicable NARR standards for each level.

## 5. Oxford House in NC

**Oxford House** is a Level 1 NARR model of peer-run recovery residences. NC has one of the largest Oxford House networks in the nation.[^1]

**Oxford House NC statistics (verified-as-of: 2026-06-22):**
- Approximately **320 Oxford Houses** operating statewide as of February 2025
- Houses statewide across urban and rural NC

**Oxford House model:**
- Resident-governed with no central oversight
- Residents pay rent collectively; no subsidy required
- House expels any resident who uses alcohol or drugs
- No fixed program duration — residents stay as long as they wish while maintaining sobriety and paying rent
- No clinical services on-site — purely a peer-run supportive housing model
- Chartered by Oxford House, Inc. (national organization)

**Who benefits:** People further along in recovery who need affordable, sober housing but not intensive clinical services. Oxford Houses work well post-residential treatment or post-incarceration.

## 6. Federal Protections — Fair Housing Act and ADA

Persons in recovery from SUD (including those in MAT) are protected under the **Fair Housing Act (FHA)** and the **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)** as persons with a disability — as long as they are not currently using illegal drugs.[^3]

**Key FHA protections for recovery housing:**

- **Disparate treatment:** Local governments may not apply different zoning rules to recovery residences because of the disability status of residents
- **Reasonable accommodation:** Recovery residences are entitled to request reasonable accommodations from local governments (e.g., zoning variances, exceptions to occupancy limits)
- **Reasonable modification:** Physical modifications to the residence to accommodate residents with disabilities are protected

**Limits:** FHA protections do NOT protect persons currently engaged in illegal drug use. The protections apply to persons "currently engaging in the illegal use of drugs" being excluded — but persons in recovery (not currently using) and persons on MAT (who are not "currently engaging in illegal use") are covered.

**HUD enforcement:** The HUD Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity (FHEO) handles FHA complaints regarding recovery housing discrimination.

## 7. Local Zoning and Municipal Requirements

Even without state licensure requirements, **local zoning ordinances can affect recovery housing**:[^3]

**What local governments CAN do:**
- Apply neutral, generally applicable zoning rules (e.g., single-family residential zoning)
- Require business registration or occupancy permits that apply equally to all similar residential uses
- Enforce fire, building, and health codes uniformly

**What local governments CANNOT do (FHA/ADA limits):**
- Apply zoning rules specifically targeting recovery housing that are more restrictive than for comparable residential uses
- Deny reasonable accommodation requests without individualized analysis
- Use neighborhood opposition alone as grounds for denial

**Local registration:** Some NC municipalities (Charlotte, Raleigh, etc.) have or are developing registration/certification requirements for sober living homes. Verify with the specific local government before opening a recovery residence.

## 8. Medicaid and Opioid Settlement Funding for Recovery Housing

Recovery housing is an allowable use under NC opioid settlement fund Exhibit A ("recovery housing support").[^4]

**Funding pathways:**

| Source | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **NC opioid settlement funds** | County/municipality Exhibit A spending; county contracts with NCARR-certified residences |
| **SAMHSA grants** | Substance Abuse Prevention and Treatment Block Grant (SABG); recovery housing grants |
| **NC Medicaid / LME-MCO** | Transitional living services as part of SUD treatment continuum; NCARR certification often required |
| **HUD / Section 8** | Housing Choice Vouchers (if operator participates and resident meets housing program criteria) |
| **Oxford House loans** | Federal outpatient treatment loans for Oxford House startup (Congress specifically authorized) |

**NCARR certification as funding prerequisite:** Most government funding sources require or strongly prefer NARR-certified homes. Certification is not optional if a residence wants to participate in the funded network.

## 9. DHSR Licensing Threshold — When Recovery Housing Becomes a Licensed Facility

Under GS 122C-3(14)(b), a facility becomes a **licensable facility** if it provides services to SUD individuals and offers:[^5]
- Day services (3+ hours) to the same individual during a 24-hour period, OR
- Residential services for 24+ consecutive hours

**The key distinction:** Providing a **structured living environment with peer support** (without clinical services) does not cross the licensure threshold. Adding on-site **clinical treatment services** (therapy sessions, clinical assessments, MAT dispensing) triggers the licensure requirement.

**Gray areas:**
- A recovery residence that employs clinical staff who provide therapy on-site → likely licensable
- A recovery residence that provides peer support only (even intensive) → likely not licensable
- A recovery residence that co-locates with an SAIOP program run by a licensed entity → the residential component may not be licensable, but the SAIOP component is

When in doubt: contact NC DHSR MHLCS for a project-specific determination.

## 10. Anti-Patterns / Common Misunderstandings

**"All sober living homes in NC must be licensed by the state"**
No. NC does not require state licensure for basic recovery residences. Licensure is required only when clinical treatment services (3+ hours/day or residential clinical treatment) are provided on-site.[^5]

**"NCARR certification is the same as a state license"**
No. NCARR certification is a voluntary quality certification by a private nonprofit (state affiliate of NARR). It is not a state license and does not substitute for DHSR MHLCS licensure if clinical services are provided.[^1]

**"FHA protects people actively using drugs in recovery housing"**
No. FHA protections apply to persons in recovery who are not currently using illegal substances. Persons currently engaged in illegal drug use are not protected under the FHA disability provisions. Recovery residences may legitimately require abstinence as a condition of residency.[^3]

**"Oxford Houses receive government funding or oversight"**
Oxford Houses are peer-run and self-supporting — residents pay rent collectively. They are not funded by NCDHHS or subject to government clinical oversight. They may, however, benefit indirectly from federal Oxford House loan programs.[^1]
