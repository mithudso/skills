# Narrative Construction & POV Craft

Every narrative decision downstream of "what happens" answers two prior
questions: **whose consciousness does the reader live inside, and how close do
they sit to it?** Point of view names the vantage (first person, limited third,
omniscient third, second person). Narrative distance names the proximity:
whether the prose reports a character from across the room or dissolves into
their raw perception. These two dials, plus the unit you build scenes from
(Swain's scene/sequel), how you juggle more than one viewpoint, your tense, and
the voice that carries all of it, are the machinery of narrative construction.
Get them coherent and the reader forgets they are reading; get them muddled and
the reader keeps bumping into the author. This reference covers the choices and
their trade-offs; for the *shape* of the events themselves see the plot sibling,
and for rendering feeling on the page see the emotion sibling.

## Core Concepts

### (a) POV taxonomy and trade-offs

Four vantages dominate prose fiction. Each trades intimacy against reach.[^1][^2][^6]

- **First person ("I"):** the narrator is a character. Maximum intimacy and
  voice; the reader is locked to one consciousness and can be misled by it (the
  unreliable narrator).[^1] Cost: you can only show what this person witnesses,
  and describing the narrator's own appearance or interiority from outside is
  awkward.
- **Close (limited) third person ("she"):** the camera rides one character's
  shoulder and reports only what that character perceives, thinks, and feels.
  Nearly the intimacy of first person with the flexibility of third; the
  workhorse of modern commercial fiction, especially mystery and suspense, where
  controlled ignorance drives the plot.[^2][^6] At its tightest it becomes
  **deep POV**, where filter words ("she saw," "she felt") drop away and the
  narration fuses with the character's idiom.[^10]
- **Omniscient third:** a single narrating intelligence that knows all
  characters and can range across time and space, dipping into any mind at
  will.[^3] Flexible and panoramic, but the hardest to execute: it invites
  telling-over-showing, exposition dumps, and, critically, is *not* a license
  to hop between characters' heads mid-scene (see Anti-Patterns).[^3][^7]
- **Second person ("you"):** addresses the reader as protagonist. The most
  intimate and the most conspicuous; it wears on the reader over novel length,
  so it usually suits short fiction or a framing device.[^5]

**Worked example** of one moment rendered four ways:

- First: *I saw the letter on the table and knew, before I touched it, that
  everything was about to change.*
- Close third: *She saw the letter on the table. She knew, before she touched
  it, that everything was about to change.*
- Omniscient: *The letter had lain on the table for an hour. She did not yet
  know what it would cost her; upstairs, the man who sent it already did.*
- Second: *You see the letter on the table. You know, before you touch it, that
  everything is about to change.*

**When to choose:** pick first or deep-close-third when voice and interiority
are the point (character study, unreliable narration, single strong protagonist);
close third when you want intimacy but need to withhold or rotate information;
omniscient when scope, irony, or a distinct narratorial voice matters more than
immersion in one head; second person for a deliberate, usually short, effect.

### (b) Narrative (psychic) distance and modulating it within a scene

Narrative distance (John Gardner's term is **psychic distance**) is how near
the prose sits to a character's consciousness, independent of which POV you
chose.[^4] Gardner illustrated it in *The Art of Fiction* with a continuum of
example sentences running from remote to fully interior. He did not label the
levels; the "far/close" tags are later convention:[^4]

1. *It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway.*
2. *Henry J. Warburton had never much cared for snowstorms.*
3. *Henry hated snowstorms.*
4. *God, how he hated these damn snowstorms.*
5. *Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up
   your miserable soul.*

Sentence 1 views the scene from orbit; by sentence 5 the narration has vanished
into the character's raw perception. **Modulating** distance means moving along
this continuum on purpose, often like a camera zoom: opening a chapter wide for
orientation, then pulling in tight as emotion rises.[^4] The craft is to change
gears in *stages* rather than jump-cut. Ease out of one mind to a level where
the narrator is in charge before easing into the next, so the reader never feels
the lurch.[^4]

**Worked example:** open wide, *The station had emptied by midnight.* Step in:
*Dana hadn't meant to be the last one on the platform.* Go close: *Empty. Cold
seeping through her coat. Just get home, just get home.* The paragraph traveled
from level 1 to level 5 without a seam.

**When to choose:** pull far out for setting, transitions, the passage of time,
or ironic authorial commentary; move close for the beats that must land
emotionally. Distance is a *within-scene* tool, not a fixed setting, so move it
deliberately (see "distance whiplash").

### (c) Scene and sequel construction (Swain's unit)

Dwight V. Swain, in *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965), defined the
cause-and-effect building block of dramatic fiction as an alternation of **scene**
and **sequel**.[^1] Jack Bickham later systematized it in *Scene & Structure*.[^1]

- **Scene (proactive, action):** **Goal**, what the POV character wants now;
  **Conflict**, the escalating obstacles to that goal; **Disaster**, the
  outcome, usually a setback that leaves them worse off. (Sources vary on the
  third term: Swain's "disaster," Bickham's "disaster," Ingermanson's "setback,"
  Weiland's "outcome" all name the same beat.)[^1][^3]
- **Sequel (reactive, processing):** **Reaction**, the emotional and physical
  fallout; **Dilemma**, weighing bad options; **Decision**, choosing the next
  course, which becomes the *goal* of the next scene.[^2]

The decision-to-goal handoff is what welds units into a chain: each scene's
disaster forces a sequel, whose decision launches the next scene. String these
and you have a plot that reads as inevitable rather than episodic.[^1]

**Worked example:**
- *Scene.* Goal: Mara wants the archivist to unseal her father's file. Conflict:
  he refuses, cites policy, stalls; she escalates, offers money. Disaster:
  security ejects her and the file is now flagged, harder to reach than before.
- *Sequel.* Reaction: outside, shaking, humiliated and furious. Dilemma: go home
  beaten, break in illegally (risking arrest), or find the clerk who owed her
  father a favor. Decision: she approaches the clerk, which is the goal that
  opens the next scene.

Chapters are a separate, elastic container: a chapter may hold several
scene/sequel units, or a single scene may span chapters, and sequels are often
compressed to a sentence or cut when pace demands.[^3]

**When to choose:** use full scene/sequel as a **diagnostic** whenever a draft
feels flat, aimless, or "and then, and then." Usually a scene is missing its
goal or its disaster, or a needed sequel was skipped so a character's turn feels
unmotivated. Not every beat needs all six parts on the page.

### (d) Managing multiple POV characters

Multiple viewpoints buy dramatic irony (the reader knows what a character
doesn't), scope, and access to scenes the protagonist can't attend.[^2] The
governing rule for limited POV: **one viewpoint character per scene**, with any
switch signaled by an unmistakable boundary such as a chapter break or a section
break (blank line or asterisks).[^2][^7] Anchor each POV early (name, sensory
frame, a thought) so the reader re-orients instantly. Discipline questions: does
each POV carry information no other POV can? Are there few enough that the reader
can hold them? Does each voice feel distinct (see (f))?

**Worked example:** Chapter 7 stays entirely in Mara's head at the archive. A
section break (`* * *`) opens Chapter 8 with *The archivist waited until her
footsteps faded before he made the call.* That is a clean handoff to his POV,
its own scene, no blending.

**When to choose:** rotate POV when the story genuinely needs eyes in more than
one place (epics, thrillers with parallel threads, romance with dual leads).
Stay single-POV when intimacy and a tight information economy matter more.
Omniscient is the alternative when you want cross-character knowledge *without*
switching viewpoint characters at all.[^3]

### (e) Tense choice trade-offs

Past tense is the default and the least conspicuous; present tense trades
neutrality for immediacy.[^8]

- **Present tense** feels like real-time, film-like immediacy and pairs well
  with tight deep-POV over a short story span. Costs: it forfeits the suspense
  that only hindsight creates (a past-tense narrator implies survival and
  selection), it can bog down in mundane play-by-play, and it gives you only a
  narrow band of verb tenses to sequence events with.[^8]
- **Past tense** grants the full range of English tenses (letting you flash
  back, summarize, and leap across time cleanly), is easier to sustain, and
  reads as invisible to most readers. Cost: a small loss of immediacy.[^8]

**Worked example:** *She turned the key and the door swung open on an empty
room.* (past) versus *She turns the key and the door swings open on an empty
room.* (present). The present version feels closer and more urgent; the past
version disappears and lets the room, not the tense, land.

**When to choose:** past tense for stories spanning years, many POVs, or heavy
narration; present tense for a short, film-like, single-POV story where
immediacy is the whole effect. When unsure, choose past; it fights you least.[^8]

### (f) Voice consistency

Narrative voice is the recognizable personality of the narration, built from
**diction** (word choice), **syntax** (sentence construction and length),
**rhythm**, and what the narrator notices and judges.[^9] In first person and
deep third, voice belongs to the *character*; in omniscient it belongs to the
*narrator*. Consistency keeps the reader inside one coherent intelligence;
inconsistency reads as the author's hand slipping through. The practical tool is
a **style profile or voice sheet** per narrator: characteristic phrasings,
attitudes, vocabulary level, and sentence habits, checked against as you draft
and revise. Voice may *evolve* deliberately to track character growth, but only
gradually; reading aloud is the fastest way to catch a line that has drifted out
of character.[^9]

**Worked example:** the same observation, two voices. Terse ex-soldier:
*The room was wrong. Too clean. Somebody had scrubbed it.* Ornate academic
narrator: *There was, to the room, a fastidiousness that struck me as unnatural,
as though its usual disorder had been not tidied but abolished.* Same fact,
different mind. If paragraph one sounds like the soldier and paragraph two like
the academic within a single narrator, the voice has fractured.

**When to choose:** invest hardest in voice for first-person and deep-third
narratives, where voice *is* characterization, and for multi-POV books, where
each viewpoint must sound distinct enough to identify without a name tag.

## Anti-Patterns

**Head-hopping.** Switching which character's thoughts we're in *within a single
scene*, with no break. *Symptom:* the reader gets Mara's private thought, then
the archivist's, in adjacent sentences, and has to reread to learn whose head
they're in.[^7] It is often mistaken for omniscient POV, but true omniscient
speaks in one narratorial voice that *reports* on characters from outside; it
does not dive into and out of interiors sentence by sentence.[^3][^7]
*Diagnose:* highlight every interior thought or feeling in a scene and mark whose
it is; more than one owner with no section break is head-hopping. *Fix:* pick one
viewpoint character for the scene and cut or externalize the others' interiority
(show it through observable behavior), or move the second character's inner life
to its own scene behind a break.[^7]

**POV drift.** Staying nominally in one character's POV but slipping in facts
they can't know, as in *She didn't notice the guard smirk behind her.* If she
didn't notice it, the limited POV can't report it. *Diagnose:* audit for anything
the viewpoint character couldn't perceive or wouldn't think. *Fix:* re-render
strictly through their senses (*Behind her, she heard the guard exhale, a sound
too much like a laugh.*).

**Distance whiplash.** Lurching between psychic-distance levels without staging:
a deep-interior sentence jammed against a remote authorial one, so the reader
feels yanked. *Diagnose:* number the distance level of each sentence in a rough
patch (1 to 5, per (b)); non-adjacent jumps with no transition are the culprit.
*Fix:* insert an intermediate-distance beat to bridge, or hold one level for the
passage.[^4]

**Voice inconsistency across chapters.** A narrator who is clipped and profane in
Chapter 2 and suddenly lyrical and Latinate in Chapter 9, with no in-story
reason. *Diagnose:* read the two chapters aloud back to back, or check each
against the voice sheet. *Fix:* revise the outlier to the established diction,
syntax, and rhythm; or, if the shift is intentional (growth, deterioration),
make it gradual and motivated rather than abrupt.[^9]
