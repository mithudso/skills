## 1. Good Samaritan Law — GS 90-96.2

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

### Overview

North Carolina enacted its first Good Samaritan (overdose immunity) statute in 2013, subsequently expanded in 2023 and 2025. GS 90-96.2 provides *limited* criminal immunity — not blanket protection — to persons who seek emergency help during a drug overdose.[^1]

### Who Qualifies: The Samaritan

A person calling for help ("the samaritan") is immune from prosecution if they:

1. **Called for help** — contacted 911, law enforcement, or EMS for a drug-related overdose
2. **Acted in good faith** — had a reasonable belief they were the first to call
3. **Provided their name** to responding authorities
4. **Were not executing a warrant** or conducting a lawful search themselves
5. **Evidence against them arose from** their help-seeking action (not other independent activity)

### Who Qualifies: The Overdose Victim

The overdosing person themselves receives the **same immunity** as the samaritan, with one difference: the victim need NOT provide their name.[^1][^2]

### Key Definition

A **"drug-related overdose"** means an acute condition — including mania, hysteria, extreme physical illness, coma, or death — resulting from the consumption or use of a controlled substance (or combined substance) that a layperson would reasonably believe requires medical assistance.[^2]

### Covered Offenses (Post-2023)

GS 90-96.2 immunity applies only to these specific charges:[^1][^2]

| Category | What's Covered |
|---|---|
| Misdemeanor drug possession | Any misdemeanor violation of GS 90-95(a)(3) |
| Small-amount felony possession | Felony possession of **less than 1 gram** of any controlled substance |
| Drug paraphernalia | Violations of GS 90-113.22 or GS 90-113.154(b)(1) |
| Embalming fluid (2025) | Class I felony possession of **less than 28 grams** of embalming fluid (effective Dec. 1, 2025) |

### Key Legislative History

| Year | Session Law | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | Original enactment | Covered cocaine, heroin, paraphernalia, misdemeanor possession |
| 2023 | SL 2023-123 (S.B. 189) | Replaced "cocaine/heroin" with **"any controlled substance"** — covering fentanyl, methamphetamine, etc. |
| 2025 | SL 2025-71 | Added criminal possession of embalming fluid (a street drug adulterant) |

### Probation/Parole/Pretrial Release Protection

Under GS 90-96.2(c1): a person who qualifies for immunity **cannot be arrested for or have revoked** their:
- Pretrial release conditions
- Probation
- Parole
- Post-release supervision

...if the arrest or revocation would be based on an offense covered by subsection (b) or (c) arising from the same incident. An arrest alone does not constitute a new criminal offense for supervisees.[^1][^2]

### Critical Limitations

The law does **NOT**:
- Bar law enforcement from investigating, seizing evidence, or arresting for **other, non-covered crimes** (e.g., trafficking, PWIMSD, weapons)
- Prevent probation officers from conducting drug testing
- Prohibit law enforcement from remaining at the scene
- Create immunity from civil liability in all contexts
- Protect a person who is the subject of a warrant being executed at the scene
- Provide immunity in all contexts — defendants must affirmatively raise and **prove** immunity applies[^3]

### Appellate Note

Per *State v. Branham* (2025): bare assertions of overdose are insufficient; corroborating evidence of acute overdose symptoms (EMS records, hospital records) strengthens immunity claims. A defendant who pleads guilty loses the statutory right to direct appeal from a denied immunity motion; certiorari review is discretionary.[^3]

## 2. Naloxone / Opioid Antagonist Laws — GS 90-12.7 & Standing Orders

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

### GS 90-12.7 Framework

GS 90-12.7 authorizes:

1. **Pharmacists** to dispense FDA-approved opioid antagonists (not just naloxone — any approved antagonist, including naloxone, nalmefene) under a valid standing order or prescription
2. **Governmental and nongovernmental organizations** to distribute opioid antagonists obtained OTC to:
   - Persons at risk of opioid overdose
   - Family, friends, or others in a position to assist at-risk persons
3. **Lay persons** who receive an opioid antagonist to administer it to another person if they have a good-faith belief that person is experiencing a drug overdose and they exercise reasonable care[^4]

### The State Health Director Standing Order

North Carolina's **State Health Director** has signed a standing order that:
- Authorizes **any NC-licensed pharmacist** to dispense opioid antagonists to any person meeting criteria
- Does **not expire** (renewed when the State Health Director changes or new information warrants)
- Requires no additional steps from individual pharmacies — any licensed NC pharmacy can participate once they notify DHHS via an online form[^5]

**Eligibility criteria** under the standing order (any one qualifies):
- Person at risk of opioid overdose due to medical conditions or use history
- Family member, friend, or anyone able to respond to a person at risk
- A person in a position to assist another person at risk

### 2023 Expansion

S.B. 206 (2023-2024 Session) expanded GS 90-12.7's definition of "opioid antagonist" from **naloxone hydrochloride** specifically to **any opioid antagonist approved by the FDA** for treatment of drug overdose — a forward-looking expansion to cover nalmefene (Zurnae) and future approvals.[^4]

### Real-World Availability Gap

A published study found that naloxone was obtainable in only **53% of NC pharmacies** contacted — 26% incorrectly stated a prescription was required, and 21% didn't stock it at all.[^6] This implementation gap is distinct from the legal authorization (which is broad).

## 3. Syringe Exchange Programs — GS 90-113.27

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

### Authorization

Enacted in 2016, GS 90-113.27 authorizes **any governmental or nongovernmental organization** that promotes scientifically proven ways of mitigating health risks associated with drug use to establish and operate a syringe services program (SSP).[^7]

### Program Objectives (Statutory)

1. Reduce spread of HIV, AIDS, viral hepatitis, and other bloodborne diseases
2. Reduce needlestick injuries to law enforcement and emergency personnel
3. Encourage enrollment in evidence-based treatment
4. Reduce drug overdose deaths in NC

### Required Services

All SSPs must provide:
- Disposal of used needles and syringes
- Free needles, syringes, and injection supplies in quantities sufficient to prevent sharing/reuse
- Reasonable site security with written plans shared annually with local police/sheriff
- Educational materials on overdose prevention, disease transmission, treatment referrals
- **Access to opioid antagonist kits** (FDA-approved) or referrals to programs providing access (added 2023)
- Individual consultations on mental health and addiction treatment upon request

### Immunity Protections

| Who | Protection |
|---|---|
| **Employees & volunteers** | No arrest or prosecution for possessing program-supplied needles, syringes, injection supplies, or residual controlled substance amounts |
| **Participants** | Same immunity as employees; must carry **written verification card** from the program |
| **Law enforcement** | Good-faith immunity from civil liability for arrests made under this section |

**Key:** Participant immunity requires the written verification card documenting enrollment; card possession is the practical evidentiary requirement.[^7][^8]

### Registration & Reporting

- SSPs must register with NC DHHS **before** beginning operations
- Annual reporting to DHHS required: individuals served, supplies distributed, antagonist kits provided, treatment referrals made

### NC Safer Syringe Initiative

NC DHHS Division of Public Health operates the **NC Safer Syringe Initiative** in partnership with the NC Harm Reduction Coalition (NCHRC) — providing technical assistance to SSPs (not operating them directly).[^8]

**2023–2024 data:**[^9]
- **16,176+** referrals to SUD or mental health treatment (up **653%** from prior year)
- **8,700+** referrals to wraparound services (food, housing, employment, medical)
- **113,189** naloxone kits distributed

### Implementation Challenges

A 2022 study across seven NC counties found:
- Inconsistent law enforcement application of SSP immunity
- **Coercive encounters** (improper seizure of supplies, seizure of participation cards, or arrest of participants) reported in **every county examined**
- "Complete ignorance of the law" by an officer likely does not qualify as good faith for the civil immunity provision[^7]

## 4. Drug-Checking Equipment (Fentanyl Test Strips) — Current Status

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

### NC Legal Status

NC is one of **seven states** plus DC that includes testing equipment in its statutory definition of **"drug paraphernalia"** but does **not impose criminal penalties** for possession by certain individuals — particularly those in authorized syringe service programs.[^10]

- **No standalone NC law** explicitly decriminalizing fentanyl test strips for all persons
- **SSP participants and staff** covered by GS 90-113.27 immunity are protected from possession charges for equipment obtained through the program
- **SL 2023-123** (S.B. 189) expanded Good Samaritan coverage to any controlled substance but did not explicitly address fentanyl test strips

### Federal Conflict (2026)

As of June 2026:[^11]
- **SAMHSA** banned grant funding for fentanyl test strips and sterile syringes as "facilitating illegal drug use"
- **Contradictory federal signals:** 2026 National Drug Control Strategy and the SUPPORT Act (December 2025) authorize drug-checking equipment
- **NC DHHS** temporarily paused state purchase of fentanyl and xylazine test strips pending SAMHSA clarification
- **Alternative funding:** NC state/local opioid settlement funds remain available
- NC achieved a **~29% drop in overdose deaths** from 2024–2025, which public health experts partly attribute to harm reduction efforts

### Practical Impact

For SSP workers: fentanyl test strips distributed through an authorized GS 90-113.27 program are covered by that program's immunity provisions. For individuals outside SSPs: possession remains technically within the paraphernalia definition, though enforcement is inconsistent.

## 5. Anti-Patterns / Common Misunderstandings

1. **"The Good Samaritan law protects everything."** False — immunity is limited to specific possession offenses and paraphernalia. Trafficking, PWIMSD, weapons, and many other charges are NOT covered.

2. **"You only need to call 911 to qualify."** Not quite — the caller must also act in good faith, provide their name, and reasonably believe they were the first caller. Evidence seized during the call can still be used for non-covered offenses.

3. **"Probation officers can't test me after I call."** Wrong — GS 90-96.2 does not restrict drug testing by supervision officers. It only prohibits revocation based on the covered offense from the overdose incident.

4. **"Any pharmacy must give me naloxone."** Not in practice — legal authority exists under the standing order, but studies show roughly half of NC pharmacies actually dispense it. Law and practice diverge.

5. **"Fentanyl test strips are fully legal in NC."** The status is complex — they're in the paraphernalia definition but penalty-free for SSP participants. Statewide decriminalization for all persons has not been enacted.

6. **"SSP cards protect participants everywhere."** The written verification card is a legal tool but real-world protection is inconsistent — documented coercive encounters occur in every NC county studied.

---

## 6. Practical Guidance for Harm Reduction Workers

- **SSP participants:** Always carry the written verification card. Keep documentation of enrollment for encounters with law enforcement.
- **Calling for an overdose:** Call 911, provide your name, stay on scene if safe. Note that you cannot be revoked from probation/parole for the covered offense from this incident.
- **Drug checking:** If working with an SSP, fentanyl test strips obtained through the program are covered by GS 90-113.27 immunity. If outside an SSP, consult legal counsel.
- **Probationers:** The Good Samaritan law explicitly prohibits arrest for violations of supervised release conditions arising from the covered overdose offense — not from other conditions or offenses.
- **Connecting to treatment:** NC SSPs are required by law to offer treatment referrals. NCCARE360 (NC's care coordination network) provides referrals statewide.
