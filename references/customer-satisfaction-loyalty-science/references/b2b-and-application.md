## 1. Satisfaction vs loyalty vs advocacy — three distinct constructs

These are routinely conflated; they are different. **[F]**

- **Satisfaction** = an attitudinal, post-consumption **evaluation** (Oliver: "pleasurable fulfillment" —
  consumption met a need/goal against a standard). It is the *expectancy-disconfirmation* output
  (`satisfaction-theory.md`).
- **Loyalty** (Oliver 1999) = **"a deeply held commitment to rebuy or repatronize a preferred
  product/service consistently in the future… *despite situational influences and marketing efforts
  having the potential to cause switching.*"** Note the deliberate inclusion of *resistance to
  switching*. Loyalty has two dimensions:
  - **Attitudinal loyalty** — commitment, attachment, intention.
  - **Behavioral loyalty** — observed repeat purchase / share-of-wallet.
  **Repeat buying from inertia, habit, lock-in, or absence of alternatives is NOT true loyalty** —
  "spurious loyalty" (Dick & Basu 1994) / "false loyalty" (Jones & Sasser) / the "hostage." Day (1969)
  first argued loyalty needs *both* attitudinal and behavioral criteria; Han, Kwortnik & Wang (2008)
  show satisfaction → behavioral loyalty is *mediated* by attitudinal loyalty. **[F]**
- **Advocacy** = active **recommendation / referral** (positive word-of-mouth) — a *behavioral* step
  beyond private loyalty. It's what NPS's "would you recommend" tries to capture (and why the
  intention-action gap, `metrics-and-critiques.md` §2, bites: stated recommend-intent ≠ actual
  advocacy). The apostle (`loyalty-profit-chain.md` §2) is the advocate. **[F]**

**Dick & Basu's loyalty 2×2** (relative attitude × repeat patronage): **true loyalty** (high/high),
**latent loyalty** (high attitude / low repeat — blocked by situational factors), **spurious loyalty**
(low attitude / high repeat — inertia/lock-in), **no loyalty** (low/low). **[Q]** This is the cleanest
lens for separating a genuinely loyal account from one that merely hasn't left yet.

## 2. Oliver's four phases of loyalty

Oliver (1999, "Whence Consumer Loyalty?," *Journal of Marketing* 63 Special Issue:33–44) — loyalty
develops sequentially, each phase with its own vulnerability: **[F]**

1. **Cognitive loyalty** — loyalty to *information* (price, features, quality beliefs). Weakest; Oliver
   calls it "phantom loyalty" because it's "directed at costs and benefits, not the brand." Vulnerable to
   a better cost/benefit deal.
2. **Affective loyalty** — loyalty as a *liking/attitude* built from cumulative satisfying experiences.
   Vulnerable to accumulated dissatisfaction and to competitors' imagery/association appeals.
3. **Conative loyalty** — loyalty as *behavioral intention/commitment to rebuy* (a motivation).
   Vulnerable to counter-persuasion (competitive messages, trial-inducing offers).
4. **Action loyalty** — intention converted into *action*, with "action inertia" + willingness to
   overcome obstacles to keep buying; the deepest, most resistant state.

**Oliver's central thesis (the satisfaction-loyalty asymmetry):** *"Although loyal consumers are most
typically satisfied, satisfaction does not universally translate into loyalty… satisfaction is an
unreliable precursor to loyalty."* Satisfaction is a **necessary** step in loyalty formation but becomes
**less** significant as loyalty sets through other mechanisms — **personal determinism ("fortitude")**
and **social bonding**. Ultimate loyalty = perceived product superiority + personal fortitude + social
bonding + their synergy. A "disquieting conclusion": for many firms (commodities, disinterested
consumers) true loyalty is unattainable and "mere satisfaction" is the only feasible goal. **[F]**

**Crucial B2B caveat — Oliver scoped this to B2C.** A footnote in the article: *"The analysis to be
presented is intended to apply to consumer goods and services, **not to the personal relationships in
business-to-business markets. The relationship literature is vast and involves many additional variables,
such as power dependencies**, that would require coverage beyond the intended scope."* → Apply the
four-phase model to B2B accounts **with care**; the social-bonding/fortitude mechanisms work differently
when an account team and contractual switching costs are involved. **[F — load-bearing for B2B use.]**

**Independent corroboration that satisfaction ≠ loyalty.** Kumar, Pozza & Ganesh (2013), "Revisiting the
Satisfaction–Loyalty Relationship," *Journal of Retailing* 89(3):246–262 — the link "is not as strong as
it is believed," the variance in loyalty explained by satisfaction alone is "rather small," moderators/
mediators are needed, and the relationship changes over time. **[F]**

## 3. B2B vs B2C — what changes

**(a) An account is a buying center, not one respondent.** A B2B "customer" is an *account* containing a
**buying center / decision-making unit (DMU)** — economic sponsor, technical buyer, end users,
procurement, IT, finance, champion — each evaluating the vendor on *different criteria* and reaching
*different satisfaction levels*. B2B buying committees commonly run **~7–12 stakeholders**. **[Q]**
(buying-center *theory* is academically grounded; the 7–12 figure is practitioner-sourced, as of 2026.)

- **Best academic source — Austen, Herbst & Bertels (2012), "When 3 + 3 does not equal 5 + 1,"
  *Industrial Marketing Management* 41(6):973–983.** The relationship between *individual* buying-center
  members' satisfaction and
  *conjoint* (account-level) satisfaction is **non-linear — simple averaging is invalid** — and **negative
  individual judgments carry more weight than positive ones** in determining conjoint satisfaction and
  repurchase intention. This is the B2B analogue of Anderson & Mittal's asymmetry: a single dissatisfied
  stakeholder can sink the account number, and averaging hides it. **[F]**

**(b) A single account-level CSAT/NPS hides stakeholder variance.** "High executive satisfaction may mask
daily frustration among users," and vice versa. Practitioner-consensus fixes (as of 2026): per-role
stakeholder mapping, role-segmented scoring, weighting (a detractor *decision-maker* = higher churn risk
than a detractor *end-user*), and treating **high role-divergence within an account as a risk signal in
itself.** The highest churn risk often sits in the roles *least likely to be surveyed* (procurement
governance, reliability engineering, finance). **[Q — practitioner-sourced.]**

**(c) Dissatisfaction ≠ near-term churn (the dangerous lag).** High switching costs (migration,
retraining, re-integration, contract renegotiation) and multi-year contracts mean a customer can be a
genuine detractor with no near-term intent to leave — Jones & Sasser's **"hostage"** / **false loyalty**
in B2B form. Lock-in retention is *not* loyalty; it can flip to active churn (and "terrorist"
word-of-mouth) the moment a credible alternative appears. Skogland & Siguaw (2004) and B2B studies
(n=267 businesses) confirm in *business* settings that satisfaction and attitudinal loyalty are distinct
and high satisfaction doesn't always yield loyalty. **[F]**

**(d) Other structural differences (well-attested, mostly practitioner-sourced). [Q]**
- **Fewer, larger accounts** → concentration risk; account-level (not transaction-level) measurement
  dominates; whale accounts justify bespoke research (win/loss interviews at renewal).
- **The account team is itself a driver** of satisfaction/retention — relationship-based switching costs
  and the social bonding Oliver flags. A TAM can build attitudinal loyalty independent of product
  satisfaction (and a strong relationship can *mask* product dissatisfaction).
- **More "rational" / ROI- and TCO-driven** evaluation (longer cycles, justification to a CFO) vs. B2C's
  more emotional/brand/convenience drivers; the documented TCO ledger is itself the switching cost.
- **Measurement implication:** B2B needs **multi-stakeholder survey logic + role-weighted account-health
  rollups**, not B2C's high-velocity single-respondent transactional surveys.

> *Sourcing note:* the buying-center *theory* and the Austen et al. non-linearity/negativity finding are
> peer-reviewed and strong; the "how to measure B2B satisfaction" prescriptions (role-weighting,
> divergence-as-signal) come from recent industry/vendor sources (2025–2026) that are internally
> consistent but not peer-reviewed. Treat operational prescriptions as practitioner consensus; the
> underlying constructs (buying center, switching costs, satisfaction≠loyalty in B2B) as academically
> grounded.

## 4. How a TAM reads (and moves) an account score — operating synthesis

Pulling the whole skill together into a TAM stance:

1. **Distrust the single number.** No metric is the predictor (`metrics-and-critiques.md` §4). Use NPS as
   a relationship pulse + closed-loop trigger; CSAT (top-box) and CES on specific touchpoints; read them
   together, never as one verdict.
2. **Measure multi-stakeholder; segment by role.** Don't average a buying center (Austen et al. 2012). Map who's
   satisfied/dissatisfied by role, weight the decision-maker, and treat role-divergence as a risk signal.
3. **Watch the asymmetry.** Fix detractors / must-be failures *before* delighting promoters (Anderson &
   Mittal negativity bias + Kano must-be's). A basic-attribute failure (reliability, high-effort support)
   destroys satisfaction faster than any delighter builds it.
4. **Separate true loyalty from lock-in.** A "stable" renewal can be a *hostage* held only by switching
   costs (Jones & Sasser; Dick & Basu spurious loyalty). Ask: is this attitudinal loyalty (they'd choose
   us again) or just high switching costs? The former is durable revenue; the latter is churn waiting for
   an alternative.
5. **Manage expectations, not just delivery.** Satisfaction is *relative* to expectations (EDT) — and
   SERVQUAL Gap 4 says over-promising manufactures dissatisfaction. Set expectations deliberately.
6. **Diagnose shortfalls with the Gaps Model.** A satisfaction miss is a listening gap, a standards gap, a
   delivery gap, or a communication gap (`service-quality-servqual.md` §3) — locate it before "fixing
   satisfaction" generically.
7. **Be honest about evidence.** When you cite a metric or claim to a customer or internally, carry its
   confidence tag and never launder a vendor claim (Bain relative-NPS growth figures, CEB CES
   superiority) into established fact.
