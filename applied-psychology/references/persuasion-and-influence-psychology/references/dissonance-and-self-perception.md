# Cognitive Dissonance & Self-Perception

The consistency engine, plus its main rival account. This is where attitudes
change *because behavior changed first* — the reverse of the persuasion-message
direction.

## Cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957)

### The mechanism
Two inconsistent cognitions — or an act that contradicts an attitude — produce an
unpleasant state of **arousal** ("dissonance"). The drive to reduce it pushes the
person to change the **most-malleable** element. Behavior already happened and is
hard to undo, so the **attitude** usually moves to fit the behavior. Dissonance
can be reduced by: changing an attitude, adding consonant cognitions, trivializing
the inconsistency, or (rarely) changing the behavior.

### The four classic paradigms

**1. Induced compliance (forced compliance).** Get someone to say/do something
counter-attitudinal with **insufficient external justification**, and they shift
their private attitude toward the act.
- *Festinger & Carlsmith (1959):* participants did a boring task, then were paid
  **$1 or $20** to tell the next person it was fun. The **$1** group later rated
  the task as genuinely more enjoyable — they had no large external reason for
  lying, so they resolved the dissonance by believing it. The **$20** group had
  ample justification and felt no dissonance. **Less external pressure → more
  internal attitude change.** This is the counter-intuitive core operators must
  internalize.

**2. Effort justification.** The more effort/cost invested toward a goal, the more
the goal is valued — to justify the effort.
- *Aronson & Mills (1959):* people who underwent a **severe** initiation to join a
  (deliberately dull) group rated it more attractive than those with a mild
  initiation. Explains why a hard-won certification, a grueling onboarding, or an
  expensive migration can *increase* commitment and perceived value.

**3. Free-choice paradigm.** After a difficult choice between **closely-rated**
alternatives, people **spread the alternatives**: they upgrade the chosen option
and downgrade the rejected one, reducing post-choice doubt. The closer the call,
the more spreading.

**4. Belief disconfirmation.** When a strongly held belief is disconfirmed (e.g.,
a failed prophecy), believers often **intensify** the belief and proselytize
rather than abandon it (Festinger's *When Prophecy Fails*).

### Post-decision dissonance / buyer's remorse
Dissonance **peaks immediately after** a hard, committing choice — the moment of
maximum doubt. Practically: right after a champion commits, they are *most*
vulnerable to a competitor's counter-pitch or an internal skeptic. Reinforce the
decision fast (supply consonant cognitions, celebrate the choice) before the doubt
resolves the wrong way.

### Modern refinements (why dissonance "works")
Later accounts narrowed *what* produces dissonance arousal:
- **Aversive-consequences / "New Look" (Cooper & Fazio):** dissonance arises mainly
  when behavior produces a foreseeable **unwanted outcome** for which one feels
  **personally responsible**, not from mere inconsistency.
- **Self-consistency (Aronson):** dissonance is sharpest when the act threatens a
  **positive self-concept** ("I am honest/competent").
- **Self-affirmation (Steele):** affirming an unrelated valued self-domain can
  dissolve the need to change the attitude — people have many ways to restore
  self-integrity.
- A 2020 emotion-regulation review (Frontiers/PMC) reframes all of these as varied
  **strategies for regulating the aversive arousal**.

## Self-perception theory (Bem, 1967) — the rival account

### The claim
People often **don't have** strong pre-existing attitudes; they **infer** their
attitudes the way an outside observer would — by watching their own behavior and
the circumstances. "I chose to do X with no obvious external pressure, so I must
favor X." No inner conflict, no arousal required — pure self-attribution.

Self-perception explains the **same induced-compliance results** as dissonance
(the $1 participant infers "I said it was fun and wasn't paid much, so I must have
found it fun") **without** invoking arousal.

### The decisive distinction
Both theories predict the **same attitude change**. They differ on **arousal**:
dissonance predicts (and lab work confirms via misattribution and physiological
studies) that counter-attitudinal behavior produces real **discomfort/arousal**;
self-perception predicts none.

### The reconciliation (Fazio, Zanna & Cooper, 1977)
Both are right — in different zones:
- **Self-perception** operates in the **latitude of acceptance**: behavior only
  *mildly* discrepant from the existing attitude → calm self-inference.
- **Dissonance** operates in the **latitude of rejection**: behavior *clearly*
  counter-attitudinal → arousal → motivated attitude change.

## Operator translation
- To get **internalized** change, elicit the desired behavior under conditions of
  **free choice and minimal external pressure** (small incentive, not a big one;
  no heavy-handed mandate). Over-justifying (big rewards, threats) kills the
  attitude shift — the person attributes the act to the external reason.
- **Protect** a fresh commitment immediately (post-decision dissonance peaks now).
- A demanding deployment can *raise* perceived value — don't reflexively make
  everything effortless.
