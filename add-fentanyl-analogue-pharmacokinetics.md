# Fentanyl & Illicit Analogue Pharmacokinetics

## Core Concepts
- Fentanyl is a fully synthetic μ-opioid receptor (MOR) full agonist; 100× more potent than morphine, ~50× more potent than heroin by weight
- Extreme lipophilicity (log P ~4.05) drives rapid CNS penetration and redistribution to muscle/fat compartments
- Illicit analogues (carfentanil, acetylfentanyl, butyrfentanyl, furanylfentanyl) share the piperidine scaffold with variable N-substituents that shift potency by orders of magnitude
- Nitazenes (isotonitazene, etonitazene, metonitazene) are structurally unrelated but also high-efficacy MOR full agonists appearing in the illicit supply since ~2019
- Both fentanyl analogues and nitazenes show poor in vitro–to–in vivo potency correlation, complicating risk prediction

## Mechanisms / Biology
- High lipophilicity enables blood–brain barrier crossing in <1 min; peak brain concentrations precede plasma peak — explains ultrafast overdose onset (seconds to minutes vs. ~10 min for heroin)
- MOR activation in the medulla oblongata and pons suppresses respiratory center sensitivity to hypercapnia/hypoxemia → apnea at doses near the analgesic threshold
- Fentanyl undergoes rapid redistribution from brain to muscle/fat, producing a documented secondary plasma-concentration peak ("fentanyl rebound") that can re-sedate after initial naloxone reversal
- Carfentanil and ohmefentanyl show slow MOR dissociation kinetics (hours vs. seconds for morphine), creating pseudo-competitive antagonism: naloxone must compete with drug already "locked" to the receptor
- Nitazenes similarly exhibit slow receptor off-rates (isotonitazene, etonitazene); this mechanism — not just raw potency — explains their reduced in vivo naloxone sensitivity
- Metabolism is primarily hepatic CYP3A4 (norfentanyl + minor hydroxylated metabolites); terminal elimination half-life 3–12 h, but CNS effects outlast plasma levels due to tissue redistribution

## Clinical Picture
- Carfentanil is ~100× more potent than fentanyl (10,000× morphine); microgram-range doses cause fatal respiratory depression — detected in US street supply since 2016
- Overdose triad: pinpoint pupils, unconsciousness, apnea; chest-wall rigidity ("wooden chest syndrome") occurs with rapid high-dose exposure, complicating bag-valve-mask ventilation
- Onset of overdose with smoked/insufflated illicit fentanyl: as fast as 30–90 seconds; leaves bystanders minimal intervention window
- Nitazene overdoses (case reports, EU/US, 2020–2025) present identically to fentanyl OD but with slower withdrawal onset and reportedly more severe withdrawal intensity
- US overdose deaths involving synthetic opioids (primarily fentanyl): >73,000 in 2022 (CDC); fentanyl now present in >85% of opioid overdose deaths

## Evidence & Treatment
- Naloxone reverses all currently detected fentanyl analogues and nitazenes — no analogue is fully naloxone-resistant, but higher and repeated doses are required
- Standard 0.4 mg IM/IV naloxone may be insufficient; 2 mg intranasal (or 2× 1 mg doses) is now recommended by CDC/SAMHSA for suspected synthetic opioid OD; some EMS protocols use 4 mg IN as first dose
- Re-narcotization risk is real: fentanyl's redistribution from peripheral compartments back to CNS after initial naloxone wears off (half-life 30–90 min) requires observation for ≥2 h or continuous naloxone infusion
- No approved pharmacotherapy specifically targets carfentanil or nitazene OD; buprenorphine induction (low-dose or "micro-induction") is the preferred OUD treatment given its partial-agonist ceiling reducing OD risk in maintenance
- PBPK modeling across 34 fentanyl analogues (2025) confirms lipophilicity and slow receptor off-rates as the two strongest predictors of naloxone dose requirement

## Key Facts
- Carfentanil potency: ~10,000× morphine; estimated lethal dose in humans ~20 µg
- Fentanyl blood–brain barrier transit: <60 seconds after IV administration
- Naloxone half-life: 30–90 min vs. fentanyl terminal t½ 3–12 h — mismatch drives re-narcotization
- Nitazene MOR off-rate (isotonitazene): ~10× slower than fentanyl, ~100× slower than morphine
- PBPK models predict acetylfentanyl brain Cmax 3× higher than fentanyl at equivalent plasma dose due to even higher lipophilicity
