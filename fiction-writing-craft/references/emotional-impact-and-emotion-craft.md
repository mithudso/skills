# Emotional Impact, Emotions vs. Feelings & Somatic Craft

"She felt sad" fails not because the sentence is grammatically wrong but
because it hands the reader a label instead of an experience. A label is
processed intellectually, filed, and forgotten; a dramatized bodily state,
action, or perception is processed the way the reader processes their own
felt life, and that is what produces the visceral hit fiction is built to
deliver. The craft move is consistent across every technique in this file:
stop naming the internal state and instead render its physical signature, its
behavioral leakage, or its distortion of what the character notices, and let
the reader do the inferring. This reference covers how to do that without
sliding into two opposite failure modes — clinical flatness (naming) and
melodramatic clutter (over-somaticizing) — and how to keep a scene's
emotional temperature rising instead of restating the same beat.

## Core Concepts

### (a) Show-don't-tell, applied specifically to emotion

The general show-don't-tell rule has a sharp, well-documented edge case in
emotion: naming the emotion word (sad, angry, terrified, devastated) is
almost always the weakest available choice, because it substitutes a summary
for the sensory/behavioral evidence a reader would use to diagnose that
emotion in real life. Chuck Palahniuk's widely cited "thought" verbs essay
argues for banning not just emotion nouns but the whole family of
interiority-summarizing verbs — *thinks, knows, understands, realizes,
remembers, believes, wants* — because each one lets the writer report a
conclusion instead of staging the evidence that would let the reader reach
it themselves.[^1] K.M. Weiland's rule of thumb is blunter still: never name
the emotion; dramatize it so the reader arrives at the label on their own.[^2]

K.M. Weiland's own before/after pair illustrates the gap:[^2]

**Before:** Sam was shocked.

**After:** Sam stopped short and stared, his lungs turning inside out, his
heart trying to thrash its way out of his chest.

**When to use naming anyway:** Labeling is not forbidden, only weak as a
default. A named emotion can work as a compressor for a beat that does not
deserve full dramatization (transitions, minor characters, pacing an
otherwise slow paragraph), or as a one-word landing *after* a dramatized
build-up, where it functions as punctuation rather than substitute
evidence.[^2] The failure mode is using the label as the *only* rendering of
a beat that matters.

### (b) Emotion vs. feeling: the craft distinction

Writers and some psychology-of-emotion sources (notably neuroscientist
Antonio Damasio) draw a working distinction between an **emotion** — the
largely involuntary, bodily reaction that happens *to* a person (adrenaline
spike, muscle tension, flushed skin) — and a **feeling** — the conscious,
named interpretation layered on top of that bodily state after the fact
("I'm angry"). Damasio frames emotions as the (unconscious) bodily change and
feelings as the mind's after-the-fact sensing and labeling of that change.[^3]
For fiction craft this maps directly onto show vs. tell: the emotion is the
material a scene should render (the involuntary, pre-verbal thing happening
in the body), and the feeling-word is the label a character's conscious mind
(or the narrator) might or might not ever put on it. A POV character does
not need to think "I am furious" for the reader to know they are furious; the
white-knuckled grip on the steering wheel and the clipped one-word answers
are the emotion, unlabeled, and readers will supply the feeling-word
themselves.

**Before:** She felt a wave of grief.

**After:** The kettle she'd bought him whistled on, unanswered, until the
sound stopped meaning anything.

**When to use the label:** When a character's conscious *awareness* of their
own feeling — the moment they notice and name it to themselves — is itself
the story point (self-deception breaking down, an alexithymic character
finally finding a word, a therapist scene). In that case the *naming* is the
dramatized event, not a shortcut around one.

### (c) Somatic/body cues as emotion shorthand — and their cliché trap

Physical, bodily signals are the single most common vocabulary for
dramatizing emotion without naming it, because human bodies react to strong
feeling in fairly universal, recognizable ways — racing heart, tight chest,
cold hands, dry mouth, a stomach that drops.[^4] The trap is that this same
universality makes a small handful of phrases wildly overused: "heart
hammering," "stomach dropped," "palms sweating," clenched fists, a hitched
breath, the eyebrow raise. Editors flag the eyes/heart/lungs/stomach cluster
as the "four biggies" precisely because they recur across nearly every
manuscript, to the point that a familiar gesture like wiping tears to signal
crying has become functionally equivalent to writing the word "sad" — it is
as pre-processed and as skippable as a label.[^5][^6] Some craft sources
push back partway: a physiological cue is "not inherently bad," and because
bodies really do react to strong feeling in predictable ways, using one is
acceptable so long as the writer keeps looking for a fresher way to render
the same moment rather than reaching for the same three or four stock
phrases every time.[^6]

**Before:** Her heart pounded and her stomach dropped when she saw the
letter.

**After:** She read the return address twice before she let herself open it,
and even then her fingers wouldn't stop finding the edge of the envelope
after there was nothing left to hold.

Ways to vary the vocabulary beyond stock physiology:

- **Idiosyncratic, character-specific tics** instead of generic ones — what
  *this* character's body does that a different character's would not.[^6]
- **What the character notices** as the emotion made visible: a grieving
  person notices absence; a suspicious person notices exits and unlocked
  doors; attention itself becomes the somatic signal, without a single body
  part being named.[^5]
- **Voice and dialogue distortion** — clipped sentences, interruption,
  a verbal tic surfacing — as an alternative channel to physiology
  entirely.[^4]
- **Context and situation** doing the emotional work so no cue is needed at
  all: a rich enough setup makes the reader supply the feeling without a
  single physical tag.[^6]
- Reference works like Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi's *The Emotion
  Thesaurus* exist specifically to break writers out of their three or four
  default tics by cataloguing a wide range of body-language, thought, and
  visceral options per emotion at varying intensities — useful as a
  brainstorming tool for variety, not as a phrase bank to lift verbatim into
  prose.[^7]

**When to use somatic cues:** As the default rendering technique for
in-scene, moment-to-moment emotion, especially in close POV — but rotate the
specific cue, and prefer cues distinctive to the character over the four
generic zones.

### (d) Why readers feel what characters feel: narrative transportation

The mechanism craft writers gesture at when they say a scene should "pull
the reader in" has a named counterpart in narrative-psychology research:
Green and Brock's narrative transportation theory (2000, *Journal of
Personality and Social Psychology*) describes transportation as an
immersive mental state combining focused attention, mental imagery,
emotional engagement, and a felt detachment from one's actual surroundings —
readers who are highly transported evaluate a story's characters and events
more favorably, notice fewer inconsistencies, and carry the story's
emotional coloring with them afterward.[^8][^9] For working fiction craft
purposes the actionable takeaway is narrower than the full research
literature: transportation is the state you are trying to produce, and every
technique in this file (rendering rather than naming emotion, concrete
somatic and behavioral detail, escalating stakes) is a lever for it because
transportation depends on sustained sensory imagery and emotional
engagement — a labeled, unrendered emotion gives the reader nothing to build
imagery or engagement from, and a scene stuffed with cliché cues gives them
imagery they've already used up on other books.

**Before (low transportation):** The room felt tense.

**After (high transportation):** Nobody had moved in the last thirty
seconds. Even the ice in the untouched glasses had stopped clinking.

**When to use:** treat transportation as the "why it works" footnote behind
every other technique in this file, not as a separate checklist to apply
scene by scene — reach for it when you need to explain *why* a rendered
beat outperforms a labeled one, not as a new rule to layer on top.

### (e) Escalating emotional stakes across a scene or chapter

A single well-rendered emotional beat is not enough if the scene needs to
build; craft guidance on tension and stakes converges on a few structural
moves rather than a formula:

- **Escalate one character's stakes at a time** rather than raising the
  temperature for everyone simultaneously — diffuse stakes read as noise,
  concentrated stakes read as tension.[^10]
- **Keep external stakes tethered to a personal cost.** An abstract threat
  (the building might collapse) only lands emotionally once it is translated
  into what a specific character has to lose (their daughter is inside).[^10]
- **Escalation does not require volume.** Stakes can rise quietly — a
  secret nearly surfacing, a relationship straining by degrees, a character
  inching toward a flaw they've been avoiding — as legitimately as they can
  rise through shouting or violence.[^11]
- **Build in beats, not jumps.** Each new beat in the scene should cost the
  character slightly more than the last (a harder question, a smaller
  window to answer it, less deniability) so the reader can feel the ratchet
  turning rather than experiencing a flat plateau followed by a sudden
  spike.
- **Anchor tension to investment, not event.** Readers do not track plot
  events for their own sake; they track what a character they're invested
  in stands to gain or lose, so stakes-escalation only pays off if the
  reader already cares about the character carrying them.[^10]

**Before (flat, no escalation):** She was worried about the test results.
She was still worried the next day. By the time the doctor called, she was
very worried.

**After (escalating):** She told herself three days was normal. By the
second day she'd stopped checking her phone on purpose, which was its own
kind of checking. By the third, she'd rehearsed both answers — the one where
she cried and the one where she didn't — often enough that neither felt like
hers anymore.

**When to use:** default to escalating whenever a scene or chapter sustains
one emotional thread across multiple beats; a single isolated beat (a short
scene, a minor character's reaction) does not need the full ratchet.

### (f) Calibrating emotional register to POV distance

How much somatic and interior detail a passage can carry is bounded by how
close the narration is sitting to the character — a decision this hub covers
in full under narrative distance.[^12] As a rule of thumb for emotion
rendering specifically: distant/omniscient narration can name an emotion in
summary without it reading as weak, because the convention at that distance
is report rather than immersion; close third or first person cannot get
away with the same naming, because the whole contract of close POV is that
the reader is inside the sensation, not being briefed on it from outside.
When a scene's emotional beat matters, that is usually also the moment to
dial the narration closer, not just to add more somatic detail at a
constant distance.

**Before (distant — naming works at this remove):** The whole town agreed,
afterward, that Marta must have been terrified that night.

**After (dialed close because the beat now matters — naming would break the
contract):** Marta's hand found the doorframe before she knew she'd reached
for it.

**When to use:** match the technique to whichever distance the surrounding
narration is already using; if a beat deserves more than the current
distance can carry, shift distance first, then apply the dramatizing
technique. See `references/narrative-construction-and-pov.md` for the full
distance mechanics; this file only flags the interaction.

## Anti-Patterns

**Naming instead of dramatizing.** "He was angry" as the sole rendering of
a beat that is supposed to matter. Fix: replace the label with the
behavioral or somatic evidence a reader would use to diagnose anger in a
real person, and let the label go unwritten (per (a), (b)).

**Somatic-cliché overload.** Every emotional beat routed through the same
four zones — racing heart, dropped stomach, sweating palms, clenched fists —
until the pattern itself becomes as skippable as a label. Fix: vary the
channel (notice, voice, context, character-specific tic) and ration the
generic physiological cues rather than defaulting to them every time
(per (c)).

**Melodrama / purple prose.** Overcorrecting against naming by stacking
five somatic cues, a metaphor, and an exclamation point onto a single beat,
producing prose that reads as trying too hard rather than as felt. Fix:
pick the one or two most specific, most character-true details and cut the
rest; specificity does more work than volume.

**Emotional flatlining.** A scene or chapter that sustains one emotional
note (worry, sadness, tension) at a constant level across many pages instead
of building it, so each successive beat lands with the same weight as the
last and the reader stops feeling forward motion. Fix: apply the
escalation moves in (e) — raise the personal cost beat by beat, tether
external stakes to what one character stands to lose, and vary how the
tension surfaces so repetition doesn't read as stasis.

**Mismatched register and distance.** Naming an emotion in a tight, close-POV
passage where the reader expects immersion, or over-somaticizing a
distant/omniscient passage where a plain label would be the more natural
convention. Fix: match the technique to the distance the scene is already
working at (per (f)), or deliberately shift distance before shifting
technique.
