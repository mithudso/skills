# Institutional Betrayal & the Disbelief of Abuse Survivors

Replication-honest reference on how institutions and intimates respond to abuse disclosure, why
survivors are disbelieved, and what the evidence actually supports. **Educational only · not
clinical or legal advice.** Per-claim confidence: **FACT** (3+ independent sources converge),
**QUALIFIED** (2 sources or robust-but-bounded), **TENTATIVE** (single-source or actively
contested; both sides preserved).

**Concentration-risk note (read first).** Betrayal trauma, institutional betrayal,
institutional courage, and DARVO all originate in one lab (Jennifer Freyd and students · Smith,
Smidt, Harsey, Rosenthal, Adams-Clark). Independent replication exists for DARVO's observer
effects and institutional betrayal's military/healthcare correlates, but attribute construct
ownership transparently and triangulate through the independent literatures: Ullman (disclosure
reactions), Campbell (systems), Lisak / Ferguson & Malouff (false reports), Malloy/Lyon/Quas
(recantation), Fricker/Tuerkheimer (credibility).

## Core concepts

### 1. Betrayal trauma theory (Freyd 1994/1996)

Trauma perpetrated by someone the victim **depends on** produces distinct outcomes from
stranger/accident trauma. Motivational core: when awareness of betrayal would threaten a
necessary attachment (child ↔ caregiver), "betrayal blindness" · unawareness, not-knowing,
forgetting · can be adaptive. Freyd separates two trauma dimensions: life-threat (fear/
hyperarousal) vs social betrayal (dissociation/unawareness).[^1][^2] · **FACT** (as to the
theory's content and provenance: Freyd 1994, *Ethics & Behavior*; 1996 Harvard UP book).

- **Robust:** betrayal-trauma exposure correlates with dissociation, hallucinations,
  self-injury, and revictimization more consistently than non-betrayal trauma; multiple
  datasets report more forgetting for caregiver- than stranger-perpetrated abuse.[^2][^3]
  · **QUALIFIED** (correlational convergence real; mechanism attribution unsettled).
- **Contested · always present both sides:** McNally (2007) argues no convincing evidence of
  genuine early amnesia for abuse; a child can preserve attachment by not *disclosing* without
  a special forgetting mechanism; "not thinking about it for years" is more parsimonious.[^4]
  Devilly et al. (2007) twice failed to replicate the DePrince & Freyd directed-forgetting
  effect.[^5] Freyd's reply concedes a "surprisingly common endpoint": failure to think about
  events degrades memory; the dispute is whether a betrayal-specific motivated mechanism is
  needed.[^6] · **FACT** that the amnesia mechanism is contested. State outcome correlates
  separately from the mechanism claim.

### 2. Institutional betrayal (Smith & Freyd 2014)

Wrongdoing by an institution against those who **depend on it** · including *failure to
prevent* or *to respond supportively to* wrongdoing committed in its context, where protection
is reasonably expected. Dimensions: omission vs commission; isolated vs systemic.[^7][^8]
— **FACT**. Measured by the Institutional Betrayal Questionnaire (IBQ/IBQ.2): failure to
prevent, normalizing environments, difficult reporting, inadequate response, cover-up,
punishing reporters.[^9]

- Landmark finding ("Dangerous Safe Havens", Smith & Freyd 2013): 45–47% of assaulted college
  women also reported institutional betrayal; IB **exacerbated** post-assault anxiety,
  dissociation, sexual problems, and trauma symptoms beyond assault alone.[^8] · **FACT**
  (direction replicated across settings).
- Military: IB perceptions predicted PTSD, depression, and increased odds of post-MST suicide
  attempt (Monteith 2016; extensions 2019, 2022).[^10] Healthcare: 66% reported healthcare IB;
  predicted care disengagement and trust damage (Smith 2017; Tamaian & Klest 2017).[^11]
  · **QUALIFIED** (cross-sectional self-report).
- **Psychometric caveats:** IBQ.2's single-factor model failed CFA (two factors:
  promotion-of vs response-to victimization; Reffi et al. 2018); Rasch analysis found
  dimensionality and item-fit problems (Monteith 2021).[^12] Avoid causal phrasing · evidence
  is associational. · **QUALIFIED**.
- Canonical case study (multi-institution failure, gov-report grade): USA Gymnastics / MSU /
  FBI handling of Larry Nassar · reports from 1997 onward not acted on; 2014 Title IX inquiry
  cleared him; USAG delayed FBI notification; DOJ OIG (Report 21-093, 2021) found FBI
  Indianapolis "fundamental errors" while approximately 70 or more athletes were allegedly
  abused July 2015–Sept 2016 (OIG wording).[^13][^14] Every IBQ item type is instantiated. · **FACT**.

### 3. Institutional courage (Freyd)

The proposed antidote: seek truth and act morally despite cost · accountability, transparency,
responding well to disclosures, climate surveys, self-study, "cherishing whistleblowers,"
amends.[^15] Evidence status (verified-as-of: 2026-07-04): measurement began ~2018 (ICQ);
supportive findings are cross-sectional, largely from the founding lab (e.g., IC buffered IB's
negative associations among harassed employees, *PLOS ONE* 2023).[^16] · **TENTATIVE-to-
QUALIFIED**: promising counter-construct under active validation, not an established
intervention. Freyd's own measure page: "much more measurement development is needed."

### 4. DARVO · Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender (Freyd 1997)

Perpetrator response pattern when held accountable: deny, attack the accuser's credibility,
and swap the victim/offender roles ("the offender is on the offense and the person attempting
to hold the offender accountable is put on the defense").[^17]

- Prevalence: ~72% of confronted persons used all three elements in the first empirical study
  (Harsey, Zurbriggen & Freyd 2017); nearly half of assaulted college women with post-assault
  perpetrator contact reported DARVO tactics (2022).[^18] · **QUALIFIED** (convenience samples).
- **Observer effects (experimental, replicated):** DARVO exposure makes observers rate victims
  as less believable, more responsible, more abusive · and perpetrators as less so; brief
  DARVO education partially reverses the effect; effect sizes small-to-moderate
  (ηp² ≈ .02–.09).[^19] · **FACT** (direction), effect sizes modest.
- **Boundary · never omit:** Freyd herself states DARVO is *not proof of past behavior*; an
  innocent accused can produce a DARVO-like response.[^17] Forensic commentators warn against
  courtroom use as guilt evidence; in polarized disputes both parties may accuse each other of
  DARVO.[^20] It describes a response pattern with demonstrated anti-victim persuasive
  effects · it is not a guilt detector.

### 5. Disclosure & social reactions (Ullman)

Measured by the Social Reactions Questionnaire (SRQ 2000): positive (emotional support,
tangible aid) and negative (blame, disbelief, stigmatization, control, egocentric,
distraction) reaction types.[^21]

- ~97% of disclosing survivors receive at least one positive AND ~98% at least one negative
  reaction; the 2019 meta-analysis (1,871 correlations) confirms the **asymmetry**: negative
  reactions correlate strongly with worse psychopathology; positive reactions are weakly/
  inconsistently protective.[^22] In Ullman 1996 the only reactions related to *better*
  adjustment were **being believed** and **being listened to**.[^23] · **FACT**. The signature
  finding: negative hurts more than positive helps.
- Two negative factors: "turning against" (blame, disbelief, stigma) vs "unsupportive
  acknowledgment" (acknowledging but controlling/distracting/egocentric). Unexpectedly, the
  latter related *more* to PTSD/depression; 94% of survivors received it (Relyea & Ullman
  2015).[^24] · **QUALIFIED**.
- Longitudinal: negative reactions and PTSD are reciprocal over 3 years.[^25] Negative
  reactions silence survivors (Ahrens 2006: professional reactions teach disclosure is
  ineffective; friend/family reactions reinforce self-blame and doubt).[^26] ~75% of first
  disclosures go to informal supports; formal help-seekers get *more* negative reactions.[^27]
  · **QUALIFIED**.

### 6. Secondary victimization / the "second assault" (Campbell)

Across community samples, most survivors reporting to legal/medical systems did not receive
needed services, and system contact was associated with elevated distress; rape crisis centers
and advocates reduce it. Police and doctors corroborated survivors' accounts of what occurred
but significantly **underestimated the distress they caused** (validation study 2005).[^28]
— **FACT** (multi-study, multi-method program; designs mostly self-report/cross-sectional).

### 7. False-report rates · state precisely or not at all

Confirmed-false reports are **2–10% of police reports; best point estimate ~5%** (Lisak et al.
2010: 5.9% under IACP definitions; Ferguson & Malouff 2016 meta-analysis: 5.2%, 95% CI
3.0–8.9%; UK Home Office ~3%).[^29][^30] · **FACT**, with mandatory caveats in BOTH directions:

- Kanin's 41% figure is methodologically discredited (single department, polygraph threats) —
  never cite as prevalence.[^29]
- The 2–10% is a rate of *confirmed-false among police reports*: equivocal cases exist, the
  true rate is not precisely knowable, and the figure cannot be inverted into "92–98% of all
  accusations are true," nor extended to unreported allegations.[^30]
- Honest statement: confirmed false reports are a small minority of police reports; false
  reporting is neither negligible nor anywhere near common enough to justify default disbelief.

### 8. Credibility discounting, epistemic injustice, and rape myths

- **Epistemic injustice** (Fricker 2007): testimonial injustice = identity prejudice deflates
  a speaker's credibility below what evidence warrants; hermeneutical injustice = missing
  shared interpretive resources make experience hard to render intelligible.[^31]
  Tuerkheimer ("Incredible Women", 2017) documents **credibility discounting** at every stage
  of the criminal process · police default to doubt despite low false-report rates; discounts
  fall hardest on women of color, immigrants, LGBTQ people, poor women, sex workers.[^32]
  · **FACT** as a documented framework grounded in attrition data (analytic scholarship, not
  an effect-size literature).
- **Rape myths / "real victim" stereotypes:** real victims supposedly resist, are injured,
  report immediately, avoid the perpetrator, and display distress. Trial observation found
  defense use of myths in *every* observed trial; mock-juror studies: higher rape-myth
  acceptance → lower victim credibility, fewer guilty verdicts.[^33] **Counterintuitive victim
  behavior** (delayed disclosure, continued contact with the abuser, calm affect, incomplete
  recall, recantation) is normal and is the subject of admissible corrective expert testimony
  in many US jurisdictions.[^34] Most survivors delay or never report · up to 90% of
  institutional abuse never reaches authorities.[^7] · **FACT** for myth prevalence and
  deployment; **QUALIFIED** for expert-testimony effectiveness.

### 9. Memory and credibility (brief · depth lives elsewhere)

Normal memory (traumatic or not) is reconstructive: peripheral detail encodes poorly, repeated
interviews normally produce some inconsistency · so **inconsistency is a weak cue to
deception**, yet police and jurors treat it as disqualifying (the "video recorder" folk
model).[^35] · **FACT** for the credibility-judgment problem. Whether trauma memories are
*specially* fragmented is disputed: systematic comparisons find no special incoherence; a 2024
review criticizes both prosecution-side "indelible central detail" claims and some
trauma-informed interview protocols; philosophers likewise reject blanket skepticism of
traumatic testimony.[^36] · **TENTATIVE/contested**: strong claims in either direction outrun
the evidence. False/recovered memory debate: flagged only · Williams 1994 (38% non-report of
documented abuse) vs Loftus "lost in the mall" (25%; 2023 preregistered replication 35%); both
literatures contested; depth in `clinical-therapeutic-psychology` (false/recovered memory
spoke).[^37]

### 10. Family and intimate-context disbelief

- In substantiated dependency-court child sexual abuse cases, **58.8% of non-offending
  caregivers were unsupportive** (disbelief, pressure to recant, blaming, staying with the
  perpetrator); 23.1% of cases involved recantation, best explained by **filial dependency**
  (younger children, parent-figure perpetrator, unsupportive caregiver) · the
  recantations-are-false-allegations hypothesis was tested and not supported (Malloy, Lyon &
  Quas 2007).[^38] · **FACT** (landmark; generalize beyond dependency samples with care).
- Predictors replicate: perpetrator being the caregiver's romantic partner reduces belief and
  protection; disclosure to the caregiver first increases belief.[^39] · **QUALIFIED**.
- **Replication-honest nuance:** the pop claim "maternal support is the strongest predictor of
  child recovery" is weakly supported — meta-analysis found minimal association between global
  caregiver support and child functioning (most effects < .10), while *belief vs
  disbelief/blame* does matter for recantation and disclosure maintenance.[^40] Distinguish
  the two. · **QUALIFIED**, both sides preserved.

### 11. Religious institutions (proportionate)

John Jay reports (2004, 2011): >80% of now-known 1950–2002 clergy-abuse cases had occurred by
1985 but only 6% were reported to dioceses by then; responses centered on rehabilitating
priests, not victims; known abusers transferred to unknowing parishes; 2018 Pennsylvania grand
jury: 301 priests, 1,000+ victims, documented cover-up.[^41] Clergy abuse adds a distinct
"spiritual harm" dimension (loss of faith community, weaponized religious authority) —
construct inconsistently defined (2025 scoping review).[^42] · **FACT** for the response
pattern; **QUALIFIED** for spiritual-harm specifics.

## Adjacent constructs (fenced)

- **Moral injury** → `clinical-therapeutic-psychology`: centers the actor/witness's violated
  conscience; institutional betrayal centers the dependent member harmed.[^43]
- **Gaslighting** ≠ DARVO: gaslighting = *sustained* reality-manipulation within an ongoing
  relationship, sociologically mobilizing stereotypes against the victim (Sweet 2019); DARVO =
  *episodic* response at the moment of confrontation. Both suffer concept creep in popular
  usage.[^44]
- **Whistleblower retaliation** (institutional-betrayal cousin): ~82% of employee
  whistleblowers in one corporate-fraud sample were fired, quit under duress, or reassigned;
  ~1 in 5 disclosers report reprisal; fear of retaliation and expected inaction drive
  silence.[^45] Freyd's institutional-courage steps explicitly include "cherish the
  whistleblower."

## Anti-patterns · pop distortions this skill must correct, not repeat

1. "Only 2% of reports are false" → the supported range is 2–10% (~5%) *confirmed-false among
   police reports*; don't invert it into "98% true." Equally: don't cite Kanin's 41%.
2. "DARVO proves guilt / proves the accuser" → explicitly rejected by the construct's author.
3. "Betrayal trauma causes amnesia" stated as settled → mechanism contested; outcomes robust.
4. "Trauma memories are fragmented, so inconsistency proves trauma" AND "inconsistency proves
   lying" → both wrong; inconsistency is normal memory behavior and a weak deception cue.
5. "Maternal support is the key predictor of recovery" → belief/disbelief matters;
   global-support effects are meta-analytically weak.
6. "Institutional courage is a proven remedy" → early, cross-sectional, founder-lab evidence.
7. "Gaslighting" for any disagreement → concept creep; keep it distinct from DARVO.
8. Causal phrasing ("institutional betrayal causes PTSD") → the evidence is associational.
