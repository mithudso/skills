---

Methamphetamine disrupts at least six neuroendocrine axes simultaneously; their uncoupled recovery during withdrawal produces the paradox of coexisting insomnia and hypersomnia — not simply "sleeping more" but a fragmented 24-hour sleep architecture.

## HPA Axis and CRF (primary driver of insomnia)

Chronic meth activates the HPA axis via CRF release from paraventricular hypothalamic nucleus → ACTH → cortisol. During withdrawal, CRF hypersecretion shifts to the **amygdala/extended amygdala**, not the hypothalamus — this is wake-promoting because the CRF→locus coeruleus→norepinephrine circuit is a canonical arousal pathway. Simultaneously, serum ACTH rises while cortisol paradoxically blunts (adrenal dysregulation), decoupling the normal cortisol morning peak. Result: sustained central CRF-driven hyperarousal despite low peripheral cortisol — the neuroendocrine signature of insomnia in early withdrawal.

## Melatonin and Circadian Uncoupling

Meth binges produce irregular light exposure and suppress pineal melatonin via dopaminergic and adrenergic retinal/SCN interference. Withdrawal patients show **lower peak melatonin** and a **phase-delayed onset** relative to controls; melatonin levels correlate inversely with craving scores, making them a candidate biomarker. The cortisol-melatonin circadian rhythm (normally anti-phasic) remains uncoupled for weeks. **Treatment**: melatonin 0.5–1 mg taken 1–2 hours before the desired sleep time advances the phase without suppressing endogenous production.

## Prolactin (hypersomnia component)

Dopamine tonically suppresses prolactin. Meth-induced dopamine depletion during withdrawal → hyperprolactinemia. Prolactin itself is sleep-promoting (REM and NREM augmentation); hyperprolactinemia thus contributes to the daytime hypersomnia pole of withdrawal sleep disorder, opposing the CRF-driven nighttime arousal.

## Growth Hormone and SWS Rebound

GH is released in pulses during slow-wave sleep (SWS). Chronic meth suppresses SWS → GH deficiency during active use. Withdrawal SWS rebound (compensatory delta-sleep increase) produces excess GH secretion, contributing to hypersomnia and subjective fatigue. This rebound is transient but can persist 2–4 weeks.

## Thyroid Axis

Meth increases sympathetic tone → secondary TSH/thyroid stimulation during use. Withdrawal removes this sympathomimetic drive → transient hypothyroid-like state (low free T3 effect, even without frank TSH abnormality) → fatigue, slowed mentation, and hypersomnia.

## Melanocortin System

MC4R in the lateral hypothalamus co-localizes with orexin neurons. Meth modulates MC4R signaling; withdrawal changes disrupt the hunger–arousal coupling mediated by this circuit, contributing to the anorexia/fatigue profile and attenuating orexin's wake-promoting drive.

## Clinical Summary

| Axis | During Use | Withdrawal Effect | Sleep Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| HPA/CRF | Hyperstimulated | Amygdala CRF↑, cortisol blunted | Insomnia, hyperarousal |
| Melatonin | Suppressed, phase-delayed | Delayed onset persists | Sleep-onset difficulty |
| Prolactin | Suppressed (DA intact) | DA depletion → hyperprolactinemia | Daytime hypersomnia |
| GH | Reduced (SWS suppressed) | SWS rebound → GH excess | Hypersomnia |
| Thyroid | Stimulated | Transient hypothyroid state | Fatigue, hypersomnia |
| Melanocortin | MC4R dysregulated | Orexin coupling disrupted | Reduced wake drive |

**Therapeutic implications**: CRF1 receptor antagonists (in development for addiction) would directly target the insomnia arm. Melatonin (low-dose, timed) addresses circadian uncoupling. Bupropion partially restores dopaminergic tone, secondarily normalizing prolactin and improving subjective energy.
