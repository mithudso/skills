## 1. Overview — What Are Collateral Consequences?

**Collateral consequences** are civil penalties and disabilities that flow automatically from a criminal conviction — separate from the criminal sentence itself (incarceration, probation, fines). For drug convictions in NC, collateral consequences can affect:
- SNAP (food assistance) and TANF (cash assistance / Work First)
- Federal public housing and Section 8 vouchers
- Professional and occupational licenses
- Private employment (background checks)
- Federal student loans
- Voting rights

These consequences often have greater long-term impact on recovery and reintegration than the criminal sentence itself.[^1]

## 2. SNAP (Food Stamps) — NC Felony Drug Ban

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

NC applies a **tiered ban** on SNAP eligibility based on drug felony class:[^2]

| Conviction Class | SNAP Bar |
|---|---|
| Class H or I drug felony | 6-month bar after conviction or release from custody |
| Class G drug felony | Permanent lifetime ban from SNAP |
| Class F, E, D, C, B1, B2, A drug felony | Permanent lifetime ban from SNAP |

This is the federal 1996 welfare reform drug felony ban (21 U.S.C. § 862a), which states may opt out of or modify. NC has **partially opted in** — applying the ban with the tiered structure above rather than a complete lifetime ban or complete opt-out.[^2]

**NC HB 564 / reform:** The NC Justice Center and advocates have pushed to eliminate or reform the ban. House Bill 564 would make a person with a drug felony conviction eligible for SNAP immediately after conviction date or release if they complete a substance abuse treatment program. Status as of 2026: monitor NC General Assembly for current session status.[^2]

---

## 3. TANF (Work First) — NC Felony Drug Bar

NC applies a similar bar to **Work First (NC's TANF program)** for felony drug convictions, analogous to the SNAP bar structure.[^2]

**Reform proposals** mirror those for SNAP — advocates argue that denying food and cash assistance to people with drug convictions increases recidivism by undermining recovery and basic needs.

---

## 4. HB 564 — Pending Reform (as of 2026)

**NC House Bill 564** ("Public Safety Through Food Access") is reform legislation that would:[^2]
- Eliminate the tiered drug felony SNAP/TANF bars
- Make individuals eligible immediately upon conviction or release, provided they complete a substance abuse treatment program
- Apply to both SNAP and Work First (TANF)

**Current status:** As of 2026, monitor the NC General Assembly website for current session status. The bill has been introduced in multiple sessions.

## 5. Federal Public Housing and Section 8

Federal law (42 U.S.C. § 13661) authorizes (but does not mandate) public housing authorities (PHAs) to deny admission to individuals with certain drug-related criminal histories. NC public housing authorities set their own admissions and occupancy policies within federal guidelines.[^1]

**Mandatory denials (federal law):**
- Persons required to register as sex offenders under a state sex offender registration program must be denied admission
- Persons evicted from federally-assisted housing due to drug-related criminal activity within the preceding 3 years

**Discretionary denials (PHA policy):**
- Drug-related criminal activity (beyond the mandatory 3-year period)
- Violent criminal activity

**Key protections:**
- PHAs must look at the full context, not just the conviction
- Rehabilitation and completion of SUD treatment are relevant mitigating factors
- HUD guidance (2016 and updated) requires PHAs to consider relevant individualized circumstances
- Some NC PHAs have adopted "Second Chance" or "One Strike Reform" policies expanding access

## 6. Professional and Occupational Licensing — GS 93B-8.1

NC GS 93B-8.1 governs how occupational licensing boards may (and may not) use criminal history in licensing decisions.[^3]

### What NC Boards MAY Do
A board **may** deny a license on the basis of a conviction only if:
1. The criminal conviction is **directly related** to the duties and responsibilities of the licensed occupation, OR
2. The conviction is for a crime that is **violent or sexual** in nature

### What NC Boards MAY NOT Do
- **Automatically deny** licensure based on criminal history alone
- Deny based on an arrest without conviction
- Ignore rehabilitation evidence

### Mandatory Considerations Before Denial
Before denying based on a drug conviction, a board must specifically consider:
- The **nexus** between the criminal conduct and the prospective duties of the licensed occupation
- Prison, jail, probation, parole, rehabilitation, and employment records since the crime
- **Completion of, or active participation in, rehabilitative drug or alcohol treatment**
- A **Certificate of Relief** (GS 15A-173.2), which must be considered if granted[^3]

### 30-Day Response Right
If criminal history may prevent licensure, the board must notify the applicant in writing with sufficient time. The applicant then has **30 days** to:
- Correct any inaccuracy in the criminal history record, OR
- Submit evidence of mitigation or rehabilitation

### Practical implication for SUD recovery
Completion of SUD treatment is explicitly listed as a factor boards must consider. Individuals with drug convictions seeking professional licenses should document their treatment history and recovery.

## 7. Certificate of Relief (GS 15A-173.2)

A **Certificate of Relief** is a court-issued document stating that certain collateral consequences of a conviction no longer apply — without expunging the conviction itself.[^3]

**Purpose:** Provides evidence of rehabilitation for licensing boards, employers, and housing authorities. Boards must consider a Certificate of Relief under GS 93B-8.1.

**Eligibility:** Available for convictions of misdemeanors and Class H and I felonies. Not available for violent, sex offender, or other specified offenses.

**Effect:** Does not seal or expunge the record, but limits the use of listed convictions by boards and others. Does NOT automatically restore firearms rights or restore eligibility for federal benefits.

**Process:** Petition the sentencing court. NCDHHS and reentry organizations can assist with petitions.

## 8. Employment — Private Sector and Background Checks

No NC state law prohibits all private employers from considering drug convictions. However:[^1]

- NC does not have a statewide "ban the box" law for private employers (as of 2026); some municipalities have adopted local ban-the-box ordinances
- **GS Chapter 15A** expungement statutes allow employers to be told "no convictions" if a conviction has been expunged
- **EEOC guidance** (federal) warns that blanket exclusion of people with criminal records can constitute race discrimination if not job-related
- Certain industries (childcare, healthcare, education) have mandatory background check requirements that may bar drug felony convictions regardless of GS 93B-8.1

## 9. Federal Student Loans

The 1998 Higher Education Act drug conviction financial aid suspension has been substantially reformed:[^1]

- As of July 1, 2023, **drug convictions no longer result in automatic suspension of federal student aid eligibility** under the FAFSA Simplification Act
- Prior law suspended eligibility for 1 year (first offense), 2 years (second offense), or indefinitely (third offense) for drug convictions while receiving federal financial aid
- This change removes a major barrier to education-based reentry and SUD recovery

## 10. Voting Rights

NC restores voting rights **automatically upon completion of the sentence**, including incarceration and probation/parole:[^1]

- Persons on active probation or parole for a felony conviction are **not eligible to vote**
- Upon completion of all supervision conditions, voting rights are restored without any petition or application required
- For drug convictions specifically: completion of drug court program (with dismissal) typically means no ongoing conviction — voting rights unaffected

## 11. Expungement and Record Sealing

NC has several expungement pathways that can eliminate or limit drug conviction collateral consequences. See `nc-criminal-defense` references/nc-expungement-record-sealing.md for full detail.

**Key provisions relevant to SUD:**
- GS 15A-145.4 — expungement of nonviolent felony convictions after 10-year waiting period
- GS 15A-145.5 — expungement of certain nonviolent felonies (expanded by SB 562 / S.L. 2017-195)
- Drug treatment court completion → dismissal → eligible for expungement under GS 15A-146 (dismissal after deferred prosecution or discharge/dismissal)
- Expunged records cannot be used in licensing or benefits decisions

## 13. Anti-Patterns / Common Misunderstandings

**"NC completely bans people with drug felonies from SNAP"**
Not entirely — NC applies a tiered ban (6 months for Class H/I; lifetime for Class G and above). Some other states have fully opted out of the ban (no restriction). NC's current law is neither the most nor least restrictive.[^2]

**"Completing drug treatment automatically restores all collateral consequences"**
No. Completion of SUD treatment is a relevant mitigating factor in licensing decisions (GS 93B-8.1) and for Certificates of Relief, but it does not automatically restore SNAP eligibility, eliminate housing bars, or expunge convictions.[^3]

**"Federal student loan bars still apply after drug convictions"**
No longer true as of July 1, 2023. The FAFSA Simplification Act eliminated automatic suspension of federal student aid eligibility for drug convictions.

**"NC has ban-the-box for all employers"**
No statewide law requires private employers to remove the criminal history checkbox from applications. Some local jurisdictions have adopted ban-the-box ordinances. Federal contractors are subject to separate federal guidance.[^1]
