## The Core Dilemma

Confirmed ADHD or narcolepsy requiring stimulant therapy in a patient with stimulant SUD history creates a genuine two-sided risk. Untreated ADHD elevates relapse risk by impairing impulse control and executive function. Stimulant prescribing in early recovery may also elevate relapse risk. Neither decision is safe by default — the question is which risk is managed better with medication.

## Evidence Base: Stimulants for ADHD With SUD History

Multiple meta-analyses confirm that therapeutic stimulant doses for confirmed ADHD do **not** increase SUD risk when used as prescribed in adults. The 2024 Cortese et al. Lancet Psychiatry network meta-analysis supports a neutral-to-protective effect. A 2025 PMC safety meta-analysis across 93 RCTs confirms acceptable adverse-event profiles when standard monitoring is in place. The critical variables are: confirmed ADHD diagnosis (not symptom overlap from active SUD), therapeutic dose range, and structural monitoring. Misdiagnosis of ADHD in active stimulant intoxication or early withdrawal is a major confound — diagnosis should occur after ≥3 months of abstinence or in stable MAT.

## Meth SUD Is a Distinct Population

Methamphetamine SUD creates specific DA-system sensitization not present in alcohol or opioid SUD. The mechanistic distinction between stimulant agents matters here:

**Amphetamine salts (Adderall, Adderall XR):** Act as DAT *efflux* agents — force dopamine release outward through the transporter. This is the same mechanism as methamphetamine. In meth-sensitized DA systems, amphetamine salts carry higher misuse liability and cross-sensitization risk. **Avoid as first-line in meth SUD history.**

**Methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta):** Acts as a DAT *reuptake inhibitor* only — blocks dopamine removal without forcing release. Mechanistically distinct from meth. Lower abuse liability in meth-sensitized patients. Prefer OROS methylphenidate (Concerta) or MPH-ER over immediate-release formulations — slower Tmax reduces drug-liking and abuse potential. **Preferred first-line in meth SUD history.**

**Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse):** Prodrug metabolized to d-amphetamine in the gut; requires intact first-pass hydrolysis to become active. Intranasal/IV misuse routes are largely blocked. Lower misuse signal than standard amphetamines in trials. Still shares DAT-efflux mechanism with meth when metabolized. **Use with caution; second-line to MPH in meth history; better option than standard amphetamine salts if amphetamine class is required.**

## Narcolepsy-Specific Considerations

Narcolepsy with persistent EDS unresponsive to pitolisant, modafinil/armodafinil, and sodium oxybate may require stimulants for adequate daily function. The calculus shifts: untreated EDS causes social and occupational dysfunction that directly increases relapse risk. Methylphenidate has FDA approval for narcolepsy. Lisdexamfetamine does not (off-label for narcolepsy). Amphetamine salts are FDA-approved for narcolepsy but should be avoided in meth SUD history per mechanism rationale above.

## Structural Safeguards — Required, Not Optional

Stimulant prescribing in this population is only appropriate within a collaborative care framework:

- **Addiction medicine consultation** before initiation; shared care with SUD treatment team
- **MAT concurrency**: XR-naltrexone does not block stimulant effects but supports recovery; buprenorphine maintenance is acceptable; document rationale
- **PDMP check** at every prescription; review for controlled substance co-prescriptions
- **Urine drug monitoring** at each visit including stimulant metabolite tracking and illicit stimulant screen
- **Limited supply**: 30-day maximum; no early refills under any circumstance
- **Documented relapse action plan**: predefined steps if illicit stimulant use detected on UDS
- **Pill counts** if misuse suspected

## Red Flags — Stop and Reassess

Any of the following warrants immediate taper, non-stimulant switch, or dose hold pending addiction medicine review:

- Illicit stimulant (meth, cocaine) on UDS
- Dose escalation requests without objective clinical change
- Lost/stolen medication pattern (>1 episode)
- Early refill requests
- Behavioral changes consistent with stimulant intoxication or misuse
- Concurrent opioid or benzo prescriptions not coordinated with SUD team
