# Pediatric Narcolepsy

## Core Concepts
- Narcolepsy Type 1 (NT1) in children: chronic neurological disorder caused by selective loss of hypothalamic orexin (hypocretin)-producing neurons, yielding EDS, cataplexy, and REM-boundary intrusions
- Bimodal age of onset: peak at ~6–7 years and again ~14–15 years; ~40% of all NT1 cases begin before age 15
- Diagnosis requires PSG + MSLT (≥2 sleep-onset REM periods, mean sleep latency ≤8 min) or CSF orexin-A ≤110 pg/mL
- Narcolepsy Type 2 (NT2): EDS without cataplexy, normal or intermediate orexin; rarer in children, diagnostically harder
- Median delay from symptom onset to diagnosis: 3–5 years in pediatric populations, often misdiagnosed as ADHD, depression, or epilepsy

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Autoimmune destruction of ~70,000 orexin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus; HLA-DQB1*06:02 present in ~98% of NT1 cases (vs ~25% general population)
- Post-H1N1 influenza vaccine (Pandemrix) wave 2009–2010 increased pediatric NT1 incidence 5–14× in Scandinavia — strongest human evidence for molecular mimicry trigger
- Orexin deficiency disrupts LH/FSH pulsatility and leptin signaling, mechanistically linking NT1 to precocious puberty and rapid weight gain
- Loss of orexin removes wake-stabilizing input to monoaminergic systems → REM intrusion into wakefulness (cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations)
- Post-onset weight gain: mean +4 kg within 6 months; partly orexin-driven metabolic dysregulation, not purely behavioral

## Clinical Picture
- **Atypical cataplexy in children**: partial episodes dominate — facial hypotonia (ptosis, jaw drop, tongue protrusion), choreiform or dystonic limb movements; full-body falls are uncommon early; easily mistaken for tics, seizures, or behavioral episodes
- EDS in children presents as irritability, hyperactivity, inattention, and aggression rather than classic "sleepiness" — leads to ADHD misdiagnosis in ~30% before correct dx
- Precocious puberty observed in ~17–20% of pediatric NT1 patients (vs ~2% general population); central mechanism, not treatment-related
- Obesity: ~50–80% of children are overweight/obese at diagnosis; BMI z-score rises sharply in first 6 months post-onset regardless of stimulant use
- Psychiatric comorbidity: depression and anxiety in 30–50%; cognitive impact includes memory consolidation deficits affecting school performance

## Evidence & Treatment
- **Sodium oxybate (SXB)**: FDA-approved for pediatric NT1 ≥7 years (2018); reduces cataplexy frequency and EDS; JCSM 2024 data show significant BMI z-score reduction after 1 year of SXB (effect plateaus ~12 months); dosing weight-adjusted, split nocturnal doses
- **Modafinil/armodafinil**: first-line for EDS in children ≥6 years (off-label); 100–400 mg/day; does not address cataplexy; interaction with oral contraceptives important in adolescents
- **Pitolisant** (H3 inverse agonist): approved EU for children ≥6 years; reduces EDS and cataplexy; once-daily; lower abuse potential than stimulants — preferred when SXB compliance is difficult
- **Methylphenidate/amphetamines**: widely used for EDS but no cataplexy effect; Schedule II limits prescribing flexibility; growth monitoring required
- **Behavioral**: strict sleep scheduling, strategic naps (2×15 min/day reduces EDS severity), school accommodations (IEP/504), psychoeducation for family critical for adherence

## Key Facts
- HLA-DQB1*06:02 sensitivity for NT1: ~98%; specificity: ~75% (not diagnostic alone)
- CSF orexin-A ≤110 pg/mL is diagnostic of NT1 regardless of MSLT; rarely performed in children due to LP burden
- Pandemrix-associated risk: OR ~14 in children aged 4–19 in Finland/Sweden
- SXB BMI effect: mean z-score decrease of −0.3 to −0.5 at 12 months in treatment-naive pediatric patients
- Mean diagnostic delay in children: 3–5 years; earlier diagnosis associated with better weight and school outcomes
