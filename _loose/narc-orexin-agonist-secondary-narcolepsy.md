## Scope

Orexin type-2 receptor (OX2R) agonists — oveporexton (TAK-861), danavorexton (TAK-925), firazlor — were developed for narcolepsy type 1 (NT1), but the pharmacological rationale extends directly to any secondary/acquired narcolepsy in which orexin deficiency is the confirmed endpoint.

## Why mechanism transfers to secondary forms

NT1 destroys orexin-producing neurons autoimmunally; secondary narcolepsy destroys or damages them through external insult (methamphetamine neurotoxicity, TBI, paraneoplastic, hypothalamic tumor). In both cases the downstream result is the same: insufficient orexin peptide reaching OX2R. An OX2R agonist bypasses the peptide source entirely — it acts directly on the receptor. If the receptor is intact and downstream circuits are functional, the pharmacodynamic response is expected to be equivalent regardless of why orexin levels fell.

Preclinical rationale for meth-specifically: meth causes incomplete orexin neuron loss (not total ablation). Surviving neurons are dysfunctional but the target receptors in the dorsal raphe, locus coeruleus, and tuberomammillary nucleus remain expressed. Replacement agonism restores signaling without requiring neuron recovery.

## Clinical evidence base

**NT1 pivotal data (approved indication):** Danavorexton Phase 2b — 11.1-point MWT improvement vs. placebo. Oveporexton Phase 3 (FirstLight, RadiantLight, 2025): met all primary and secondary endpoints at week 12 (p < 0.001 across all doses). FDA NDA accepted; PDUFA Q3 2026, NT1 only. EMA review ongoing.

**Secondary narcolepsy — no completed RCTs.** OX2R agonists have not been studied in meth-induced, TBI-induced, or paraneoplastic narcolepsy in any registered trial as of mid-2026.

**Secondary narcolepsy — mechanistic anchor (TBI):** Baumann et al. 2007 documented CSF hypocretin-1 deficiency (< 110 pg/mL) in a subset of post-TBI patients with EDS indistinguishable from NT1, establishing that TBI-induced narcolepsy shares the same biomarker endpoint. This is the strongest indirect evidence that OX2R agonism would be active in secondary forms.

**NT2 signal:** Danavorexton Phase 2 included NT2 subjects and showed wakefulness improvements, suggesting OX2R agonists work even when orexin deficiency is partial — further supporting extension to secondary forms with partial neuron loss.

## Meth-induced narcolepsy: patient selection criteria

Before off-label OX2R agonist use is clinically defensible, confirm:
1. CSF hypocretin-1 < 110 pg/mL (AASM diagnostic threshold for orexin deficiency)
2. MSLT-confirmed narcolepsy phenotype (mean SOREMPs ≥ 2, mean sleep latency ≤ 8 min)
3. Stable SUD recovery — active stimulant use is a contraindication (sympathomimetic interactions; also confounds efficacy assessment)
4. No structural hypothalamic lesion requiring primary management

OX2R agonists carry no intrinsic abuse potential — they are not monoamine-active and do not produce reinforcing effects, making them preferable to amphetamine-class wake-promoters in this population.

## Regulatory and label status

All approved/NDA-stage indications are NT1 only. Secondary narcolepsy use is **off-label**. No regulatory pathway currently exists for secondary narcolepsy as a separate indication; it would require a sponsor-initiated IND or investigator-initiated trial with CSF-confirmed cohorts.

## Comparison: OX2R agonist vs. intranasal orexin peptide

Intranasal orexin-A delivery has shown efficacy in preclinical models of orexin deficiency (including TBI models) but has not completed Phase 2 trials in humans as of 2026. OX2R agonists are orally bioavailable (oveporexton QD), have validated human PK/PD, and are closer to clinical availability. Intranasal orexin remains a research-stage alternative with theoretical CNS delivery advantages for patients with receptor downregulation.

## Evidence gaps

- No Phase 2/3 RCT in meth-induced, TBI, or paraneoplastic narcolepsy
- No published case series of OX2R agonist use in CSF-confirmed secondary narcolepsy
- Long-term receptor expression after meth neurotoxicity uncharacterized in humans
- Optimal dosing in partial (vs. complete) orexin deficiency unknown
