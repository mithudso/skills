# Medicaid Work Requirements

> **Educational only — NOT legal or political advice.** This is a policy area with significant legal and political volatility; current status should be verified through CMS and state sources.

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

---

## 1. What Are Work Requirements?

**Medicaid work requirements** (officially "community engagement requirements") are conditions attached to Medicaid eligibility that require certain enrollees to work, participate in job training, perform community service, or engage in similar activities to maintain their coverage.

They are implemented through **Section 1115 demonstration waivers** — the federal mechanism that allows CMS to approve experimental Medicaid program variations. Work requirements are not authorized by the Medicaid statute itself.

---

## 2. Policy History

### Trump Administration (2018–2020)
- January 2018: CMS under administrator Seema Verma announced it would approve work requirement waivers for the first time — a significant policy reversal from prior administration positions
- Multiple states received initial approvals: Arkansas, Indiana, Kentucky, New Hampshire, Arizona, Michigan, Utah, Ohio, Wisconsin, South Carolina

### Arkansas Works — First Implementation (2018)
Arkansas was the first state to implement a work requirement (June 2018). Results in the first months:
- ~18,000 Arkansans lost Medicaid coverage (largest group: failed to report compliance online)
- Studies showed most enrolled Arkansans subject to the requirement were already working, caregiving, or had exemption-qualifying conditions — but the online reporting system was difficult to navigate
- Federal court (D.D.C.) blocked Arkansas's implementation in March 2019 — Gresham v. Azar (later Gresham v. Becerra)

### Gresham v. Becerra / Stewart v. Azar Litigation
Federal courts consistently held that CMS could not approve work requirements because:
- The primary purpose of Medicaid is to "furnish medical assistance" (42 U.S.C. § 1396)
- Work requirements would cause significant coverage loss
- CMS did not adequately consider whether work requirements furthered Medicaid's core purpose
- D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed in Gresham v. Becerra (2021)

### Biden Administration (2021–2025)
- Biden administration rescinded all approved work requirement waivers (early 2021)
- CMS issued guidance stating it would not approve new work requirement waivers
- **Georgia Pathways** was the exception — Georgia challenged the rescission; federal courts allowed Georgia to implement its waiver pending litigation

### Trump Administration Return (2025–2026)
- 2025: CMS under new administration again signaled openness to work requirement waivers
- Multiple states resubmitted applications; as of mid-2026, several are pending CMS review
- **NC**: Has not submitted a work requirement waiver as of 2026-06-22

## 3. Georgia Pathways — The Only Active Program (as of 2026)

**Georgia Pathways to Coverage** is the only active work requirement Medicaid program in the US as of mid-2026:
- Launched July 1, 2023 (limited rollout)
- Covers adults 19–64 at 0–100% FPL who complete 80 hours/month of qualifying activities (work, job training, community service, education)
- Georgia did NOT fully expand Medicaid; instead implemented this partial expansion conditioned on work requirements
- **Enrollment**: By mid-2024, only ~3,000–4,000 Georgians enrolled — far below projections
- Ongoing litigation challenging legality under federal Medicaid statute

## 4. Policy Research Findings

**On who is affected**:
Studies of Arkansas and projected analyses consistently found that a large majority of non-elderly adults subject to work requirements:
- Are already working (42–56%)
- Are caregiving (family members with disabilities, small children)
- Are in school or training
- Have health conditions that should qualify for exemptions but face documentation barriers
- Face administrative/bureaucratic barriers to reporting compliance

**Coverage loss impact**: Estimates suggest work requirements cause significant coverage loss — predominantly from reporting/administrative failures rather than actual non-compliance. Arkansas: 18,000 lost coverage in 4 months. Projections for national expansion: hundreds of thousands to low millions.

**On employment**: No rigorous evidence that Medicaid work requirements increase employment rates. Most of those subject were already working or exempt-eligible.

---

## 5. Exemptions (Standard Structure)

Approved work requirement waivers generally exempt:
- Individuals medically determined unable to work (disability certification)
- Pregnant women
- Primary caretakers of young children
- Students enrolled full-time
- Individuals age 65+ (or 60+ in some waivers)
- Recipients of SSI or unemployment benefits
- IHSS/personal care workers (in some states)
- American Indian/Alaska Native members

**Problem**: Exemptions require documentation and affirmative reporting. Many individuals who qualify for exemptions fail to successfully document them, leading to coverage loss.

---

## 6. Legal Status (2026)

The core legal question remains contested:
- **Federal circuit split in lower courts** on whether CMS has authority to approve waivers that lead to coverage loss
- **No Supreme Court ruling** directly on work requirements
- Trump-era approvals + Biden rescissions + Trump re-approvals = political/legal ping-pong
- Georgia Pathways is surviving litigation partly on procedural grounds

**Likely near-term developments**: More state applications under second Trump administration; continued litigation; possible Congressional action to clarify.

---

## 7. NC Position

As of 2026-06-22, **North Carolina has not applied for or implemented** a Medicaid work requirement waiver. NC's Medicaid expansion (December 2023) was a clean expansion under Section 1396a(a)(10)(A)(i)(VIII) with no work requirement conditions.

NC General Assembly Republican leadership has discussed work requirement interest historically, but the bipartisan SB 355 that enacted NC expansion did not include work requirements.

---

## 8. Anti-Patterns

**"Work requirements save Medicaid money"** — Mixed evidence. Coverage loss reduces state costs short-term, but increased emergency care use from uninsured individuals, worse health outcomes, and administrative overhead from compliance verification are not fully offset. CBO and state fiscal analyses show variable results.

**"Work requirements are about people who could work but choose not to"** — Empirical evidence consistently shows most non-elderly adults on Medicaid are working, caregiving, in school, or have health limitations — not able-bodied non-workers.

**"Work requirements are the same as work requirements for SNAP/TANF"** — Different programs, different purposes, different legal authority. Medicaid's primary statutory purpose is medical assistance; courts have found work requirements conflict with that purpose.
