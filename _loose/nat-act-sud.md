# ACT for Substance Use Disorders and Meth Recovery

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) is a third-wave behavioral therapy that targets **psychological flexibility** rather than thought content. For SUD — and especially meth SUD — this distinction matters: ACT does not ask patients to challenge or reframe cravings, it changes their *relationship* to those cravings.

## The Hexaflex: Six Core Processes

ACT targets six interlocking inflexibility processes; the therapeutic moves are their opposites:

| Inflexibility | ACT Process |
|---|---|
| Cognitive fusion (thoughts = reality) | **Defusion** — step back; thoughts are events, not commands |
| Experiential avoidance | **Acceptance** — open to discomfort without struggling |
| Autopilot / rumination | **Present-moment contact** |
| Self-as-content ("I am an addict") | **Self-as-context** — stable observer perspective |
| Values confusion | **Values clarification** — recovery as values-driven, not just rule-following |
| Inaction / impulsive action | **Committed action** — behavioral steps toward values despite discomfort |

Psychological flexibility is the core outcome; the six processes serve it collectively.

## Defusion for Cravings — The Key Mechanism in SUD

Standard SUD counseling often tells patients to *resist* cravings. ACT reframes the struggle itself as the problem. Ironic process theory (Wegner) predicts that suppression rebounds: the more a person tries not to think about using, the louder the urge becomes. ACT defusion breaks that cycle.

Techniques:
- **Urge surfing** (Marlatt): observe the craving as a passing wave — notice its shape, peak, and subsidence without acting on it. ACT frames this as defusion from a transient mental event.
- **Labeling**: "I notice I'm having the thought that I need meth" — the added distance weakens the thought's directive power.
- **Silly-voice / singing exercises**: reframing the craving thought through an absurd delivery strips its urgency without debate.

For meth specifically: cravings can emerge suddenly from sensory cues, loneliness, fatigue, or shame — internal states that also trigger use. Defusion handles all these triggers with one skill rather than requiring separate CBT modules per trigger type.

## Values Clarification in Meth Recovery

Meth hijacks the dopaminergic reward system, crowding out intrinsic motivation and eroding connection to what the person actually cares about. Values work is the ACT answer.

Key distinction: **values vs. goals**. Goals can be completed and fail; values are directions — "being present for my kids" is a value that survives a lapse. This reframing reduces shame spirals after slips.

Values clarification reconnects patients to family, health, creativity, contribution — domains that competing with meth's artificial salience. Recovery becomes a *choice toward something*, not just abstinence from something. This matters for sustained motivation beyond the acute withdrawal window.

## Shame and Self-Stigma

Meth SUD carries heavy stigma — from providers, families, and the patient themselves. Shame drives hiding, which drives relapse. The typical response (challenge the shame thought; "think more positively") requires meta-cognitive ability that meth-related neurotoxicity may impair.

ACT's approach: **defuse from shame thoughts, not eliminate them**. The patient does not have to believe they are a good person to make values-congruent behavioral choices. This decoupling — "I can have the thought that I'm worthless AND still show up for my kids today" — is clinically usable even when insight is limited.

## ACT vs. CBT for Meth SUD

| Dimension | CBT | ACT |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Content of thoughts (challenge distortions) | Function/relationship to thoughts (defuse regardless of content) |
| Craving strategy | Identify and dispute | Accept and defuse |
| Cognitive load | High — requires metacognitive identification of distortions | Lower — behavioral skills usable with impaired cognition |
| Mechanism | Thought modification | Psychological flexibility |
| Long-term data | Strong, well-replicated | Comparable to CBT; ACT advantage at 18-month follow-up in one study |

Meth-related cognitive impairment — including executive function deficits, attention problems, and working memory impairment — creates a practical obstacle to CBT's cognitive restructuring tasks. ACT's defusion and values exercises are concrete, behavioral, and less dependent on abstract metacognition, making them potentially better tolerated during early meth recovery when cognitive recovery is incomplete.

## Evidence Base

**Meta-analyses:**
- Lee et al. (2015) — 10 RCTs, N=1,386; small-to-medium effect size favoring ACT vs. active comparisons at end of treatment and follow-up ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26298552/))
- Osaji et al. (2020) — 22-study review; abstinence rates 20–43.8% depending on population; incarcerated women: 43.8% ACT vs. 26.7% CBT at 6-month follow-up ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7524566/))
- 2024 ScienceDirect systematic review/meta-analysis: ACT effective across addictive behaviors; effect sizes comparable across SUD types

**Stimulant/meth-specific:**
- Preliminary RCT (Tehran, n=30 meth-using men): significant reduction in addiction severity with ACT
- Active RCT at CAMH: "ACT for Methamphetamine Use Disorder in Women and Gender Non-Conforming Individuals" (ACT Matrix + contingency management; NCT05334914)
- ACT long-term advantage: one comparison found ACT superior to CBT for maintaining abstinence at 18-month follow-up

**Caveats:** Most SUD studies have small samples, lack blinding, and rely on self-reported abstinence. Long-term abstinence effects are less consistent than short-term. ACT evidence is "encouraging but limited" for meth specifically — the larger stimulant SUD evidence base supports the mechanism, but meth-specific RCTs are still emerging.

## Integration with Other Modalities

- **12-step**: ACT acceptance aligns well with Step 1 surrender; values work parallels the "searching and fearless moral inventory"
- **Contingency management**: complementary — CM provides external reinforcement while ACT builds internal values-based motivation
- **EMDR / trauma work**: ACT psychological flexibility is a precondition for tolerating trauma processing; often sequenced first
- **IFS**: compatible — self-as-context parallels the "Self" perspective in IFS
- **MAT**: fully compatible; ACT does not require abstinence-only framing
- **Group delivery**: ACT translates well to SUD treatment groups; brief ACT interventions (2–4 sessions) show utility in intensive outpatient and residential settings

## Clinical Delivery Notes

- The hexaflex is a map, not a sequence — processes are woven together, not delivered in order
- Metaphors are central: quicksand (struggling amplifies sinking) vs. solid ground (acceptance enables movement); passengers on a bus (thoughts arise and don't have to drive)
- Values cards/Bull's Eye worksheet useful for meth patients who feel disconnected from what matters
- Defusion exercises should be practiced during sessions with low-intensity cravings before high-stakes situations
- Shame-focused defusion is often the entry point for meth patients before values work is accessible
