# Opioid Receptor Pharmacology

## Core Concepts
- Three classical receptor subtypes: mu (MOR/MOP), kappa (KOR/KOP), delta (DOR/DOP) — all GPCRs (Gi/Go-coupled); NOP/nociceptin is a 4th related receptor, not naloxone-sensitive
- Endogenous ligands: beta-endorphin/endomorphin → MOR; dynorphins A & B → KOR; enkephalins → DOR
- Receptor localization: highest density in striatum, thalamus, hypothalamus, PAG, spinal cord dorsal horn, and peripheral sensory neurons
- MOR subtypes: mu-1 mediates supraspinal analgesia and dependence; mu-2 mediates euphoria, respiratory depression, miosis, and constipation
- KOR is the only opioid receptor subtype that does not cause respiratory depression; activates dysphoria, diuresis, sedation

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Gi/Go coupling → inhibits adenylyl cyclase (↓cAMP) → opens inward-rectifying K⁺ channels (GIRK) → closes voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → net hyperpolarization and reduced neurotransmitter release
- Desensitization (acute tolerance): agonist binding → GRK2/3 phosphorylates receptor → β-arrestin recruitment → uncouples receptor from G-protein within minutes
- Internalization (receptor trafficking): β-arrestin + AP-2/clathrin complex → endocytosis; receptor either recycled (resensitization) or degraded (downregulation)
- Drug-specific internalization profiles: methadone, etorphine, fentanyl → strong internalization; morphine, oxymorphone → desensitization without internalization; oxycodone → neither; mechanism does not reliably predict clinical tolerance magnitude
- β-arrestin pathway (biased signaling): responsible for respiratory depression and constipation; G-protein pathway mediates analgesia — basis for "biased agonist" drug development (oliceridine/TRV130)
- Chronic exposure → transcriptional upregulation of adenylyl cyclase isoforms (cAMP superactivation on withdrawal) and CREB activation in locus coeruleus → withdrawal syndrome

## Clinical Picture
- OUD affects ~16 million people globally; ~2.7 million with OUD in the US (2023 SAMHSA); overdose deaths ~80,000/year in US with >75% involving opioids
- Tolerance develops to analgesia, euphoria, and respiratory depression; less tolerance to constipation and miosis (clinically useful signs of use)
- Physical dependence manifests as withdrawal within 8–24 h of last short-acting opioid dose (COWS scale: lacrimation, piloerection, tachycardia, GI cramping, myalgia, anxiety)
- Overdose triad: miosis, respiratory depression, unconsciousness — reversed by naloxone (competitive MOR antagonist; 0.4–2 mg IV/IM/intranasal; repeat q2–3 min)
- Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) occurs in ~60% of infants with in-utero opioid exposure

## Evidence & Treatment
- **Methadone** (full MOR agonist; long t½ 24–36 h): suppresses withdrawal and craving; reduces illicit use by ~50%, mortality by ~30%; requires OTP clinic dispensing (42 CFR Part 8); QTc prolongation risk
- **Buprenorphine** (partial MOR agonist + KOR antagonist; ceiling on respiratory depression; very high MOR affinity — displaces full agonists): sublingual or buccal; XR injectable (Sublocade) monthly; buprenorphine/naloxone (Suboxone) deters IV misuse; X-waiver requirement eliminated (2023 SUPPORT Act)
- **Naltrexone** (pure competitive MOR antagonist): XR IM monthly (Vivitrol 380 mg) achieves ~95% receptor occupancy; no dependence risk; requires 7–10 days opioid-free before initiation; non-inferior to buprenorphine in RCTs when adherence ensured (X:BOT trial, NEJM 2018)
- All three FDA-approved MAT agents reduce opioid mortality; combination with counseling improves retention; MAT is underutilized — only ~20% of individuals with OUD receive pharmacotherapy (SAMHSA 2022)
- Naloxone access expanded via OTC approval (Narcan nasal spray, March 2023); harm-reduction cornerstone

## Key Facts
- MOR occupancy of ≥80% required for buprenorphine to block euphoria from superimposed full agonists
- Buprenorphine dissociation half-life from MOR: ~166 min (slow off-rate underpins long duration and ceiling effect)
- Respiratory depression risk: MOR >> DOR > KOR (none for KOR)
- Naloxone t½ ~60–90 min — shorter than most opioids, requiring repeat dosing or infusion for long-acting overdose (methadone, fentanyl patch)
- cAMP superactivation on abrupt cessation accounts for the autonomic storm of opioid withdrawal; clonidine (α2 agonist) attenuates this via parallel Gi pathway
