# Drug Decriminalization Policy & Health Outcomes

## Core Concepts
- **Decriminalization** removes criminal penalties for personal drug possession/use; supply and trafficking remain criminal offenses
- **Dissuasion Commissions** (Portugal model): administrative panels replacing courts — can mandate treatment, impose fines, or community service; coercive but non-carceral
- Oregon Measure 110 (Nov 2020): first US full decriminalization of all drug classes for personal possession; officially reversed September 2024
- **Confounding variables** in natural experiments: concurrent fentanyl market shift, treatment infrastructure, housing/social services, enforcement culture
- Decriminalization ≠ legalization; supply-side law remains unchanged in all major decriminalization models

## Mechanisms / Policy Architecture
- Portugal (2001 Law 30/2000): personal possession thresholds set at ≤10-day personal supply per substance; violators referred to Dissuasion Commissions rather than prosecutors
- Portugal paired decriminalization with €400M+ investment in integrated harm reduction: needle exchange, methadone, housing, social reintegration
- Oregon Measure 110: redirected cannabis tax revenue (~$300M) toward addiction services but built no coercive referral mechanism; issued health referral cards only
- Oregon and British Columbia both lacked dissuasion/sanction infrastructure; both reversed or paused decriminalization within 3–4 years
- Incarceration itself elevates overdose risk: first 2 weeks post-release, overdose death risk is 129× the general population rate — decriminalization reduces this exposure

## Clinical Picture (Epidemiological Outcomes)
- Portugal overdose death rate: 80 deaths/million (2001) → 6 deaths/million (2021), a 93% decline over 20 years
- Portugal HIV among people who inject drugs: 1,016 new cases (2001) → 56 new cases (2012); <16 annually by 2020
- Portugal treatment uptake: ~60% increase by 2012 post-decriminalization
- Oregon overdose deaths: spiked ~50% between 2021–2023 during Measure 110; state declared 90-day fentanyl emergency early 2024
- JAMA Psychiatry early study (2021–2022 data): found no statistically significant association between Oregon/Washington decriminalization and fatal overdose rates — illustrating how confounding (fentanyl wave timing) complicates attribution

## Evidence & Methodological Limits
- **Strongest evidence for Portugal**: sustained 20-year trend across multiple indicators (mortality, HIV, incarceration, treatment) — convergent validity
- **Counterfactual problem**: Portugal's success coincided with EU accession economic growth and simultaneous expansion of social services; impossible to isolate decriminalization alone
- Oregon natural experiment confounded by fentanyl's rapid market penetration 2020–2023, COVID-19 disruptions, and lagged treatment infrastructure rollout
- Systematic reviews (PMC 2025 scoping review) find mental health and overdose outcomes mixed across jurisdictions — effect size and direction vary with treatment investment, coercion model, and local drug market
- Incarceration reduction is the most consistent finding across decriminalization settings; health outcome improvements contingent on concurrent service investment

## Key Facts
- Portugal: drug-related deaths fell from ~131/year (2001) to 20/year (2008), then stabilized around 30–80 range
- Oregon Measure 110 reduced low-level drug arrests and saved ~$40M; funds designated for addiction services were slow to deploy (most grantees not operational until 2022–2023)
- Pre-decriminalization Portugal: highest drug-related AIDS rate in EU; by 2015, among lowest
- British Columbia decriminalization (Jan 2023–Jan 2025): paused after public disorder concerns; overdose deaths continued rising during fentanyl crisis — mirroring Oregon failure pattern
- The 129× post-incarceration overdose risk estimate comes from Washington State prison data (2014–2019), representing one of the clearest health arguments for reducing carceral exposure
