# Undiagnosed Sleep Disorders in SUD Treatment: Screening & Clinical Integration

## Core Concepts

- Sleep disorders in SUD are bidirectional: substances disrupt sleep architecture AND pre-existing sleep disorders drive self-medication, masking the primary diagnosis
- Three distinct disorder classes require different screening workflows: obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), narcolepsy/NT2, and idiopathic hypersomnia (IH)
- Up to 80–90% of OSA in the general population is undiagnosed; SUD populations carry additional OSA risk factors (obesity, opioid-induced central apneas, alcohol-related pharyngeal relaxation)
- Stimulant and opioid use confound EDS presentation: withdrawal hypersomnia mimics IH; stimulant use suppresses symptoms of narcolepsy and may be self-medicating treatment
- MSLT validity in SUD requires a drug-free washout (typically 2 weeks minimum, longer for long-acting substances), making SUD treatment settings a natural window for diagnostic testing

## Mechanisms / Neurobiology

- Opioids suppress REM sleep and reduce hypoglossal motor tone, directly causing or worsening OSA via central and obstructive mechanisms
- Alcohol increases upper airway collapsibility during sleep and fragments slow-wave sleep; acute withdrawal causes REM rebound that persists 1–2 years
- Stimulant cessation unmasks orexin-deficit EDS (narcolepsy) that had been pharmacologically compensated — the "unmasking hypothesis"
- Opioids suppress CSF orexin levels acutely; chronic opioid use may desensitize orexin-2 receptors, independently worsening wakefulness maintenance
- IH involves GABA-A receptor hypersensitization (somnogen hypothesis, UNC data); substance use alters GABAergic tone and can both precipitate and mask IH phenotype

## Clinical Picture

- **OSA in SUD**: STOP-BANG positivity (score ≥3) runs 40–60% in inpatient detox/residential SUD populations; opioid use disorder patients on MOUD carry additional central apnea risk
- **Narcolepsy/IH misdiagnosis**: Estimated mean delay to narcolepsy diagnosis is 8–22 years in the general population; SUD complicates this further — stimulant use masked as "recreational" may be years of unrecognized self-medication
- **Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)**: Score ≥10 indicates EDS warranting further workup; ESS administered at SUD program entry provides a low-cost IH/narcolepsy triage, but acute withdrawal scores are confounded — repeat at 2–4 weeks post-washout
- **MSLT in SUD**: Mean sleep latency ≤8 min + ≥2 SOREMPs = narcolepsy criteria; false positives increase if washout is incomplete; AASM recommends 2-week washout minimum, with documentation of actigraphy and PSG prior night

## Evidence & Treatment

- Sleep disturbances affect 70–85% of individuals entering SUD treatment; insomnia is the single strongest modifiable predictor of relapse
- Treating comorbid OSA (CPAP) in SUD populations improves sleep continuity, reduces fatigue-driven craving, and may reduce relapse rates — RCT data limited but observational evidence is consistent
- CBT-I during SUD treatment shows short- and long-term efficacy for sleep and improves program retention (multiple RCTs); first-line before pharmacotherapy
- Daridorexant (orexin receptor antagonist, low misuse potential) shows preliminary abstinence benefit in comorbid insomnia-SUD (65.8% abstinence rate in a small 2025 cohort)
- Narcolepsy treatment in recovered SUD patients: sodium oxybate (GHB-derivative) requires risk-benefit assessment; pitolisant (H3 antagonist, non-scheduled) and solriamfetol are preferred when stimulant avoidance is indicated

## Key Facts

- 40–60%: STOP-BANG ≥3 screen-positive rate in SUD residential treatment populations
- 70–85%: Proportion of SUD patients entering detox with clinically significant sleep disturbances
- ≥2 weeks: Minimum drug-free washout before valid MSLT in SUD patients (actigraphy confirmation required)
- 8–22 years: Average narcolepsy diagnosis delay in general population; SUD extends this further
- ESS ≥10 + 2+ weeks abstinence = trigger for PSG/MSLT referral in SUD treatment settings
