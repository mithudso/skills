# Meth-Induced Psychosis

## Core Concepts
- Methamphetamine-induced psychosis (MAP) is a substance-induced psychotic disorder per DSM-5; onset during or shortly after intoxication or withdrawal
- Presents identically to paranoid schizophrenia in the acute phase — the strongest substance-psychosis association of any drug class
- Key symptom triad: persecutory delusions, auditory/visual hallucinations, and formication (tactile hallucination of insects crawling under skin — "crank bugs")
- Classified as acute (<1 month), prolonged (1–6 months), or persistent (>6 months) based on symptom duration after cessation
- Spontaneous recurrence without re-exposure ("flashback psychosis"), triggered by stress or sleep deprivation, is well-documented

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Meth causes massive, sustained dopamine release via reverse transport through DAT (dopamine transporter) and blocks reuptake — dopamine surge far exceeds natural reward signals
- VMAT2 (vesicular monoamine transporter 2) inhibition forces dopamine out of vesicles into cytoplasm, amplifying and prolonging the effect beyond other stimulants
- Hyperdopaminergia in the mesolimbic pathway (especially nucleus accumbens and striatum) directly drives positive psychotic symptoms
- Serotonin dysregulation contributes to hallucinations; norepinephrine excess drives agitation and paranoia
- Chronic use causes dopaminergic neurotoxicity — DAT downregulation and striatal dopamine depletion — which may predispose to persistent psychosis even after abstinence
- Oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation are elevated in MAP; antioxidant defense markers inversely predict psychosis onset

## Clinical Picture
- Prevalence of psychotic symptoms among meth-dependent users: 26–46% across studies; meta-analytic estimate ~36.5% for full psychotic disorder
- Regular meth users are ~11× more likely to experience psychosis than non-users
- Healthy volunteers with no psychosis history became psychotic at oral doses of 55–75 mg in controlled challenge studies, confirming direct causal mechanism
- Distinguishing MAP from primary schizophrenia: MAP has more prominent visual hallucinations, less prominent negative symptoms (flat affect, alogia), and clearer onset tied to use; formal thought disorder is less severe
- Risk factors for MAP: dose ≥0.2 g per use, duration of use >3 months, comorbid heroin or tobacco use, sleep deprivation, trauma history, family history of psychosis

## Evidence & Treatment
- Acute MAP: antipsychotics (haloperidol, olanzapine, risperidone) plus benzodiazepines for agitation — most acute episodes resolve within days to weeks of abstinence
- ~15–30% of MAP cases persist beyond 1 month; ~5–15% develop a chronic psychotic disorder indistinguishable from schizophrenia
- No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for methamphetamine use disorder; bupropion + naltrexone combination (ADAPT-2 trial, NEJM 2021) showed modest reduction in meth use (13.6% vs 2.5% abstinence at 12 weeks)
- Contingency management (CM) is the highest-evidence behavioral intervention for meth use and indirectly reduces MAP relapse risk
- Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) targeting psychosis-specific beliefs reduces recurrence risk in individuals with history of MAP

## Key Facts
- 36.5%: meta-analytic prevalence of psychotic disorder in meth-dependent individuals
- 11×: relative risk of psychosis in regular meth users vs non-users
- 55–75 mg: oral dose inducing psychosis in drug-naive healthy volunteers in challenge studies
- ~80% of acute MAP resolves within 1 month of abstinence; ~20% persist longer
- Stress-triggered relapse of psychosis (without drug re-exposure) occurs in a documented subset — mechanism involves sensitization of dopaminergic pathways
