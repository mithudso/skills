Prazosin (alpha-1 adrenergic antagonist, non-selective) is the most evidence-supported pharmacotherapy for PTSD-related nightmares. Clinically useful in meth recovery patients with comorbid PTSD where two distinct noradrenergic mechanisms collide.

## Mechanism

PTSD drives locus coeruleus (LC) norepinephrine overflow → alpha-1 receptor activation in prefrontal cortex during REM → trauma nightmare generation + hyperarousal-mediated sleep fragmentation. Prazosin blocks alpha-1 receptors, attenuating this pathway → reduced nightmare frequency/intensity, less WASO, improved total sleep time. It does not suppress REM globally — it specifically dampens NE-driven dream disturbance.

## Evidence Base

**Supportive RCTs (Raskind et al., VA Seattle PTSD Research Center):** Multiple smaller RCTs in combat veterans demonstrated prazosin reduced trauma nightmares from ~4–5/week to <1/week vs. placebo, with significant reductions in distressed awakenings and improved sleep time. Replicated in civilian (non-combat) PTSD. Simon et al. 2019 meta-analysis (6 RCTs) confirmed statistically significant nightmare reduction.

**Null trial (Raskind et al., NEJM 2018):** VA Cooperative Study, n=304 veterans, 26 weeks, max 20mg (men) / 12mg (women) — no significant benefit over placebo for nightmares or overall PTSD severity. Critical caveat: the subgroup with higher baseline nightmare frequency did show benefit. The null result likely reflects heterogeneous patient selection rather than a true null mechanism effect.

**Current guideline status:** APA 2022 PTSD guidelines list prazosin as "insufficient evidence to recommend" based on the 2018 trial. Still widely prescribed; clinical consensus is that it works in the right patient. PTSD pharmacotherapy algorithms (2024–25 update) continue to recommend addressing sleep disturbances with prazosin as a first step before SSRIs/SNRIs.

## Meth Recovery Comorbidity

Competing NE dynamics require clinical discrimination:

| Condition | NE State | Sleep Pattern |
|-----------|----------|---------------|
| Meth withdrawal (early) | LC depletion → hypoarousal | Hypersomnia, flat sleep architecture |
| PTSD (comorbid) | Sensitized amygdala → NE hyperarousal | Nightmares, hypervigilance, fragmented sleep |

**Prazosin most useful when:** (a) nightmares are trauma-themed (vs. generic drug-use dreams), (b) hyperstartle response present on exam, (c) daytime hypervigilance present, (d) sleep fragmentation > hypersomnia. An additional mechanism: drug dreams in SUD patients share the same noradrenergic circuit — prazosin may reduce drug-cue nightmares independent of PTSD diagnosis, with case-report evidence in adolescent SUD patients.

**Less useful if:** primary complaint is meth withdrawal hypersomnia or flat/unrestorative sleep without nightmares.

## Dosing

Start 1mg QHS (lying down — first-dose syncope risk is real). Titrate 1mg every 3–7 days by tolerability. Typical effective dose: 3–15mg QHS. VA cooperative study used up to 20mg (men) / 12mg (women).

**Safety in meth recovery:** orthostatic hypotension is the primary concern; meth causes cardiac/vascular compromise. Take lying BP before and after initiation. If stimulants co-prescribed (methylphenidate, amphetamine for comorbid ADHD), net BP effect is partly offset — monitor closely but the combination is clinically used. Prazosin is not scheduled, carries no abuse potential, no physical dependence.

## Comparators in PTSD Sleep

| Agent | Mechanism | SUD Caution |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| Prazosin | Alpha-1 blocker | Low risk; BP monitoring |
| Suvorexant | Orexin antagonist | Moderate; can combine with prazosin |
| Quetiapine | D2/H1 sedation | Avoid in SUD — abuse potential, metabolic |
| Benzodiazepines | GABA-A | Contraindicated in SUD |
| SSRIs | Serotonin reuptake | Daytime PTSD; modest sleep benefit |

Doxazosin (longer-acting alpha-1 blocker, once daily) is an alternative with a similar mechanism and emerging nightmare evidence but fewer RCTs than prazosin.

## Patient Selection Signal

Use prazosin when: trauma-themed nightmares, hyperstartle, hypervigilance, elevated resting heart rate, and inadequate response to sleep hygiene. The 2018 NEJM null result argues against blanket prescribing — target patients with prominent nightmare burden and hyperarousal signature.
