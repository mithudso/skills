# Meth Neurotoxicity Mechanisms

## Core Concepts
- METH neurotoxicity = selective degeneration of DA and 5-HT axonal terminals driven by converging insults: oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, hyperthermia, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ER stress
- Damage is dose-dependent and regionally selective: striatum (caudate/putamen) for DA; striatum + cortex + hippocampus for 5-HT
- Terminal degeneration is structurally distinct from neuronal apoptosis — cell bodies often survive while axon endings are destroyed ("chemical axotomy")
- Deficits are partially reversible with prolonged abstinence (12–24 months) but full recovery is rarely documented
- Animal models (rodents, primates) closely replicate human neuroimaging findings, establishing causal mechanistic chains

## Mechanisms / Biology
- **ROS/RNS cascade**: METH forces vesicular DA into cytosol via VMAT2 reversal; cytosolic DA auto-oxidizes, generating OH•, H₂O₂, O₂•⁻, NO, and ONOO⁻; these species directly damage proteins, lipids, and DNA in DA terminals
- **Hyperthermia amplification**: METH raises core body temp 2–4 °C; heat potentiates every downstream toxic step — ROS generation, glutamate release, and microglial activation; blocking hyperthermia (cold environment) substantially attenuates terminal damage in primates
- **Excitotoxicity**: METH triggers striatal glutamate surge via corticostriatal projections; NMDA receptor activation → Ca²⁺ influx → nNOS activation → NO production → ONOO⁻ formation; activated microglia further secrete excitotoxic glutamate
- **Mitochondrial failure**: ROS inhibit complexes I and III of the electron transport chain; ATP depletion depolarizes mitochondrial membrane potential, creating a positive-feedback loop amplifying ROS and triggering cytochrome c release / apoptotic signaling
- **Neuroinflammation**: Microglial activation (↑TNF-α, ↑IL-1β) and reactive astrogliosis persist weeks after acute exposure; activated microglia produce superoxide via NADPH oxidase, sustaining oxidative damage long after METH clearance
- **Serotonergic targeting**: 5-HT terminals lack the buffering capacity of DA terminals; METH-induced 5-HT efflux + MAO-mediated oxidative metabolism + shared ROS exposure produces widespread 5-HT axon degeneration in cortex, hippocampus, and thalamus

## Clinical Picture
- Human PET imaging (DAT, VMAT2 ligands) shows 20–30% striatal DA terminal reduction in chronic METH users vs. controls; 5-HT reductions visible in frontal and parietal cortex
- Cognitive sequelae: deficits in working memory, attention, inhibitory control, and executive function correlate with degree of DAT loss
- Psychiatric: heightened risk of psychosis (5× elevated vs. general population), depression, and anxiety — partially mediated by hypo-dopaminergic and hypo-serotonergic states
- Neuroimaging also shows white-matter abnormalities (DTI), reduced hippocampal volume (↑cortisol from HPA dysregulation), and prefrontal gray-matter loss
- Abstinence-related partial recovery of DAT binding observed at 12–24 months; cognitive improvement lags structural recovery

## Evidence & Treatment
- No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy specifically for METH-induced neurotoxicity; antioxidant strategies (NAC, ascorbate) show protection in animal models but limited human RCT data
- **NAC (N-acetylcysteine)**: replenishes glutathione, attenuates ROS; reduces METH self-administration and cue-induced craving in small trials (600–2400 mg/day studied)
- **Hyperthermia prevention** is the best-validated neuroprotective strategy in primates — practical implication: cooling interventions during acute METH intoxication may limit long-term damage
- Methamphetamine Use Disorder Contingency Management (CM) programs show the strongest behavioral evidence for abstinence (effect size d ≈ 0.58 across meta-analyses); abstinence itself drives partial neurological recovery
- Mirtazapine (30 mg/day) reduced METH use in a 2021 RCT (n=120) and may mitigate some serotonergic disruption via 5-HT receptor modulation

## Key Facts
- Striatal DA terminal depletion of ≥50% in rodents reliably produced by 4 × 10 mg/kg doses (2 h apart) — the standard binge protocol
- DAT PET binding in abstinent users recovers ~20% at 14 months but may not return to control levels even after years of abstinence
- Body temp ≥40 °C during acute METH exposure is a strong predictor of severe long-term DA terminal damage in primate models
- Serotonergic deficits (measured by 5-HTT binding) persist at 3+ years of abstinence in some PET studies, suggesting they are more permanent than DA deficits
- METH users show 5–10× higher rate of Parkinsonism later in life, consistent with DA terminal reserve depletion lowering threshold for clinical symptom onset
