# Narcolepsy Diagnosis Delay

## Core Concepts
- Mean diagnostic delay: 10.3 years (mean onset age ~18, mean diagnosis age ~28); median ~7 years; range 1–60 years
- Delay driven by symptom-condition overlap, clinician knowledge gaps, and absence of a single pathognomonic early sign
- Narcolepsy type 1 (NT1, with cataplexy/orexin deficiency) and type 2 (NT2) share EDS but NT2 lacks cataplexy, increasing ambiguity
- Onset skews adolescent: ~50% of patients develop symptoms in their teens, a period when sleepiness is normalized
- Delayed diagnosis independently predicts worse QoL, higher healthcare utilization, and employment impairment

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Primary driver: progressive or fluctuating loss of orexin (hypocretin) neurons in the lateral hypothalamus — full symptom constellation may not manifest for months to years post-onset, creating a moving diagnostic target
- Cataplexy — the most specific NT1 marker — is often subtle (partial: jaw drop, knee buckling) or absent early; patients may not recognize or report it
- REM-sleep boundary dysregulation underlies hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations and sleep paralysis, both frequently attributed to anxiety or dissociative disorders
- EDS attributed to lifestyle, poor sleep hygiene, or depression by both patients and clinicians; HLA-DQB1*06:02 positivity (~98% in NT1) not routinely screened
- Autoimmune destruction of orexin neurons follows a lag period (often post-infection/vaccination trigger), so early-stage patients may lack low CSF orexin (<110 pg/mL), the diagnostic threshold

## Clinical Picture
- Core tetrad: EDS, cataplexy (NT1), sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations — only ~10–15% present all four at onset
- Most common misdiagnoses: depression, ADHD, epilepsy (cataplexy misread as absence/atonic seizures), psychosomatic disorder, insufficient sleep syndrome, sleep apnea, burnout
- Prevalence: ~1 in 2,000 in the US and Europe; estimated 200,000 affected Americans, majority undiagnosed
- Psychiatric comorbidity is genuine (depression ~30%, anxiety ~20%) and amplifies misattribution — treating the comorbidity without resolving EDS masks the underlying diagnosis
- Korean multicenter real-world data (2025) identified lower sleep efficiency, reduced N2 sleep, and nocturnal hypoxia as correlates of longer delay

## Evidence & Treatment
- First-line EDS: modafinil 100–400 mg/day (non-amphetamine wake-promoter); solriamfetol 75–150 mg/day (dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, FDA 2019); pitolisant 17.8–35.6 mg/day (H3 inverse agonist, FDA 2019)
- Gold standard for cataplexy + EDS: sodium oxybate (GHB) 6–9 g/night in divided doses; low-sodium oxybate (Lumryz) approved 2023 for once-nightly dosing
- Orexin receptor agonists (e.g., TAK-994, NLS-2) in clinical development — may address root-cause orexin deficiency rather than symptoms
- Diagnosis confirmed via MSLT (mean sleep latency ≤8 min + ≥2 SOREMPs) ± CSF orexin-1 ≤110 pg/mL; requires PSG the preceding night to exclude sleep apnea
- Patient advocacy and narcolepsy registries (Narcolepsy Network, Wake Up Narcolepsy) have reduced delay in recent cohorts through awareness campaigns

## Key Facts
- 10.3 years: mean diagnostic delay across global cohorts; median 7 years
- 50%: proportion of patients with symptom onset in adolescence
- <110 pg/mL CSF orexin-1: diagnostic threshold for NT1 (sensitivity ~87%, specificity ~99%)
- ≥2 SOREMPs on MSLT: required alongside ≤8 min mean latency for PSG-based diagnosis
- ~98%: prevalence of HLA-DQB1*06:02 in NT1 — high sensitivity but low specificity (25–30% of the general population carries it)
