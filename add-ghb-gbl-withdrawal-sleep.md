# GHB/GBL Withdrawal Sleep Effects

## Mechanism During Use

GHB is a GABA-B agonist and endogenous GHB-receptor agonist that produces potent sedation and sleep consolidation. During regular use it:
- Dramatically increases slow-wave sleep (SWS/N3) — the basis of its FDA approval as sodium oxybate (Xyrem) for narcolepsy-cataplexy
- Suppresses REM during active dosing
- Has a very short half-life (~4–6 h), requiring redosing every 2–4 h in heavy users; some dose every 30–90 min around the clock

GBL is a prodrug rapidly converted to GHB by serum lactonases; pharmacologically identical, often more potent per volume.

## Withdrawal Syndrome: Why It's Dangerous

Abrupt cessation after physical dependence produces a GABA-B rebound syndrome analogous to benzodiazepine/alcohol withdrawal but with unique features:

- **Onset**: 1–6 h after last dose (faster than benzo withdrawal due to short half-life)
- **Peak severity**: 24–72 h
- **Duration**: 5–15 days; occasional protracted syndromes >1 month
- **Life-threatening features**: delirium, psychosis, visual hallucinations, seizures, autonomic instability (tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis)

Mechanism: chronic GHB downregulates endogenous GABA production and upregulates GABA-B receptors; cessation leaves receptor supersensitivity with insufficient ligand → CNS hyperexcitability identical in profile to severe alcohol withdrawal.

## Withdrawal Sleep Effects (Insomnia-Dominant)

In sharp contrast to stimulant withdrawal (which produces rebound hypersomnia), GHB withdrawal causes:

| Feature | GHB withdrawal | Meth/stimulant withdrawal |
|---------|---------------|--------------------------|
| Sleep quantity | Severely reduced; 24–72 h total insomnia common | Dramatically increased (hypersomnia) |
| SWS | Absent | Preserved or increased |
| REM | Rebound — vivid nightmares, REM intrusion | Suppressed initially then rebound |
| Medical danger | Life-threatening | Generally medically benign |
| Timeline | Days 1–5 most severe | Days 3–10 hypersomnia then recovery |

The insomnia is not simply subjective — patients in severe GHB withdrawal are physiologically unable to sleep due to CNS hyperactivation. Prolonged sleeplessness worsens delirium risk and creates a dangerous positive-feedback loop.

**REM rebound**: When sleep does occur, it is REM-dense with intense, frightening dreams. This produces false-positive MSLT findings (shortened REM latency, SOREMP) that can mimic narcolepsy — **GHB use and withdrawal must be excluded before interpreting any MSLT**.

## Management

First-line: **benzodiazepines** (GABA-A agonism provides partial bridge); however, benzodiazepines frequently show treatment resistance in moderate-severe withdrawal — patients may require escalating doses that exceed safe limits.

Second-line / adjunct: **baclofen** (GABA-B agonist) — directly targets the mechanism; case series and one RCT feasibility trial support its use; allows lower benzo doses and smoother taper. A 2025 case report in *Drug and Alcohol Review* documented successful management with slow benzodiazepine + baclofen wean over 6 months after three hospital admissions.

Severe/refractory: **barbiturates**, **dantrolene** (case reports), ICU monitoring; phenobarbital loading protocols used in some centers.

**Pharmaceutical GHB taper** (supervised dose reduction) has shown superior outcomes to benzo-alone in European centers but is not available in most US settings.

## Chemsex Co-Use: Mixed Withdrawal Picture

GHB + methamphetamine co-use is common in MSM chemsex contexts. Concurrent withdrawal creates competing signals:
- GHB withdrawal → insomnia, CNS hyperexcitation, delirium risk
- Meth withdrawal → rebound hypersomnia, fatigue, mood crash

The clinical picture is unpredictable. GHB withdrawal dominates medically and must be managed first (life-threatening > medically benign). Patients may experience a paradox where the urge to sleep from meth crash coexists with physiological inability to sleep from GHB withdrawal. Post-chemsex tolerance breaks (e.g., patient sleeps for 12 h and skips GHB doses) can precipitate GHB withdrawal unexpectedly.

## Key Differential Diagnosis Points

- **vs. alcohol/benzo withdrawal**: clinically identical; treat similarly; GHB onset faster
- **vs. stimulant withdrawal**: opposite sleep picture; GHB = insomnia-dominant, dangerous; stimulant = hypersomnia-dominant, benign
- **vs. narcolepsy**: REM rebound on PSG/MSLT can mimic narcolepsy; always obtain substance use history including GHB before MSLT; sodium oxybate (= GHB) is the treatment for narcolepsy — a patient on it should not be evaluated for narcolepsy acutely
