# Fundraising & Donor Psychology

A spoke of the **applied-psychology** hub. Evidence-grounded, replication-honest reference for the
PRACTICE psychology of fundraising — how relationships, the ask, recurring giving, stewardship, and
win-back strategy actually affect donor behaviour. The sibling skill `psychology-of-charitable-giving`
owns the motivational science (why people give at all); this skill owns what happens after the first
gift: retention, the mechanics of the ask, the stewardship relationship, and reactivation.

## 1. Donor Retention & the Leaky-Bucket Problem

### The retention crisis

The nonprofit sector loses donors at staggering rates. Sargeant's foundational work (2004, 2006, 2013) consistently documents that approximately **50-70% of first-time donors never make a second gift**. At the same time, even marginal improvements in retention compound dramatically: a 10-percentage-point improvement in retention can double the lifetime value of an acquired donor cohort because of the multiplicative, year-over-year effect of retention rates on LTV.

The "leaky bucket" framing — popularized by Sargeant and Shang — captures the futility of acquisition-focused fundraising when retention is neglected: filling the bucket faster never solves the problem if the hole remains.

**Fundraising Effectiveness Project (FEP)**: The AFP/Urban Institute FEP annual data consistently shows overall donor retention hovering in the 43-47% range across US nonprofits. First-year new donor retention is substantially lower, often 20-30%. Repeat donors retain at 60-65%. Donors who give 7+ times retain at 87.9% (2023 FEP data). This gradient underscores that the relationship compounds: each additional gift makes the next far more likely.

### The trust → commitment → behaviour chain

Sargeant and Lee (2004, Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly) used structural equation modelling to establish that **trust does not directly drive giving behaviour**; rather, trust drives commitment (to the mission and the organisation), and commitment drives giving. This mediated path has critical implications: organizations that try to improve retention by simply increasing contact frequency without building trust first may fail to move the commitment dial.

Key antecedents of commitment (Sargeant 2006):
1. Perceived service quality of the fundraising relationship
2. Shared beliefs / values alignment
3. Perceived risk (of the gift being wasted)
4. A personal link to the organisation or cause
5. Trust (itself driven by communication quality, frequency, relevance, timeliness)

### Identity and wellbeing: the next-generation model

The 2022 Sargeant-Shang-Edworthy longitudinal study ("Loyalty Meets Philanthropic Psychology", Institute for Sustainable Philanthropy) found that **giving intentions do not predict actual behaviour** — a classic intention-behaviour gap. What does predict actual giving one year later:
- **Supporter wellbeing**: how giving leaves people feeling
- **Donor identity**: strength of identification with the cause/organisation

This aligns with Philanthropic Psychology (PhilPsych®) research (Shang and colleagues): giving that is consistent with a donor's self-concept is more durable. Donors who think of themselves as "someone who gives to X" are retained not by persuasion but by identity affirmation.

Self-congruence research (Zogaj 2020, Schmalenbach Business Review) confirms: **ideal self-congruence** (giving matches who you want to be) and **functional congruence** (giving matches what you want to accomplish) predict donor loyalty; actual self-congruence does not. Issue involvement moderates these effects.

### What drives donors to lapse

Burk (Cygnus Applied Research, annual Burk Donor Survey, tens of thousands of donors) identified the top reasons donors stop giving:
- 69% over-solicited
- 64% communications focused on asking rather than reporting
- 63% lack of measurable results reported
- 62% no longer inspired by the mission
- 58% belief that overhead costs are too high

The last three are addressable through stewardship; the first two are a cadence/framing problem.

## 2. Psychology of the Ask

### Anchoring and ask-string design

The sequence and magnitude of suggested amounts on a donation page or letter significantly shapes what donors give, through **anchoring** (the first number in the ask string disproportionately influences the gift) and **range effects** (donors are drawn to amounts within the scale).

Key findings:
- **Shifting an ask string up by 20-50%** tends to increase average gift with modest effect on response rate in public radio field experiments (Reiley et al., referenced in Edwards & List 2014)
- **Prior-gift reminder** as the implicit first anchor is effective for renewing donors: reminding donors what they gave last time raises the reference point without the ambiguity of a new scale
- **Round vs. precision anchoring**: familiar "just-noticeable differences" matter; amount proximity to a scale point pulls donations toward it (Desmet & Feinberg 2003, attraction effect model: anchoring explains 12% of donation variance, proximity 7%)

### Legitimizing paltry contributions (LPC): "Even a penny will help"

Cialdini and Schroeder (1976, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) ran door-to-door charity experiments showing that adding "even a penny will help" to a standard request:
- **Increased compliance from 28% to 50%** (a ~78% lift in donor rate)
- Left average gift **unchanged** in the original study

Subsequent meta-analysis (Andrews et al. 2008; updated meta, Sénémeaud et al. 2012, Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly) and a 3-explanation meta-analysis (Garnett et al. 2015, Communication Research):
- LPC reliably increases compliance rates
- LPC also **reduces average donation size** among those who comply (r* = −.23; 95% CI −.34 to −.12) — approximately 69% of LPC donors gave below the control-group median
- The effect is partly anchoring (penny as lower bound), partly legitimacy (making a small gift feel acceptable)
- **Net revenue effect**: in canvassing contexts where response rate dominates, LPC can raise total revenue 64% despite the lower average gift (Donor Voice Science of Ask Strings — vendor white paper; N and methodology not independently verified)

Practical implication: LPC is most useful when **response rate is the binding constraint** (acquisition, lapsed reactivation) and least useful when a higher average gift per respondent matters more than breadth (major donor upgrade, warm base).

### Social information effects (Shang & Croson)

Shang and Croson (2009, Economic Journal) ran field experiments in a US public radio pledge drive:
- Telling callers that another donor gave **$300 (90th-95th percentile)** increased average contributions by **$13 (12%)** vs. control
- Effect on **new donors**: up to **29% ($25.33)** lift
- **No significant effect on renewing donors** — they already have an internal reference point
- The $300-condition also tripled renewal likelihood one year later among new donors
- **Downward social information backfires**: telling donors another person gave $75 (below their likely anchor) directionally reduced contributions (Croson & Shang 2008, Experimental Economics)

The implication: social proof for the ask should cite aspirational (high-percentile), not typical, donors. The mechanism is informational (what is "appropriate" to give?) for new donors, not conformity in the behavioral economics sense.

**Lapsed-donor caveat** (Meer 2015, VOLUNTAS field experiment): High social information raises *amount* given by lapsed donors (+$14.95, 45%) but *reduces* the probability they give at all (−4.1%). The fundraiser must weigh this participation/amount tradeoff.

### Default and upsell effects on donation pages

Digital fundraising research (Blackbaud, NextAfter, M+R Benchmarks) shows:
- Pre-selecting a giving **frequency** (monthly vs. one-time) significantly shifts donor type acquired — frequency default interacts with audience; current active donors default to monthly performs better; lapsed/prospect audiences may benefit from annual defaults (M+R UCS study 2024)
- **Upsell prompts** to upgrade to monthly giving post-transaction capture donors who would not have self-selected a recurring option

## 3. Recurring / Monthly Giving Psychology

### The LTV and retention gap

The data on recurring vs. one-time donors is among the most robust in applied fundraising:

| Metric | Recurring donors | One-time donors |
|---|---|---|
| Annual retention rate (2023) | 83.6% (global), 79.3% (N. America) | 45.2% |
| Average donor lifetime (2022) | 8.08 years | 1.68 years |
| Average annual gift (2022) | $941 | $2,155 |
| 10-year LTV | ~$7,604 | ~$3,621 |
| Global LTV multiplier | 5.4x | baseline |

Sources: Dataro 2024 Recurring Giving Benchmark (N > 100,000 donors across 2,000+ orgs); Neon One Recurring Giving Report 2022; Blackbaud Sustainer Study 2021.

### Why recurring donors retain at higher rates

Two psychological mechanisms (Jarvis / Chronicle of Philanthropy 2024):
1. **Deep cause commitment**: donors who self-select into monthly giving tend to have stronger identity alignment with the cause — they are intrinsically motivated
2. **The "forgotten subscription" effect**: passive persistence — the gift continues because the donor never actively cancels; cognitive inertia keeps them in

The Blackbaud 3-year threshold finding (97-98% retention for sustainers 3+ years) suggests that if you can survive the early-attrition period (months 1-12, where most cancellations occur), the relationship becomes extremely durable.

**Minguez & Sese (2022, Schmalenbach Business Review)** found that higher donation frequency among regular donors raises total annual donation amount — donors who give monthly cumulatively donate more than equivalent donors giving quarterly or annually, controlling for motivation. The "temporal reframing" effect (breaking $120/year into $10/month) can also lower the psychological cost of committing.

**Corporate CSR analogy**: Across seven pre-registered studies (N = 147,996), periodic donation framing increases perceived commitment from external audiences — consumers rate monthly donating companies as more committed than aggregate-amount companies (but only above a minimum magnitude threshold; $10/month does not signal commitment) (Schamp et al. 2023, SSRN preprint #32 — not yet peer-reviewed; treat as preliminary).

### Conversion and upgrade psychology

Converting one-time donors to monthly requires overcoming:
- **Status quo bias**: the donor's current pattern is the path of least resistance
- **Perceived loss of control**: recurring feels like an obligation; framing as "pause anytime" or "cancel anytime" reduces this
- The upgrade ask performs best immediately post-transaction (warm-state reciprocity) or at a high-engagement touchpoint (event confirmation)

## 4. Gratitude, Stewardship, and Donor-Centered Fundraising

### Penelope Burk's three requirements

Burk (Cygnus Applied Research, Donor-Centered Fundraising, 1st ed. 2003, 2nd ed. 2020; annual Burk Donor Survey with tens of thousands of respondents) identified three things donors say they need before they give again generously:

1. **Prompt, personal, genuinely grateful acknowledgement** — not a form letter, and without a nested ask
2. **Gift restricted to a project narrower in scope than the mission** — not the "excellence fund" or general operating; specificity creates accountability and emotional ownership
3. **A measurable results report before the next ask** — demonstrating impact in concrete terms

Outcome data from the Burk model vs. standard fundraising:
- Standard: 35% of new donors renew next time asked; 21.4% make a larger gift
- Donor-centered: 67% would definitely/probably renew; 52% would definitely/probably make a larger gift
- <10% of standard-approach donors give to the same org indefinitely vs. much higher long-term retention under donor-centered approach

Note: Burk's data are large-scale proprietary survey research (not RCT-designed). The effect sizes are self-reported intention, not observed behaviour. Treat as directionally strong but not randomized-experiment-grade.

### Thank-you timing and board calls

- **Speed matters**: donors who receive a thank-you letter within 24-48 hours are significantly more likely to give again (Burk donor survey data)
- **Board member thank-you call** (within a few days of gift): 95% of respondents said they would be "very appreciative"; 93% said they would definitely give again; 84% would definitely or probably make a larger gift
- Burk's controlled test of board thank-you calls to first-time donors: **subsequent contributions 39% higher** than control; long-term retention superior after even a single call
- 34% of Burk Donor Survey respondents said the thank-you call wholly or partly caused their decision to give again; 21% said it caused them to give more generously

### Reciprocity and non-donor-centric failure modes

When gratitude communications pivot to organizational accomplishments rather than donor impact, or include a nested ask, donors perceive the communication as transactional — activating reactance rather than reciprocity. Burk's data shows that the most common complaint is "they only contact me when they want money." The antidote is communications in which **the donor is the protagonist** — their gift accomplished X — rather than the organization.

Broader reciprocity research (Cialdini, Influence): receiving something unexpected triggers a felt obligation to give back. A prompt, personal thank-you that is not transactional creates the same dynamic — the donor feels they received something (recognition, personal connection) and is primed to reciprocate with a future gift.

## 5. Overhead Myth and Impact Trust

### The Gneezy et al. Science field experiment

Gneezy, Keenan & Gneezy (2014, Science, N = 40,000) compared three uses of seed money in a nonprofit mailing campaign:
- Seed funds as a matching gift: moderate positive effect on donations
- Seed funds already raised (general): positive effect
- Seed funds used to cover **overhead expenses** (overhead-free giving): **80-94% higher donation rate** and **75-89% higher total donations** vs. seed/matching controls

The mechanism: when overhead is already covered by a dedicated philanthropist, donors can tell themselves their gift goes 100% to the cause, removing the warm-glow-diminishing effect of overhead spending. This is not an education intervention (telling donors overhead is good) — it is a structural solution that removes the aversion trigger entirely.

### The evaluability bias explanation

Why does overhead aversion persist despite evidence that overhead ratios are poor proxies for impact? Baron & Szymanska (2011), Coupet & Berrett (2019), and Journal of Behavioral Public Administration (2021) converge on the **evaluability bias**:
- Overhead ratio is easy to calculate, publicly reported (IRS Form 990), and clearly interpretable (lower = better in most mental models)
- Impact / cost-effectiveness is hard to measure, rarely published, and cognitively demanding to evaluate
- In joint evaluation (comparing two charities), overhead aversion is much stronger than in single evaluation; providing tangible "what your gift buys" information mitigates the effect in both settings

### The nonprofit starvation cycle

The overhead myth feeds the nonprofit starvation cycle (Stanford Social Innovation Review): donors reward low overhead → nonprofits underreport and underinvest → under-investment reduces effectiveness → nonprofits are less able to demonstrate results → donors remain overhead-focused.

Kim, Charbonneau & Sowa (2025, Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly) find that nonprofit managers may artificially lower overhead ratios by approximately 10 percentage points to remain competitive. This makes overhead ratios even less reliable as donor-evaluation tools.

The 2013 "Overhead Myth" open letter (Charity Navigator, BBB Wise Giving Alliance, GuideStar) formally called on donors to stop using overhead as a primary evaluation criterion, citing Indiana University research, the Urban Institute, and the Bridgespan Group.

### Practical implication for fundraisers

- Do not lead with overhead ratio in stewardship materials
- Provide concrete, tangible "your $X did Y" reporting (mitigates evaluability bias)
- For major campaigns, consider whether an overhead-free structure (seed gift covering admin costs) is feasible — the Gneezy effect is large and well-replicated

## 6. Lapsed-Donor Reactivation

### Who is worth pursuing

Not all lapsed donors are equal. Fader, Hardie & Shang (BG/BB model for nonprofits) and Schweidel & Knox demonstrate that **recency is far more important than frequency** in predicting reactivation:
- A donor who gave once 6 months ago is more likely to give again than a donor who gave 10 times but whose last gift was 5 years ago
- Long-lapsed donors tend not to be productive for repeat giving (Journal of Marketing Research, managing members vs. donors study)
- Long-lapsed **members** (membership NPOs) may still be worth pursuing for renewal — the commitment signal of membership is more durable

### First-year giving as a commitment signal

Neon One practitioner research: supporters who made **more than one gift in the first year** were 39% less likely to lapse in subsequent years compared to one-gift first-year donors. The dollar amount of the first gift mattered far less (a 10x increase in first gift predicted only a 4% decrease in lapse likelihood). Implication: early second-gift cultivation (the "first-gift follow-through" campaign) is high-leverage.

### Mailing frequency for reactivation

Feng (2013, Journal of Nonprofit & Public Sector Marketing) using hazard-model analysis of a large NPO:
- Sending marketing communications with appropriate frequency **positively predicts revival**
- Overly frequent communications **trigger resistance** and reduce reactivation probability
- The optimal frequency is donor-history dependent

Social information for lapsed reactivation (Meer 2015): high social information raises the amount among those who do give back (+45%) but **reduces the probability of giving** (−4.1%). For lapsed audiences, the participation effect may dominate, making high social-information tactics counterproductive.

### Reactivation message framing

- **Acknowledge the lapse** without guilt-loading — "we've missed you" rather than "you haven't given"
- **Remind of past impact** to reconnect identity ("your 2021 gift helped us do X")
- **Lower the threshold** for re-entry: consider a reduced or flexible ask for first win-back gift; rebuilding the relationship matters more than the transaction amount
- **Reactivation vs. acquisition economics**: it generally costs less to reactivate a lapsed donor than to acquire a new one (Blackbaud; Aldrich 2000), but only within a reasonable recency window
