# LinkedIn Profile Optimization and Personal Branding

## Overview

A resume is evaluated once, by one employer, for one role. A LinkedIn profile is evaluated continuously,
by an unbounded set of recruiters, search crawlers, and connections, for whatever role a search query
implies that day. It is an indexed, ranked, permanently-searchable database record. Optimization means
writing for three simultaneous readers: LinkedIn's search/ranking system, a recruiter skimming a
search-results snippet, and a human deciding whether to click "connect" or send an InMail.

This reference does not repeat the resume-vs-LinkedIn tone distinction already covered in
`resume-and-cv-writing.md` §7 (first-person, conversational, don't paste bullets verbatim). It covers the
mechanics of getting found and getting responded to:

Nearly everything below about *how the algorithm weighs sections* comes from career-coaching and SEO
blogs running informal tests, not from LinkedIn engineering documentation. Where a claim traces to
LinkedIn's own published data (its Talent Blog, Economic Graph research, or company statements to press),
that is noted explicitly. Where a specific number (e.g., "headline is 60% of ranking weight") has no
traceable primary source, it is flagged as directional, not verified.

## 1. Headline Formula

**The single highest-leverage field on the profile.** The headline appears in every search result,
every comment, every connection request, and next to your name in messages — it is read far more often
than the About section is ever opened.[^1]

**The default is a wasted opportunity.** LinkedIn auto-populates the headline from your current job
title and employer ("Marketing Manager at Acme Corp"). This tells a recruiter nothing beyond what the
Experience section already shows, and it competes for search visibility against every other person who
holds that exact title.[^1][^2]

### The formula

Multiple independent sources converge on the same structure, differing mainly in how many of the four
slots they make explicit:[^1][^2]

> **[Target role/title] | [Specialization or domain] | [2-3 supporting keywords] | [Outcome or
> credibility signal]**

Concretely: `Senior Product Manager | B2B SaaS + Payments | Grew ARR $5M → $20M` beats `Product Manager
at Company` because it front-loads the searchable title, states the specialization a recruiter is
filtering for, and closes with a quantified result that gives a human reader a reason to click through.

**For career changers or those without a clean title match:** lead with the *target* title language,
not your current internal title, then bridge with the transferable domain: `Data Analyst → Data
Scientist | SQL, Python, Predictive Modeling | Ex-[Industry]`.

### Character budget and front-loading

The headline field allows up to 220 characters, but mobile search results and the LinkedIn app truncate
display to roughly 100-120 characters — put the job title and top keyword first; anything after that is
a bonus for desktop viewers, not something to rely on.[^1]

### Exact market terminology, not internal titles

If your employer's HRIS calls you a "Customer Happiness Wizard" or "Growth Ninja," that phrase does not
match a recruiter's Boolean search for "Customer Success Manager." Pull the standard market title from
5-10 job postings for the role you want and use that phrase, not your employer's internal branding.[^1]
This mirrors the ATS keyword-mirroring principle in `ats-resume-optimization.md` §4 — the audience
differs (LinkedIn search vs. an ATS parser) but the mechanism (exact-phrase matching) is the same.

### Avoid keyword stuffing in the headline specifically

Cramming eight keywords separated by pipes reads as spam and, per several practitioner sources, is
penalized by LinkedIn's more recent ranking passes.[^1] Keep the headline to one primary title plus 2-3
supporting terms; push broader keyword density into the About section, which has far more room (see §2).

### Reported impact

Practitioner sources report that rewriting a generic auto-filled headline into a keyword-plus-outcome
version can lift profile views by roughly 40% and increase recruiter messages several-fold.[^1] This
figure comes from career-coaching case studies, not a controlled study — treat as directional, not a
guaranteed multiplier.

## 2. About Section Structure

### Field mechanics

The About section allows up to 2,600 characters. Only the first ~265-300 characters (roughly 3 lines)
display before a "…see more" truncation on both desktop and mobile — everything after that is read only
by someone who chose to click through.[^3] Treat those first 300 characters the way a landing page treats
its above-the-fold hook: it is competing for attention, not narrating your full career.

### Recommended structure: Hook → Bridge → Proof → CTA

A structure several career-coaching sources converge on:[^3]

1. **Hook** (first ~300 characters, visible without a click): a specific, first-person statement of what
   you do and for whom — not "Results-driven professional with 10 years of experience." State the
   specific value or problem you solve.
2. **Bridge**: how you got here — the trajectory, not a chronological list (that's what Experience is
   for).
3. **Proof**: 2-4 quantified accomplishments or a short list of domains/tools, written in prose, not
   resume bullets.
4. **CTA**: an explicit, low-friction invitation — what you want a reader to do next (connect, reach out
   about a specific kind of role, check the Featured section).

### Length: don't max out the field

Despite the 2,600-character ceiling, most guidance recommends staying in an 1,800-2,200 character range.
A full-field wall of text is rarely read past the hook; the field's generosity is capacity, not a target.[^3]

### Keyword density without stuffing

The About section is indexed for search, and 5-12 relevant keywords woven naturally through the prose
(not stacked as a list at the end) is the commonly cited range.[^1][^3] A trailing block of comma-
separated keywords at the bottom of the About section is an older tactic that more recent LinkedIn
ranking passes reportedly penalize rather than reward.[^9]

### Voice reminder (pointer, not repeated)

First-person, conversational — see `resume-and-cv-writing.md` §7 for the full resume-vs-LinkedIn tone
distinction. Do not open with "I am a results-driven professional" — that phrase appears on a large
fraction of profiles and signals template use rather than voice.

## 3. Featured Section Curation

### What it is and why it's underused

The Featured section sits directly below the About section and above Activity — it is the first
opportunity to show, not tell: work samples, published articles, case studies, press mentions,
presentation decks, or a LinkedIn newsletter. Estimates suggest only 15-20% of profiles actively curate
it, most leaving it empty or filled with stale, off-topic content — which makes a well-curated Featured
section a real differentiation opportunity rather than a checkbox.[^4]

### Curate, don't accumulate

LinkedIn caps the section at a small number of pinned items, and every source converges on the same
principle: 3-5 items, ordered by priority (the first slot gets disproportionate attention), each one
representing current, relevant work.[^4] A Featured section with 3 strong, on-message items outperforms
one with a dozen mediocre or outdated links — old projects from a previous career direction actively
confuse a visitor about current focus and should be removed, not left as a "more is more" archive.

### What belongs there

Original posts/articles demonstrating expertise, external portfolio or GitHub links, case studies or
whitepapers, a LinkedIn newsletter (which auto-populates into Featured), media mentions, and testimonial
or demo videos.[^4] Match content to career stage — a job seeker's Featured section should skew toward
work samples and proof of skill; a business-development-facing profile should skew toward case studies
and client outcomes.

### Maintenance

Treat it as a living section, not set-and-forget — review quarterly and rotate content as priorities
shift. Desktop shows a wider grid; mobile shows a swipeable carousel with fewer visible thumbnails, so
custom Featured images should be checked on both.[^4]

## 4. Experience Section: Rewriting for LinkedIn's Format

This section covers the *structural* mechanics LinkedIn provides for Experience entries — not the tone
shift already covered in `resume-and-cv-writing.md` §7. Three mechanics matter and are routinely left
unused:

### Per-position skills tagging

LinkedIn lets you attach a set of skills directly to each individual position, distinct from the
profile-level Skills section — these per-position skills surface directly under that job's description
and also roll up into the aggregate Skills section.[^5] Guidance commonly cited is roughly the top 5
skills genuinely used in that specific role, not a copy-pasted list repeated identically across every
position — repetition without differentiation signals template-filling rather than genuine per-role
expertise, and undermines the specificity that helps a recruiter's search match a *particular* role's
requirements to a *particular* position on your profile.

### Multimedia attachments per position

Each Experience entry supports attached media: images, PDFs, slide decks, external links, and video,
scoped to that specific role.[^5] This is the mechanism for showing artifacts recruiters otherwise never
see — a shipped design, a published deck, a demo recording — anchored to the job where you produced it,
rather than dumped generically into Featured. For individual contributors (engineers, designers,
analysts) this is frequently higher-leverage than prose description, because it is evidence rather than
a claim.

### Achievement-metric-impact, not job-description prose

The most common failure mode in LinkedIn Experience sections is pasting the internal job description
instead of writing achievement-first, metric-anchored statements — the same X-Y-Z discipline covered in
`resume-and-cv-writing.md` §1-2, just rewritten in first person for LinkedIn's voice. LinkedIn's search
weighting reportedly favors Experience descriptions that use specific role and skill terminology over
generic responsibility language, which rewards the same discipline recruiters reward on the human read:
achievement language surfaces the keywords a search actually needs, and job-description prose does not.[^9]

## 5. Skills & Endorsements Strategy

### The top-3 mechanic

LinkedIn displays only your first three (pinned) skills before a visitor has to click "Show all
skills" — those three receive dramatically more visibility than everything ranked below them, both to a
human scanning the profile and reportedly in how the search algorithm weights skill matches.[^6] The
practical rule: pin the three skills that most precisely match your current target role, and re-pin
immediately if that target changes — a stale pinned skill from a prior job search actively misdirects
recruiter search relevance.

### What recruiters actually search for

Recruiters overwhelmingly search LinkedIn Recruiter by specific hard-skill and tool terms — "Python,"
"Salesforce," "GAAP," "Figma" — not generic soft-skill terms like "Leadership" or "Communication,"
which nearly every profile lists and which carry no discriminatory power in a filtered search.[^6] This
mirrors the ATS hard-skill-vs-soft-skill guidance in `ats-resume-optimization.md` §4: soft skills belong
in narrative (About, Experience achievement statements), not in a keyword-indexed skills field where they
add no filtering signal.

**Use specific, long-tail skill terms over generic ones.** "Leading Cross-Functional Teams Through
Reorgs" surfaces in fewer, more targeted searches than "Leadership," and the searches it does surface in
are the ones a hiring manager for a specific, matching role is actually running.[^6]

### The endorsement-gaming problem

LinkedIn's endorsement mechanic — one-click, no-verification approval of a skill by any connection — is
widely gamed and widely distrusted as a signal. Sources disagree on how much endorsement *count* matters
to actual ranking: one recruiter-facing view is dismissive (not something recruiters filter by, chasing
volume beats adding relevant skills or recommendations); a competing view treats a zero-endorsement skill
as looking unverified, worth a handful of endorsements even if chasing dozens isn't. A stronger claim —
that an unendorsed skill is invisible to search entirely — has no verifiable primary citation; treat as
unconfirmed.[^6]

**Practical synthesis:** endorsement *quality* (who endorsed you — a peer or manager with domain
authority) matters more than *quantity*. The reciprocity mechanic is real and low-effort: endorsing five
colleagues a week for skills you've genuinely observed reliably prompts a return endorsement, without
resorting to endorsement-swap groups or bot services that read as inauthentic to a recruiter who checks
who's doing the endorsing.[^6]

### LinkedIn Skill Assessments

LinkedIn's own timed skill-assessment quizzes award a visible "Verified" badge on a passed skill, and
this is one of the few endorsement-adjacent signals that is not gameable by reciprocal-endorsement rings
— it functions as a real, if narrow, credential rather than a popularity count.

### Prune irrelevant skills

Remove obsolete tools, skills from a prior career direction, and filler ("Microsoft Office," "team
player" — the latter isn't even a hard skill and shouldn't be in a skills field at all). A cluttered
skills list dilutes the signal for your actual target role and can surface you in searches for roles you
are not pursuing.[^6]

## 6. Recommendations

Distinct from endorsements: a recommendation is a written testimonial from a named connection, and
carries substantially more weight than a one-click endorsement because it provides context and specific
evidence rather than a bare skill checkmark.[^7]

**Reported impact:** profiles with three or more recommendations are reported to be viewed several times
more often than profiles without any, and recruiters report being more likely to reach out to a candidate
with strong, specific recommendations.[^7] These figures come from career-coaching sources citing
aggregated LinkedIn data, not an independently audited study — treat the specific multipliers as
directional.

**Quality over quantity — the same pattern as endorsements.** Three to five specific, named
recommendations that cite a concrete project or outcome read as more credible than twenty generic ones.
A recommendation that says "great to work with" from an unspecified relationship reads as noise;
recruiters have learned to discount generic recommendation text the same way they discount generic resume
language.[^7]

**Request strategy:**
- **Timing**: ask within about two weeks of finishing a project or right after a positive
  performance-review conversation, while the specifics are fresh — a request sent months or years later
  forces the recommender to reconstruct details from memory and yields a vaguer result.[^7]
- **Specificity**: a bare "can you write me a recommendation?" gives the recipient no direction and gets
  postponed indefinitely. Suggest the specific project, outcome, or quality you'd like them to speak to.
- **Reciprocity**: writing a recommendation for a peer first measurably increases the odds they return
  one.
- **Diversify sources**: recommendations from different roles, managers, peers, and clients demonstrate
  range better than five recommendations from the same team.

## 7. Open To Work Signaling

### Two distinct modes

LinkedIn's Open To Work setting has two visibility tiers with materially different tradeoffs:[^8]

| Mode | Visible to | Visual signal |
|---|---|---|
| **All LinkedIn Members (public)** | Everyone, including your current employer and colleagues | Green #OpenToWork photo frame |
| **Recruiters Only (private)** | Only users holding a paid LinkedIn Recruiter seat | No visible frame |

Note the caveat: "Recruiters Only" does not mean *every* recruiter — LinkedIn sells its recruiting
product in tiers, and a recruiter without a Recruiter seat license will not see the private signal at
all.

### Measured effectiveness

LinkedIn's own reporting states that activating Open To Work (either mode) roughly doubles the rate at
which recruiters message a candidate, and the public green-frame option adds a further ~40% lift on top
of that.[^8] A separate LinkedIn-reported figure: members who activated the signal saw roughly a 14.5%
positive response rate to recruiter outreach, versus 4.6% for members without it.[^8] Scale context: more
than 220 million members have activated some form of the signal (public or private), a reported 35%
year-over-year increase reflecting a harder job market; more than 3 million have used the public photo
frame specifically since its 2020 launch.[^8]

### The social-cost tradeoff

The public badge is visible to your current employer and colleagues — a real risk if you are employed
and quietly exploring. The private "Recruiters Only" setting captures most of the benefit (the ~2x
messaging lift) without that exposure; you forgo only the incremental ~40% public-badge boost.[^8] For
anyone currently employed, this makes the private mode close to a strictly dominant choice unless you are
also comfortable being visibly job-hunting to your employer.

### Perception is genuinely split, not settled

A 2023 ResumeBuilder.com hiring-manager survey found 46% viewed the public badge favorably, 34%
neutrally, 20% negatively; a separate November 2023 poll of nearly 3,000 recruiters (Hung Lee, Recruiting
Brainfood) found recruiters overwhelmingly positive — it identifies people who will actually respond.[^8]
The "signals desperation" critique concentrates in agency and executive search, chasing passive
candidates who are *not* visibly looking; for ordinary IC and mid-management roles, recruiters put off by
the badge were unlikely to reach out productively regardless. Mass adoption during layoff waves has also
diluted the signal's distinguishing power simply through volume.[^8]

**Practical rule:** employed and passively exploring → Recruiters Only. Unemployed or actively,
visibly searching → the public badge's incremental lift is usually worth the exposure, since the
audience most likely to react negatively (your current employer) is no longer a live liability.

## 8. Social Selling Index (SSI) — Mostly Skip It for Job Seekers

The SSI is a LinkedIn-published 0-100 score across four 25-point pillars: establishing a professional
brand, finding the right people, engaging with insights, and building relationships.[^11] It was built
and marketed as a sales-enablement diagnostic for quota-carrying salespeople using LinkedIn for
prospecting — not as a job-search or personal-branding metric.

**Why it's largely irrelevant to this file's audience:** LinkedIn's own Sales Navigator materials now
state the score "no longer accurately reflects the modern sales environment" and doesn't correlate with
measurable sales outcomes — LinkedIn itself is deprioritizing SSI in favor of AI-driven tools inside
Sales Navigator.[^11] It measures activity that *resembles* social selling (posting, connecting,
engaging), not deal quality or, for a job seeker, actual recruiter response rate.

**When it has any diagnostic value:** a very low score (well under 30) does flag an essentially dormant
profile — a real problem independent of what SSI calls it. But optimizing *toward* an SSI number chases
a proxy metric LinkedIn itself has disavowed. The mechanics in §1-6 (headline, About, Featured, skills)
drive recruiter discovery directly; chasing SSI points does not.

## 9. Recruiter InMail Response Psychology

This section covers what happens on the *receiving* end of profile optimization: once you're found (or
once you receive a cold InMail as a candidate), what determines whether it gets a reply.

### Baseline response rates

LinkedIn InMail substantially outperforms cold email — commonly cited average InMail response rates run
10-25%, versus roughly 1-5% for cold email generally.[^10] Recruiting-specific outreach performs at the
high end of that range (18-25%) because the recipient is, by definition, more receptive to a job-related
message than to unsolicited sales outreach.[^10]

### What drives a reply, mechanism by mechanism

- **Personalization.** A tailored InMail referencing something specific to the recipient's profile
  outperforms templated bulk sends by a wide margin — one cited figure is a 30% response-rate lift.[^10]
  Maps to Amy Cuddy's competence/warmth framework: personalization signals the sender did the work of
  reading the profile before writing.
- **Warmth and natural language.** Corporate boilerplate reads as low-warmth; messages that sound like a
  person talking perform better — Cialdini's similarity-based liking: people respond more readily to
  senders who read as similar to themselves rather than as a scripted recruiter.[^10]
- **Brevity.** InMails over roughly 200 words see lower response rates; under 100 words outperforms.[^10]
- **The information/curiosity gap.** Counterintuitively, InMails that withhold some detail (full salary,
  full scope) rather than front-loading everything perform better — Loewenstein's information-gap theory:
  a message that answers every question up front removes the reason to reply and ask.[^10]
- **Social proof via mutual connections.** One of the strongest single levers measured — InMails citing a
  mutual connection are reported at roughly 3x the response rate of those that don't; weaker affiliation
  signals (same school, same group) measurably help too.[^10] Same "trust your trusted contacts' trust"
  mechanism documented generally in `applied-psychology`.
- **Timing.** Tuesday-Thursday, mid-morning or early afternoon, outperforms Monday/Friday sends.[^10]
- **Low-friction close.** A permission-based ask ("Open to a quick chat?") outperforms an aggressive
  calendar link or "Apply here" CTA.[^10]

**For the candidate side of this same exchange:** the profile mechanics in §1-6 are what get you *found*
and what an InMail sender reads before writing — a strong headline and About section give the sender
something specific to personalize around, which is the single highest-leverage lever on the list above.

## 10. Profile Photo & Banner Conventions

### Profile photo

LinkedIn's own published data states that profiles with a photo receive substantially more engagement —
figures LinkedIn has cited include roughly 21x more profile views, 9x more connection requests, and 36x
more messages compared with profiles lacking a photo.[^12] These are LinkedIn's own internal-data
figures (first publicized around 2017 and repeated since, including by LinkedIn career-expert
spokespeople); some LinkedIn-adjacent sources cite a lower 14x figure for views, suggesting the exact
multiplier has varied across publications and should be read as directional, not a precise current
statistic. The underlying point — a photo produces a large, LinkedIn-verified engagement lift — is not
in dispute even if the specific multiple is inexact.

**Composition guidance:**
- Light or neutral background; a darker background is acceptable if it doesn't compete with the subject.
- Face occupying roughly 60% of the frame; top-of-shoulders to top-of-head framing performs best in
  likeability research.
- A Photofeeler perceptual study found artsy, tightly cropped close-ups reduce likeability ratings versus
  a standard head-and-shoulders headshot.[^13]
- Avoid group-photo crops and selfies — a JDP survey found roughly 9% of users still use selfies and 15%
  use group-photo crops, both of which read as unprofessional and, for selfies, distort framing angle and
  distance.[^13]

### Banner (background) image

**Technical spec:** 1584 × 396 px (4:1 ratio), max 8MB, with any text or logo kept inside the central
1260 × 300px "safe zone" — content outside that zone crops on mobile and where the profile photo overlaps
the bottom-left corner.[^13]

**Function, distinct from the photo:** the profile photo answers "who is this," the banner answers
"why should you care" — it is brand space, not a second headshot. Common, low-risk conventions: a
workspace or office image (signals professional, reserved competence), an abstract brand-color gradient
carrying a short value statement, or industry-relevant imagery tied to the About section's narrative.[^13]

**The default is a visible non-signal.** A large share of profiles still display LinkedIn's default blue
gradient banner — which reads, at minimum, as an unattended profile and forfeits free, high-visibility
brand real estate immediately adjacent to the headline and photo.[^13]

## 11. How LinkedIn's Search/Ranking Algorithm Actually Surfaces Profiles

LinkedIn has never published its ranking formula. What follows is the consensus model reconstructed by
career-SEO practitioners and coaching sources through observed testing — treat specific weights (e.g.,
"headline is X% of ranking") as directional claims from unverified third-party sources, not confirmed
LinkedIn engineering documentation, even where they are repeated consistently across sources.[^9]

### The commonly cited factor set

1. **Keyword relevance and placement.** Reported scan order, descending weight: headline → About →
   current-position title/description → skills → past positions → education. Matching is reported as
   substantially exact-phrase — "data engineer" ranks exact-phrase matches above profiles where the two
   words merely both appear separately.[^9]
2. **Profile completeness ("All-Star" status).** LinkedIn's completeness ladder (Beginner → Intermediate
   → Advanced → Expert → All-Star) requires, at minimum: a photo, headline, location/industry, a current
   position with description, one or two past positions, education, roughly five skills, and 50+
   connections.[^14] Multiple sources describe completeness as a *gate* — an incomplete profile is
   reported down-ranked regardless of keyword match, capping optimization's effect until All-Star is
   reached. Some sources note LinkedIn removed the visible strength-meter UI around 2023 in some rollouts
   while the underlying signal persisted; verify current UI state against your own account.
3. **Network proximity.** A 1st-degree connection with moderate keyword match is reported to often
   outrank a 3rd-degree connection with a stronger match — LinkedIn's trust layer favors graph closeness.
   Growing a relevant, real network (not connection-request spam) compounds search visibility.[^9]
4. **Activity and engagement signals.** Profiles with recent posts, comments, or edits are reported to
   rank above otherwise-identical dormant profiles, even in searches unrelated to that activity.[^9]
5. **Emerging semantic/AI matching.** 2025-2026 sources describe LinkedIn layering AI-driven semantic
   scoring atop keyword/Boolean matching, meaning profile *depth* may now matter beyond keyword presence
   — an emerging, not settled, claim with no independently verifiable primary source.[^9]

### The practical takeaway

Because the formula is unpublished and contested even among practitioners: (a) reach and maintain
All-Star completeness first — a documented gate; (b) place top keywords in headline, About, and
current-position description, in that priority order; (c) build a real, relevant network rather than a
vanity connection count; (d) stay intermittently active rather than dormant. Don't over-invest in chasing
an unverified weighting scheme reported by a single blog.

## 12. Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Leaving the auto-generated headline ("[Title] at [Company]") | Wastes the single highest-visibility field; matches thousands of identical headlines |
| Headline keyword-stuffed with 6+ pipe-separated terms | Reads as spam; reportedly penalized by recent ranking passes |
| About section opening with "Results-driven professional with X years of experience" | Generic opener used on a large fraction of profiles; signals template, not voice |
| Filling the full 2,600-character About field to the max | Rarely read past the ~300-character preview; bloats past the point of being read |
| Trailing keyword list stacked at the bottom of About | Older tactic; recent ranking passes are reported to penalize it versus natural placement |
| Empty or stale Featured section | Forfeits the highest-visibility "proof" real estate on the profile; signals inattention |
| Featured section crammed with 10+ items from old, unrelated projects | Dilutes focus; confuses a visitor about current direction |
| Identical skills list copy-pasted across every Experience entry | Signals template-filling rather than per-role expertise; misses per-position search matching |
| Soft skills ("Communication," "Leadership," "Team Player") in the Skills field | No discriminatory value in recruiter Boolean search; belongs in narrative, not a keyword field |
| Chasing endorsement volume via reciprocal-endorsement swap groups | Low-credibility signal; recruiters who check who's endorsing see through it |
| Vague recommendation requests ("can you write me a recommendation?") | No direction given; gets postponed or produces generic, low-value text |
| Public Open To Work badge while employed and not ready to be visibly job-hunting | Visible to current employer and colleagues; the private "Recruiters Only" mode captures most of the benefit without the exposure |
| Optimizing toward a specific SSI score | LinkedIn itself states the score doesn't correlate with real outcomes; it's a sales-activity proxy, not a job-search metric |
| Default blue-gradient banner | Free, high-visibility brand real estate left unused; reads as an unattended profile |
| Selfie or group-photo crop as profile picture | Reads as unprofessional; distorts framing and undercuts the photo's engagement lift |
| Sending InMail-style outreach over 200 words with no personalization | Measurably lower response rate than short, personalized, socially-proofed messages |
| Treating the profile as a one-time setup task | Recent activity is a reported ranking factor; a dormant profile is down-ranked relative to an active one with equal keyword content |

## 13. Quick Reference

**Highest-leverage moves, in priority order:**
1. Reach and confirm All-Star completeness (photo, headline, location, current + 2 past positions,
   education, 5+ skills, 50+ connections) — this reportedly gates every other optimization.
2. Rewrite the headline: target title + specialization + 2-3 keywords + one quantified outcome signal.
3. Rewrite the About section's first 300 characters as a hook; use Hook-Bridge-Proof-CTA for the rest;
   stay in the 1,800-2,200 character range.
4. Curate Featured to 3-5 current, on-message items, strongest first.
5. Tag 3-5 genuinely-used skills per Experience entry; attach a work artifact where you have one.
6. Pin the 3 skills that match your current target role; prune irrelevant/soft-skill entries.
7. Request 3-5 specific, timely recommendations rather than chasing endorsement volume.
8. Decide Open To Work mode based on employment status: employed/passive → Recruiters Only; actively
   searching → public badge is usually worth the exposure.
9. Ignore SSI for job-search purposes; it's a sales-activity proxy LinkedIn itself has deprioritized.
10. Replace the default banner; use a real headshot (no selfies, no group crops).
11. Stay intermittently active (occasional post/comment) — dormant profiles are reported to rank lower
    than active ones with identical keyword content.
