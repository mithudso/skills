## 1. SERVQUAL and the P − E logic

SERVQUAL is the dominant service-quality measurement paradigm in marketing, from a connected program by
**A. Parasuraman, Valarie A. Zeithaml & Leonard L. Berry (PZB)**. Two anchor papers:

- **1985 conceptual model** — PZB, "A Conceptual Model of Service Quality and Its Implications for Future
  Research," *Journal of Marketing* 49(4):41–50. Established the **disconfirmation logic** — "service
  quality perceptions result from a comparison of consumer expectations with actual service performance" —
  that quality covers *both* outcome and the *process* of delivery, introduced the gaps framework and the
  **10 determinants**, and called for "a standard instrument." **[F]**
- **1988 instrument** — PZB, "SERVQUAL: A Multiple-Item Scale for Measuring Consumer Perceptions of
  Service Quality," *Journal of Retailing* 64(1):12–40. Operationalized the construct as a **22-item
  instrument across five dimensions** (refined down from ~97 items / 10 dimensions). **[F]**

**Measurement form (the gap score).** Two batteries of 22 statements on (typically) 7-point Likert scales
— one for **Expectations (E)** of what firms in the industry *should* offer, one for **Perceptions (P)**
of the focal firm's actual performance. Item quality = **Q = P − E**; more positive (or less negative) =
higher perceived quality. The 1985 logic (Proposition 8): **E > P** → quality below satisfactory; **E =
P** → satisfactory; **E < P** → better than satisfactory. **[F]**

**Chronology note.** The **gaps model + 10 determinants = 1985 (*Journal of Marketing*)**; the **22-item,
5-dimension instrument = 1988 (*Journal of Retailing*)**; the **RATER** mnemonic and refined definitions
are most associated with the **1990 book** *Delivering Quality Service* (Free Press) and the 1991
refinement (*Journal of Retailing* 67(4):420–450). **[F]**

## 2. Ten original dimensions → five RATER dimensions

The **1985 paper named ten determinants:** reliability, responsiveness, competence, access, courtesy,
communication, credibility, security, understanding/knowing the customer, and tangibles. During scale
development (1988), factor analysis found high item intercorrelation: **Tangibles, Reliability,
Responsiveness stayed intact**, and the other seven **collapsed into two broader dimensions** —
**Assurance** (competence + courtesy + credibility + security) and **Empathy** (access + communication +
understanding). PZB themselves note Assurance and Empathy "contain items representing seven original
dimensions." **[F]**

**The five RATER dimensions:**

| | Dimension | Definition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R** | **Reliability** | Ability to perform the promised service **dependably and accurately**. *PZB's repeated finding: the most important dimension to customers.* |
| **A** | **Assurance** | **Knowledge and courtesy of employees and their ability to inspire/convey trust and confidence.** |
| **T** | **Tangibles** | Appearance of **physical facilities, equipment, personnel, communication materials** (and, in modern usage, digital evidence). *Consistently least important.* |
| **E** | **Empathy** | **Caring, individualized attention.** |
| **R** | **Responsiveness** | **Willingness to help customers and provide prompt service.** |

Two well-replicated regularities from PZB's multi-study program: **Reliability ranks most important,
Tangibles least important**. **[F]** The precise *mid-rankings* vary by source/industry — treat
Reliability-first / Tangibles-last as solid, the middle three as **[Q]**.

**Terminology caveat.** "RATER" is a later **mnemonic reordering**; PZB originally presented the
dimensions as tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy ("TRRAE" in the book). RATER is a
memory aid, not the original sequence. **[Q]**

## 3. The Gaps Model (the "5 gaps")

The 1985 paper's core contribution: perceived service quality (the customer-side **Gap 5**) is **a
function of four provider-side gaps** — Proposition 6: **GAP5 = f(GAP1, GAP2, GAP3, GAP4)**. **[F]**

- **Gap 1 — Knowledge / Listening gap** (customer expectation ↔ management perception): what customers
  expect vs. what management *thinks* they expect. Causes: weak market research, poor upward
  communication from frontline staff, too many management layers. **[F]**
- **Gap 2 — Standards / Design gap** (management perception ↔ service-quality specifications): correct
  understanding *not translated* into clear, measurable standards. Causes: resource constraints, no formal
  goal-setting, poor service design. (Classic example: a policy that phones be answered "quickly" without
  specifying a ring count.) **[F]**
- **Gap 3 — Delivery / Performance gap** (specifications ↔ actual delivery): failing to deliver to your
  own standards. Drivers: employee performance variability, inadequate training, role
  ambiguity/conflict, demand-supply mismatch. **[F]**
- **Gap 4 — Communication gap** (delivery ↔ external communications): external promises (advertising,
  sales, website) ≠ what's delivered — **over-promising** raises expectations and depresses perceived
  quality. (Subtler form: failing to *communicate* unseen quality you *do* deliver.) **[F]**
- **Gap 5 — Customer gap** (expected ↔ perceived service): the outcome gap that manifests as
  (dis)satisfaction; it is the **function of Gaps 1–4**. Close the four internal gaps and Gap 5 shrinks.
  **[F]**

Customer expectations in the model are shaped by **word-of-mouth, personal needs, and past experience**
(plus the firm's external communications). **[F]** Note: a minority of sources enumerate the gaps
differently or add Gaps 6–7 (employee-perception gaps) — these are **extensions**, not PZB's original
five. **[Q]**

**TAM application.** The Gaps Model is a diagnostic tree for *where* a satisfaction shortfall originates:
is it a listening gap (we don't actually know what this account expects), a standards gap (no clear SLA),
a delivery gap (we miss our own SLA), or a communication gap (sales/marketing over-promised)? Gap 4 is
the same mechanism as EDT's expectation-setting lever (`satisfaction-theory.md` §1).

## 4. Critiques and the SERVPERF challenge (genuinely CONTESTED)

SERVQUAL is heavily and persistently criticized; **SERVQUAL vs. SERVPERF is one of the least-resolved
debates in services-marketing measurement.** The canonical critical review is **Buttle (1996),
"SERVQUAL: review, critique, research agenda," *European Journal of Marketing* 30(1):8–32**, which sorts
objections into theoretical and operational classes:

- **(a) The P − E (difference-score) problem.** Difference scores are psychometrically problematic — poor
  reliability, variance-restriction, discriminant-validity issues; the relationship between overall
  quality and computed gap scores is often weak; averaging gaps across dimensions is hard to justify given
  dimensional instability. **[F]** (Buttle 1996; Cronin & Taylor 1992; Jain & Gupta 2004)
- **(b) The expectations-interpretation problem.** "Expectation" is **polysemic** (prediction vs. ideal
  vs. "should" vs. desired vs. minimum-tolerable); PZB themselves shifted the wording across revisions;
  SERVQUAL has been criticized for eliciting *inflated/ideal* expectations that mechanically produce large
  negative gaps. **[F]** (Boulding et al. 1993, *JMR*; Teas 1993, *Journal of Marketing*)
- **(c) Dimensionality instability.** The five dimensions are **not universal/stable** — the number and
  structure vary by context, items often fail to load on their a-priori factors, and the RATER dimensions
  are positively intercorrelated (poor discriminant validity); many studies recover fewer than five.
  **[F]** (Carman 1990, *Journal of Retailing*; Buttle 1996)
- **(d) SERVPERF (performance-only) — Cronin & Taylor (1992), *Journal of Marketing* 56(3):55–68.** Argued
  SERVQUAL **confounds satisfaction and attitude** and that service quality is best modeled as an
  **attitude**; proposed **SERVPERF** — the same 22 items measuring **perceived performance ONLY (no
  expectations battery)**. Across four industries, the performance-only measure **outperformed SERVQUAL**
  psychometrically and supported **service quality → satisfaction → purchase intentions** (with
  satisfaction the stronger driver). Independent replications (Babakus & Boller 1992; Brown, Churchill &
  Peter 1993) broadly supported the performance-only direction. PZB rebutted (1994, *Journal of Marketing*
  58(1):111–124); Cronin & Taylor replied in the same volume (pp.125–131). The exchange did not settle it.
  **[F]**

**The reconciliation (preserve the contradiction).**
- **Carrillat, Jaramillo & Mulki (2007), *Int'l J. Service Industry Management* 18(5):472–490** —
  meta-analysis, 17 studies / 42 effect sizes: **SERVQUAL and SERVPERF are *equally valid* predictors of
  overall service quality.** SERVQUAL's predictive validity *improves* when adapted to context;
  SERVPERF's does not. **[Q]** (one authoritative meta-analysis)
- **Jain & Gupta (2004), *Vikalpa* 29(2)** — the practitioner trade-off: SERVPERF is psychometrically
  superior and more parsimonious (half the data-collection burden), so it's preferable for
  *measuring/benchmarking* overall quality; **SERVQUAL retains superior *diagnostic* power** — its gap
  scores pinpoint *where* to intervene. The "better" scale depends on the objective. **[Q]**

**Bottom line:** SERVPERF wins on psychometrics and parsimony; SERVQUAL retains an edge on **managerial
diagnosticity**; meta-analytically they're **about equally predictive** of overall quality. The field
never reached consensus and both remain widely used. **[Q]**
