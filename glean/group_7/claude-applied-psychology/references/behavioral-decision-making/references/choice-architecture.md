# Choice Architecture, Nudges, Boosts & the Ethics of Influence

Source spine: Thaler & Sunstein, *Nudge* (2008; *Final Edition* 2021); Dolan et al.,
MINDSPACE (2010); Behavioural Insights Team, EAST (2014); Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff,
"Nudging and Boosting" (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2017); Thaler on sludge.

**Premise:** there is no neutral way to present options. Order, defaults, count, and framing
all move choices, so the environment is *always* designed — the only question is whether
deliberately and in whose interest.

---

## The default — the single most powerful lever

Whatever a chooser gets by doing nothing is what most choosers end up with. Defaults work
because of status-quo bias, loss aversion relative to the default as reference point, and the
implied recommendation ("the default must be the sensible choice").
- *Evidence:* opt-out organ-donation countries have far higher consent rates than opt-in;
  automatic 401(k) enrollment sharply raises participation.
- *Operator use:* set the recommended plan tier, the secure configuration, or the renewal
  option as the default — provided it genuinely serves the customer. Make the *good* path the
  path of least resistance.

---

## EAST — the practical nudge checklist (BIT)

Make the desired behavior:
- **Easy** — reduce friction; use smart defaults; simplify the message and the steps.
- **Attractive** — draw attention (salience, personalization); make it appealing.
- **Social** — show that others (peers, similar accounts) do it; use norms honestly.
- **Timely** — prompt at the moment of receptivity; account for present bias.

EAST is the everyday operator tool — apply it to onboarding emails, in-product prompts,
renewal outreach. It replaced MINDSPACE as BIT's working model.

---

## MINDSPACE — nine influences (Institute for Government / BIT, 2010)

**M**essenger (we weight who delivers info), **I**ncentives (mental-accounting/loss-aversion
shaped), **N**orms (what others do), **D**efaults, **S**alience (what's novel/relevant),
**P**riming (subconscious cues), **A**ffect (emotional associations), **C**ommitments (public
pledges + consistency), **E**go (acting consistently with a positive self-image).
- *Health warning:* the **Priming** element rests on social-priming research that has
  **largely failed to replicate** — treat it as the weakest element. See
  `references/replication-status.md`.

## Sludge — the dark side (find it and remove it)

**Sludge** = choice architecture that adds friction making it harder for people to do what's
in their *own* interest: cancellation mazes, hidden opt-outs, forced phone calls to downgrade,
buried settings, confirm-shaming.
- *Operator imperative:* audit your **own** onboarding, upgrade, and renewal/cancellation flows
  for sludge and remove it. A frictionless cancellation builds more long-term trust than a
  sticky one retains revenue. Deploying sludge against a customer is a dark pattern and, in a
  TAM relationship, self-defeating.

---

## Nudge vs Boost — the key contrast (Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff, 2017)

| | **Nudge** | **Boost** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Steer behavior in a direction | Build the chooser's *competence* to decide |
| Theory base | Heuristics-and-biases (exploits System 1) | Simple-heuristics / ecological rationality (equips the chooser) |
| Locus of change | The environment | The person (knowledge, skills, tools) |
| Persists without the architect? | No — remove the nudge, behavior reverts | Yes — competence stays |
| Transparency / agency | Can be opaque; agency-preserving but passive | Transparent by design; agency-enhancing |
| Example | Default enrollment; rearrange the cafeteria | Teach natural-frequency risk reading; give a fast-and-frugal decision tree |

**When to prefer boosts (most TAM work):** long-term relationship, trust matters, you want the
customer to make good decisions *without you in the room*. Teaching a customer a simple decision
rule (a boost) compounds; a nudge you control does not. They're complementary, not exclusive.

---

## Choice overload (a caveat, itself contested)

Too many options can reduce satisfaction and the likelihood of choosing at all (the "jam study,"
Iyengar & Lepper 2000). **But** the effect is fragile — a large meta-analysis (Scheibehenne et al.,
2010) found a mean effect near zero, with strong moderators (preference clarity, categorization,
decision difficulty). Practically: don't *assume* more choice is worse, but *do* curate, categorize,
and provide a recommended default for complex decisions (plan selection, configuration options).

---

## Ethics of influence — the operator's rule

Three tests before deploying any choice-architecture move:
1. **Direction** — does it steer toward the *chooser's* interest (not just yours)?
2. **Transparency** — would the customer be fine with it if they saw exactly what you did?
3. **No sludge** — are you adding friction against their interest anywhere?

Pass all three → legitimate nudge or boost. Fail any → dark pattern. Default to **boosts** when
the relationship is long-term, which it is in account management.
