# Cause & Nonprofit Marketing

Marketing for a cause is not the same job as marketing a product. You are usually asking for one
of three things — *attention*, *money*, or *an action that costs the person nothing but a decision*
(registering as an organ donor). The economics are different (acquiring a new donor
is commonly cited as several times costlier than retaining one [UNVERIFIED — exact multiplier and
origin vary by source]), the emotional stakes are higher, and the ethical floor is higher because
you are often invoking suffering, death, or vulnerability to motivate behavior.

> **Using the numbers in this skill.** Every figure tagged `[UNVERIFIED]` was not confirmable
> against a primary source at authoring time; treat it as directional only and **re-pull it from the
> cited source before it appears in any published or customer-facing copy.** All statistics here are
> as of June 2026 and perish quickly (see Sources and Disclaimer).

This skill is written for a North Carolina organ-, eye-, and tissue-donation awareness venture
whose primary conversion is **registry sign-ups**, not dollars. That changes the playbook: your
"donation" is a free decision, your "donor" is a registrant, and your North-Star metric is
*registrations driven*, not revenue. Where a section is dollar-centric (fundraising campaigns,
donation pages), the same mechanics map cleanly onto registrant acquisition; the "ask" is just
"Register now" instead of "Give $50."

**Scope boundaries.** General for-profit positioning, local SEO, and Google Business Profile work
live in `venture-marketing-strategy-local-seo`. Design tooling (Canva, logo, brand visual system)
lives in `venture-canva-founder-brand-stack`; cross-reference those rather than duplicating here.

## The supporter / donor journey

Map every supporter onto a lifecycle and design content for the stage they are actually in. The
sector uses several overlapping frameworks (Moves Management: identification → qualification →
cultivation → solicitation → stewardship; the "Five A's": Awareness, Assessment, Action,
Affiliation, Advocacy). For a lean cause venture, collapse them to four stages (Engagement folds in
Assessment + early Affiliation; Retention folds in Advocacy):

1. **Awareness.** They learn the cause exists and why it matters. Channels: organic social,
   content/SEO, earned media, Ad Grants search, word of mouth. Goal: a name in your world (a
   follower, a page view, an email opt-in).
2. **Engagement.** They interact repeatedly before you ask for anything. Channels: email welcome
   series, social engagement, stories, events. Goal: enough trust and salience that the ask lands.
3. **Action.** They do the thing: register as a donor, donate money, sign up to volunteer, share.
   Channels: optimized landing page, the ask email, the event. Goal: a completed conversion.
4. **Retention / stewardship.** You thank, report impact, and keep them close so they advocate,
   give again, or recruit others. Channels: thank-you flows, impact updates, ambassador invites.

**Why retention matters even for a registry.** A registry sign-up is a one-time act — you cannot
"re-register" someone. So your retention goal shifts from *repeat donation* to *advocacy and
referral*: a registered supporter who recruits five friends is worth five more registrations. The
sector's retention numbers are sobering and argue for treating every new contact as precious:
overall donor retention fell to **42.9% in Q4 2024**, and **first-time donor retention hit a
record low of 19.4%** (Fundraising Effectiveness Project, Q4 2024, via Nonprofit Quarterly /
NonProfitPRO). Translation: ~4 of 5 first-time supporters never come back unless you deliberately
steward them.

**Lean tactic:** don't build elaborate journeys for everyone. Build one solid path —
opt-in → 3-email welcome series → registry ask → thank-you + "share with 3 people" — and instrument
it before adding branches.

## Cause storytelling & the ethics of the appeal

### The single identifiable victim

The most well-replicated finding in charitable-giving psychology: **people give far more to one
identifiable person than to a statistic, even when the statistic represents vastly more suffering.** This is the
*identifiable victim effect* (Small, Loewenstein, Slovic and others). Its companions:

- **Psychic numbing:** as the number of victims rises, emotional response and giving *flatten or
  decline* rather than scale up (Slovic, 2007). "If I look at the mass I will never act; if I look
  at the one, I will."
- **Compassion fade:** measured giving and affect are greatest for a *single* child and start
  dropping as soon as a second is added (Västfjäll, Slovic et al., *PLOS One*, 2014).
- A neuroimaging study found a photo (vs. a silhouette) of an identified orphan drove higher
  donations, mediated by positive arousal and nucleus-accumbens activity (*Journal of
  Neuroscience*, 2013).

**Application to an organ-donation drive:** lead with *one transplant recipient or one donor
family*, by name and face, with their story, not "108,000 people are waiting." Use the big number
as the *stakes behind* the one story, not as the lead. One concrete recipient ("Maria got a kidney
from a stranger and walked her daughter down the aisle") will out-pull "thousands need organs"
almost every time. **Caveat:** deliberative thinking can blunt the effect — when you ask people to
calculate or compare, the identifiable-victim advantage shrinks (Small et al.), so keep the appeal
emotionally concrete, not analytical.

### Hope vs. guilt vs. fear

- **Guilt appeals** can work in the short term but degrade with overuse. Guilt only converts if
  paired with **response efficacy** — a clear, doable action that resolves the guilt ("Register in
  90 seconds"). Without that, you produce discomfort and avoidance, and sophisticated audiences who
  detect the manipulation ("persuasion knowledge") push back. Do **not** aim guilt at financially
  or socially vulnerable people. (Research syntheses on guilt appeals; *Critical Fundraising*.)
- **Fear appeals** raise specific ethical duties — accuracy, not overstating threat, and always
  offering an efficacious response. Fear without efficacy backfires. (See the fear-appeal ethics
  literature, e.g., AABRI.)
- **Hope / positive ("aspirational" or "similarity-based") framing** tells stories of *gains* — the
  good that happens when someone gives or registers — and tends to preserve subjects' **dignity**,
  reduce "othering" and white-saviorism, and avoid the **"poverty porn"** trap of exploiting
  suffering for shock value. Organizations like Mama Hope built strategies on this. (PR Academy;
  *Critical Fundraising*.)
- **Compassion fatigue** is real: audiences are oversaturated with guilt/fear imagery and grow
  numb. Sustainable cause brands lean hope-forward.

**Recommended default for organ donation:** hope-forward, recipient-and-donor-family-centered,
honest about the stakes, never coercive. Frame registering as *joining people who chose to save
lives* — an act of generosity and identity, not a response to shame. Let people whose lives the
cause touched **tell their own stories** rather than speaking for them.

## Campaign types

Three campaign archetypes, each with a different success metric. A lean venture should not run all
three at once — pick the one that matches the current goal.

### 1. Awareness campaigns
Goal: reach and salience, not immediate action. Outputs: impressions, reach, new followers,
earned media, branded-search lift. **Trap:** awareness campaigns generate the most vanity metrics —
tie them to a *downstream* proxy (e.g., increase in branded search, in direct traffic, or in
registry-page visits) so you know they did more than make noise. Tentpole moments for organ
donation: **National Donate Life Month (April)** and **National Donor Day (Feb 14)** — Donate Life
America coordinates a national calendar you can ride. (Donate Life America.)

### 2. Fundraising campaigns
Goal: dollars. Classic structure: a defined goal + deadline + matching gift (if available) +
escalating email/social cadence + a thank-you/impact close. For a cause venture, fundraising pays
for the awareness and registration work; treat it as a means, not the mission. Donation-page
mechanics below apply directly.

### 3. Registrant-acquisition campaigns (this venture's core)
Goal: **net new organ/eye/tissue-donor registrations.** This is the campaign type generic nonprofit
advice underserves, so design it deliberately:

- **The ask is friction-light and free.** Registration costs the person ~90 seconds and $0. That
  *raises* conversion potential but *lowers* perceived stakes, so the job is salience plus a
  frictionless path, not overcoming a price objection.
- **Route to the right registry.** People can register via the **NC DMV**, **RegisterMe.org / the
  National Donate Life Registry**, the **iPhone Health app**, or some **MyChart** patient portals.
  (Donate Life America.) Decide your primary call-to-action and make the path one tap. (Confirm
  current NC registry routing in `venture-organ-donation-system`.)
- **Instrument the registration as a conversion.** Wherever you can, define "reached the registry
  confirmation" as a tracked conversion (needed anyway for Ad Grants — see below). You often
  *cannot* see the registration complete on a third-party DMV/registry site, which is a real
  attribution limit (see Measurement).
- **Example mini-campaign:** "April: 500 New North Carolinians Say Yes." Hope-forward recipient
  story → Ad Grants search ads on "register organ donor NC" → optimized landing page with one
  button to the NC registry → social ambassadors each pledge to recruit 10 → thank-you page asking
  registrants to share. Track: clicks to registry, ambassador-attributed pledges, branded-search
  lift. (Note the attribution caveat: counted registry *click-throughs* are a proxy for actual
  registrations.)

## Digital channels for a lean nonprofit

### Email: your highest-ROI owned channel
Email consistently out-earns and out-converts social for nonprofits because you own the list and
the deliverability. Benchmarks (M+R Benchmarks 2025, reporting on 2024 data; verify current edition):

- Average nonprofit **email open rate ≈ 28.6%** (above the for-profit ~21%). Note open rates are
  inflated/unreliable post Apple Mail Privacy Protection — treat as directional. [UNVERIFIED that
  M+R's 28.6% is privacy-adjusted]
- Average **fundraising-email click-through ≈ 0.48%**; **welcome-series CTR ≈ 1.6%** (~3x a standard
  fundraising email) — which is why a welcome series is the single best lean investment.
- Roughly **$2.63 in email revenue per subscriber** (2024). [UNVERIFIED precise figure across editions]

**Lean priorities:** (1) a 3-part welcome series triggered on opt-in; (2) a monthly impact email;
(3) campaign sends around tentpole moments. Segment minimally: at least "registered vs not" and
"donor vs not." Keep one clear CTA per email.

### Organic social & content
- **Organic reach has collapsed** to roughly 2–6% without meaningful interaction; platforms reward
  comments, shares, and video views over passive likes. Shift from *broadcasting* to *conversation*.
  (Nonprofit Tech for Good; GoodUnited.)
- Format signal (Nonprofit Tech for Good): **Instagram carousels ~0.66% engagement**, single photos
  ~0.51%, reels ~0.41% — though reels still win on *reach*. [UNVERIFIED across years]
- What works: short-form video, behind-the-scenes, supporter/recipient spotlights, educational
  carousels. Authenticity over polish.
- **Facebook** remains strong for community (Groups, Events) and has native fundraising/Donate tools
  useful for peer-to-peer.
- **Content/SEO** (blog, resource pages, FAQs) compounds over time and is what your Ad Grants search
  ads need landing pages for. General SEO craft → `venture-marketing-strategy-local-seo`. Visual
  asset production → `venture-canva-founder-brand-stack`.

## Google Ad Grants ($10k/mo in-kind search advertising)

Google Ad Grants gives eligible nonprofits **up to $10,000/month of in-kind Google Search ads**
(daily limit ~$329; funds do **not** roll over). It is one of the most valuable free assets a
small cause can claim, *especially* for registrant acquisition — you can bid on high-intent queries
like "how to register as an organ donor" and route searchers straight to a registry path.

> **Verify before relying on any rule below.** Ad Grants policies change. The authoritative sources
> are Google's own pages: the Ad Grants FAQ (`google.com/grants/faq/`) and the **Ad Grants Policy
> Compliance Guide** in Google for Nonprofits Help (`support.google.com/nonprofits/answer/9314402`).
> Figures below are **as of June 2026** and cross-checked against those pages plus secondary sources;
> treat them as a checklist to confirm, not gospel.

**Eligibility (as of 2026):**
- Hold valid **501(c)(3)** status (or the non-US equivalent), validated through Google's partner
  (Goodstack, formerly Percent/TechSoup). Confirm 501(c)(3) status work in
  `venture-nc-nonprofit-formation`.
- **Excluded:** governmental entities, hospitals/healthcare orgs, and schools/universities (these
  have separate programs). An *independent* organ-donation **awareness nonprofit** is generally
  eligible; a hospital-run program may not be. [UNVERIFIED for your exact entity — verify with Google.]
- Have a **functioning, high-quality website** that meets Google's website policy (clear mission,
  substantial content, secure/HTTPS, owned domain).
- Accept Google's required certifications (donation use, non-discrimination, ToS).

**Ongoing compliance rules you must not break.** Confirm every threshold against the official guide
before acting; the "Confidence" column flags which were confirmed against a Google page at authoring
vs. need a direct check.

| Rule | Threshold | Consequence if breached | Confidence (as of Jun 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Click-through rate | **≥ 5% CTR** each month | Below 5% for **2 consecutive months** → temporary account **deactivation** (reactivate after fixing) | Confirmed on Google's compliance guide |

| Conversion tracking | Valid tracking + **≥ 1 meaningful conversion/month** (Smart Bidding accounts) | Eligibility risk; for a registry drive, count "reached the registry / confirmation" page | Confirmed requirement |

**Practical implication:** Ad Grants is powerful but high-maintenance and capped at $2/click on
standard bidding, so it competes poorly for expensive commercial keywords. It shines on
**informational, high-intent, low-competition** queries — exactly the "how do I register as an organ
donor / organ donation myths / become a donor in North Carolina" space. Budget realistically: most
small accounts spend far less than the full $10k because of the bid cap and limited relevant search
volume. General paid-search/SEO strategy beyond the Grant → `venture-marketing-strategy-local-seo`.

## Donation- / landing-page conversion optimization

Whether the conversion is a dollar or a registry sign-up, the same principles apply. Sector
benchmarks: average **donation-page conversion ~12%** (M+R 2025), with desktop (~11%) beating mobile
(~8%) — yet most traffic is mobile, so **mobile-first is non-negotiable**. (M+R Benchmarks 2025;
iDonate.)

Evidence-based levers (DonorPerfect, Fundraise Up, iDonate — figures are vendor-reported, directional):
- **Single-step form, fully visible.** Multi-step "click Next" forms can cut completions by ~52%.
- **Minimize required fields.** Ask only for what you need *now*; collect address/phone/demographics
  later. Pre-fill where possible (city/state from ZIP).
- **For dollar pages, offer a tabbed One-Time / Monthly toggle** (recurring framing can lift giving
  ~15%) and suggest specific amounts tied to impact ("$25 = X").
- **Trust signals convert.** A padlock + "Your information is secure" reportedly lifts conversion
  ~20%. Add your nonprofit status, a recipient photo/quote, and a privacy assurance.
- **Match the page to the ad/email.** Message and imagery continuity from click to page reduces
  drop-off.

**For a registry sign-up "page":** since the actual registration usually happens on a *third-party*
site (NC DMV, RegisterMe.org), your job is a clean **bridge page** — one hope-forward story, one
giant button ("Register in North Carolina →"), trust/credibility cues, and tracking on the click.
Reduce choices to one. Note you typically *lose visibility* once the user leaves for the registry
(attribution limit — see below). Page **design/visual** execution → `venture-canva-founder-brand-stack`.

## Peer-to-peer (P2P) & ambassador fundraising

P2P turns supporters into fundraisers/recruiters who tap their own networks; it scales for a lean
team because reach grows with people, not budget. Signal of scale: America's Top 30 P2P
programs raised **$1.17B in 2025** across ~2.63M participants (Peer-to-Peer Professional Forum), and
roughly **80% of first-time donors to an org arrive through a P2P campaign** [UNVERIFIED exact %].

**Ambassador model (best fit for a cause venture):** recruit a *handful* of highly motivated
champions rather than a mass campaign. Good ambassadors: board members (built-in networks),
your most active volunteers, transplant recipients / donor families with personal stake, and
locally visible people with active social presence. (OneCause; nxunite.)

**For organ donation, reframe P2P around registrations, not dollars:** each ambassador's goal is "get
N friends to register," shareable via a personal link and social posts. Equip them with: a short
personal-story script, ready-made graphics (→ `venture-canva-founder-brand-stack`), the one-tap
registry link, and a simple tracker. Recognize and thank them publicly; recognition is the fuel.

## Partnerships (the registrant-acquisition multiplier)

For an organ-donation venture, *partnerships are often the biggest registration lever*, because they
put the ask where decisions already happen. The federal model is **HRSA's DoNation Campaign**
(formerly Workplace Partnership for Life): in 2024, **1,434 partners drove 23,104 new registrations**,
and the program has helped 600k+ register over its history (organdonor.gov). Channels:

- **DMV:** the single highest-volume registration moment in the US (license renewal). DMV
  *ambassador* programs put volunteers alongside DMV staff to prompt sign-ups. Coordinate through
  the state donation organization. (DMV.org; Donate Life.) NC specifics →
  `venture-organ-donation-system`.
- **Hospitals & OPOs:** partner with your Organ Procurement Organization (in NC: **HonorBridge**,
  **LifeShare Carolinas**) for credibility, co-branded materials, and access. Note: the *hospital*
  itself can't usually hold an Ad Grant, but it can be a partner. (Domain detail →
  `venture-organ-donation-system`.)
- **Faith communities:** educate clergy so they can answer donation questions and dispel
  religious myths; many faiths actively support donation. Faith leaders are trusted messengers,
  especially for reaching communities with historically lower registration rates. (organdonor.gov.)
- **Employers:** host registration drives, wellness-fair tables, lunch-and-learns; the DoNation
  toolkit is built for workplaces. Great for batch registrations and earned trust.

**Partnership marketing job:** make it turnkey for the partner. Supply the story, the materials, the
QR/registry link, and a simple way to count sign-ups. The partner brings the audience and trust;
you bring the conversion path.

## Impact measurement: outcomes over vanity

Measure what advances the mission, not what flatters the dashboard.

- **Vanity metrics** look impressive but don't map to the mission: raw impressions, follower counts,
  likes, open rates with no downstream link, page views with no action. (Lean Impact; Torchbox.)
- **Outcome / mission metrics** for this venture, ranked:
  1. **Registrations driven** (the North Star) — net new donor registrations attributable to your work.
  2. **Registry-page click-throughs** (proxy when you can't see the completed registration).
  3. **Email list growth + welcome-series completion** (engagement pipeline health).
  4. **Ambassador-attributed registrations** and **partner-attributed registrations**.
  5. **Branded-search lift / direct traffic** (awareness actually moved demand).
  6. **Funnel metrics** (cost per registration, conversion rate by source) — these beat raw output counts.

**Attribution is genuinely hard; say so honestly.** Common, real limitations for this venture:
- The actual registration completes on a **third-party site** (DMV, RegisterMe) you can't always
  instrument — you usually measure the *hand-off click*, not the *completed registration*. State this
  caveat whenever you report "registrations driven."
- **Signal loss**: third-party cookie decline, iOS/Apple Mail privacy, and laws like GDPR/CCPA shrink
  trackable journeys. (Braze; NonProfitPRO.)
- **Last-click over-credits the final touchpoint** and undervalues awareness and word-of-mouth, yet
  ~78% of orgs still use it. Prefer simple multi-touch reasoning + holdout/lift thinking over false
  last-click precision. Offline and word-of-mouth registrations may be effectively unattributable —
  budget for "dark" conversions rather than pretending they're zero.
- Sector reality check: only ~29% of nonprofits effectively measure outcomes — a little rigor here is
  a competitive edge. [UNVERIFIED exact %]

**Lean measurement stack:** a tracked registry-bridge page (conversion event), UTM tags on every
link, Google Analytics 4, the Ad Grants conversion column, and a one-page monthly scorecard with 5–7
mission metrics — not 30 vanity numbers.

## Ethical & health-communication guardrails

You are doing **health communication**, which raises the bar above ordinary marketing. Hold these
lines:

- **Statistical accuracy.** Never fabricate or inflate numbers. Cite a current, authoritative source
  for every statistic (HRSA/OPTN, organdonor.gov, Donate Life America) and pull the *current* figure
  from `venture-organ-donation-system` — donation/waitlist numbers change constantly (e.g., the
  waitlist was ~108,000 in late 2025; do not freeze a number in evergreen content). Round honestly;
  don't cherry-pick the scariest framing.
- **No coercion or fear-mongering.** Inform and inspire; don't manipulate. Fear/guilt only ethical
  with an efficacious, easy response and never aimed at vulnerable people. Avoid implying someone is
  a bad person for not registering.
- **Dignity, not exploitation.** Avoid "poverty-porn"-style suffering imagery. Center recipients and
  donor families as whole people; let them tell their own stories and consent to their use.
- **Cultural and religious sensitivity.** Donation hesitancy is often rooted in mistrust, religious
  questions, or historical injustice in specific communities. Address myths respectfully, use trusted
  community/faith messengers, and never stereotype. (Cultural-barrier substance →
  `venture-organ-donation-system`.)
- **Donor/supporter privacy.** Treat registrant and donor data as sensitive. Follow consent norms,
  minimize data collection, secure storage, and honor unsubscribe/opt-out immediately. Don't expose
  individuals' health or donation decisions without explicit permission. (Charitable-solicitation and
  data-handling compliance → `venture-nc-nonprofit-formation`.)
- **Don't over-promise.** You raise awareness and facilitate registration; you don't perform
  transplants or guarantee outcomes. Keep claims truthful and within scope.
- **Honest impact reporting.** Report registrations *driven* with the attribution caveat, not inflated
  hero numbers. Trust is the cause's core asset; protect it over short-term metrics.
