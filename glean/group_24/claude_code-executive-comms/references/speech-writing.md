# Speech Writing

## Overview

A speech is built to be **heard, not read**. The writer's job is to construct
sentences that survive the air between the speaker's mouth and the audience's
ear — sentences that can be re-listened-to in real time only by the listener's
short-term memory. That single constraint reshapes everything: cadence,
sentence length, repetition, signposting, and the placement of payoff lines.

This skill covers the writing craft of speeches: structure, language patterns,
applause-line placement, stage-direction annotation, the ethics of
ghostwriting, and the conventions for the most common speech types
(keynote, all-hands, conference talk, panel intro, toast, departure, eulogy,
commencement).

Delivery technique — voice, breath, body, eye contact, room work — is a
separate craft and lives in `public-speaking-and-presentations`. Audio
scripts written for microphones and editing — podcasts, broadcast spots,
explainer voiceover — live in `audio-script-writing`.

## Core concepts

### 1. The talk arc (Duarte / Gallo synthesis)

Every well-formed speech has the same five-beat skeleton:

1. **Hook** — a sentence, image, statistic, or question that earns the next
   30 seconds. Often a story fragment with a withheld payoff.
2. **Frame** — what this talk is about and why the audience should care.
   One sentence, ideally Twitter-length (Gallo: "the headline").
3. **Three acts** — the body. The rule of three is not aesthetic; it is
   cognitive. Audiences reliably remember three things, badly remember four,
   and forget five. Each act is a self-contained story or argument with its
   own micro-arc.
4. **Turn** — the moment where the talk pivots from explanation to
   implication. In Duarte's vocabulary, the contrast between "what is" and
   "what could be" sharpens.
5. **Call to action** — what the audience should *do, feel, or remember*.
   In a keynote, this is a directive. In a toast, it's a raised glass. In a
   eulogy, it's a way to carry the person forward.

### 2. The 18-minute rule

Carmine Gallo's distillation of TED's curation rule: **18 minutes is the
ceiling for a focused, memorable single-speaker talk.** Past that, cognitive
backlog sets in and retention collapses. For longer slots, structure as
multiple sub-talks with clear act breaks. For all-hands or town halls,
prefer 8–12 minutes of prepared remarks plus Q&A over 30 minutes of
monologue.

### 3. The rule of three

Three is the smallest number that creates a pattern. Use it for:
- **Top-level structure** (three acts, three stories, three asks)
- **Sentence-level lists** ("government of the people, by the people, for
  the people")
- **Repeated refrains** (Jobs: "Stay hungry. Stay foolish." — repeated three
  times across the Stanford speech)
- **Triple-builds in applause lines** ("we will not yield, we will not
  retreat, we will not break")

### 4. Applause-line placement and craft

An applause line is a sentence designed to trigger collective reaction —
applause, laughter, an audible reaction. It has four reliable shapes:

| Shape | Example pattern |
|---|---|
| **Contrast pair** | "They told us X. We chose Y." |
| **Triple build** | "Not A. Not B. C." |
| **Reversal** | "We didn't change the rules. We changed the game." |
| **Naming the moment** | "And that — that is why we are here tonight." |

Place applause lines at **act transitions** and **the CTA**. Never two in a
row — the audience hasn't recovered from the first. Mark them in the
delivery script so the speaker knows to wait through the reaction.

### 5. Speech reads differently than essay

The single most common failure mode of speech writing is writing prose and
calling it a speech. Spoken language differs from written language on
five dimensions:

| Written | Spoken |
|---|---|
| Long, subordinated sentences | Short, declarative sentences |
| Nested clauses | One idea per sentence |
| Visual punctuation (em-dash, semicolon) | Audible punctuation (pause, breath) |
| Reader sets pace | Speaker sets pace |
| Re-reading allowed | Live-only, no rewind |

Read every draft aloud. If you run out of breath, the sentence is too long.
If you stumble on a phrase, rewrite it. If a clause needs visual punctuation
to parse, it does not belong in the speech.

### 6. Stage-direction annotation craft

Stage directions are inline notes to the speaker about how to deliver a
line. They are written **for the speaker only** and live in the delivery
script, not the published transcript. Conventions:

- `[pause]` — short pause, one breath
- `[long pause]` — full silence, 2–4 seconds, used after applause lines and
  emotional beats
- `[beat]` — micro-pause, half a breath, for comic timing
- `[lower voice]` — drop volume to draw the audience in
- `[slow]` — drop pace to weight the line
- `[smile]` — facial cue, used sparingly
- `[move stage right]` / `[step toward audience]` — blocking
- `[look up]` — eye contact cue when delivering memorized line
- `[wait for laughter]` — explicitly note that the speaker should hold

Place directions on their own line *above* the line they modify, in
brackets, italicized in the final document. Never bury them mid-sentence.

### 7. Trust the audience

Amateur speeches over-explain. Professional speeches **withhold**, then
**resolve**. The audience is smarter than the writer fears. Concrete moves:

- Tell a story before naming its lesson; let the room reach the conclusion
  one beat ahead of the explicit statement.
- Use specific detail, not abstraction. "A blue notebook on the kitchen
  table" beats "a personal object."
- Cut your second-best example. Two strong points land harder than three
  uneven ones.
- Don't apologize for the topic, the time slot, or the room.

### 8. Ghostwriting ethics

When writing a speech for another person:

- **Capture their voice.** Listen to 30+ minutes of their existing speech
  before drafting. Note sentence length, favorite words, recurring images,
  cadence. Their speech should sound like them on their best day, not like
  the writer.
- **Their stories, not yours.** Use anecdotes from their life. If they have
  no story for a point, find a different point.
- **No misattributed lived experience.** Never invent a personal anecdote
  the speaker did not actually experience.
- **They get the credit.** Ghostwriters do not publicly claim authorship of
  delivered speeches unless the speaker consents.
- **They get final cut.** The speaker must be able to change anything. If
  they would not say it in their own voice, it stays out.

### 9. Type-specific conventions

| Type | Length | Tone | Open | Close |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Keynote** | 18–45 min | Authoritative | Story or stat hook | Call to action |
| **All-hands** | 8–12 min | Direct, warm | Headline / why we're here | What's next + Q&A |
| **Conference talk** | 18–25 min | Expert, specific | Concrete example | One thing to take home |
| **Panel intro** | 60–90 sec | Brisk, generous | Premise of the panel | Name + 1-line bio each |
| **Toast** | 60–180 sec | Warm, specific | Concrete moment | Raised glass + 1 line |
| **Departure** | 3–6 min | Grateful, forward-looking | Gratitude, not grievance | What's next |
| **Eulogy** | 5–8 min | Loving, honest | Specific memory | Carry them forward |
| **Commencement** | 12–18 min | Wise, hopeful | Hook + admission of imposter feeling | 3-word refrain |

### 10. The cut-and-rehearse loop

A speech is finished only after it has survived being read aloud three
times — once on paper, once timed, once on the actual stage or its
substitute. Each pass cuts 10–15%. Final length is almost always shorter
than first draft. The phrase "kill your darlings" applies double in speech:
favorite written lines often die on contact with the spoken voice.

## Templates and examples

### Template 1 — Talk skeleton (18-minute keynote)

```
[TITLE]
[SPEAKER NAME]
[VENUE / DATE]
Target runtime: 18 min

────────────────────────────────────────
HOOK (0:00 – 1:00)
────────────────────────────────────────
[step to center stage]
[look up, scan audience for 2 beats]

  Twelve years ago, I was sitting in a parking lot at 2 a.m.,
  trying to figure out how to fire myself.

[pause]

  This is a talk about the thing I learned that night.

────────────────────────────────────────
FRAME (1:00 – 2:00)
────────────────────────────────────────

  The headline — one sentence, the whole talk in twelve words.

  Tonight I want to share three ideas: [A], [B], and [C].

────────────────────────────────────────
ACT 1 — [A] (2:00 – 6:30)
────────────────────────────────────────
Story → claim → evidence → micro-CTA

  [Story opens with concrete detail.]
  [Claim emerges.]
  [Evidence: one stat, one example.]
  [Land the act with a contrast pair.]

[long pause — wait for the room]

────────────────────────────────────────
ACT 2 — [B] (6:30 – 11:00)
────────────────────────────────────────
[same pattern]

────────────────────────────────────────
ACT 3 — [C] (11:00 – 15:00)
────────────────────────────────────────
[same pattern]

────────────────────────────────────────
TURN (15:00 – 16:00)
────────────────────────────────────────
[step toward audience]
[lower voice]

  Here is what these three things have in common.
  [The synthesis sentence — the line that ties them.]

────────────────────────────────────────
CALL TO ACTION (16:00 – 18:00)
────────────────────────────────────────
[slow]

  So when you leave this room tonight, do one thing:
  [the directive, in one short imperative sentence].

[pause]

  Thank you.

[hold the gaze, then exit stage right]
```

### Template 2 — Toast (90 seconds)

```
[raise glass, wait for the room to quiet]

  [Specific concrete moment — "the night we drove to Reno…"]

[brief story, 30 seconds, with one funny beat]

  And that's the thing about [name]:
  [the trait the story reveals, in plain words].

[pause]

  To [name]. [One-sentence wish or honor.]

[clink]
```

### Template 3 — Panel intro (75 seconds)

```
[brisk, warm]

  Tonight's panel is about [premise — one sentence].
  It matters because [stakes — one sentence].

  Joining us are three people who have spent their careers
  on different sides of this question.

  First — [Name], [one-line credential + one specific accomplishment].
  Second — [Name], [same].
  Third — [Name], [same].

[turn to panel]

  Welcome.
```

### Example — Applause-line shapes

```
CONTRAST PAIR
  "They told us this was impossible.
   We built it anyway."

TRIPLE BUILD
  "Not because it was easy.
   Not because it was cheap.
   Because it was right."

REVERSAL
  "We didn't fix the system.
   We replaced it."

NAMING THE MOMENT
  "And that — right there —
   is the question we have to answer
   before any of us go home tonight."
```

## Anti-patterns

- **The written-essay-read-aloud.** Long sentences, semicolons, nested
  clauses. The audience falls behind by sentence three.
- **The acronym dump.** Every acronym needs to be unpacked the first time.
  Spoken language has no glossary.
- **The five-point list.** Audiences forget points four and five. Cut to
  three.
- **The unearned emotion.** Asking the room to feel something the speech
  has not built. The audience must be walked there.
- **The apology open.** "I'm not really a speaker…" / "Sorry the slides
  aren't working…" The opening 30 seconds are the only ones guaranteed to
  land — do not spend them apologizing.
- **The unmotivated joke.** Humor that doesn't serve the arc. If the joke
  dies, the whole act dies with it.
- **The buried CTA.** Telling the audience what to do in the third-to-last
  sentence, then drifting into "thank you" boilerplate. Land the CTA last.
- **Reading the slides.** If a slide and the script say the same thing, cut
  one.
- **Ghostwriter voice intrusion.** The speaker uses the word "leverage" in
  the third paragraph and has never said "leverage" in their life. Caught
  at delivery, painful to repair.
- **Stage directions in the published transcript.** `[pause]` belongs in
  the speaker's delivery copy, not the version posted to the company blog.

## Decision heuristics

- **Is it shorter than 18 minutes?** If no, split into two talks or cut.
- **Can the speaker read any sentence in one breath?** If no, rewrite.
- **Does each act end on a payoff line?** If no, add one.
- **Is the CTA in the last 30 seconds?** If no, move it.
- **Have you read it aloud three times?** If no, you are not done.
- **Would the speaker say "leverage / synergy / utilize" in real life?** If
  no, replace with "use" or "fit together" or cut.
- **Is the rule of three respected at the structural level?** If you have
  four acts, you have two talks.
- **Are stage directions on their own line, in brackets?** If no, fix the
  formatting before handing the script over.

## References

1. Nancy Duarte, *Resonate: Present Visual Stories that Transform Audiences*
   (Wiley, 2010). The contrast-with-what-is / what-could-be model, the
   audience-as-hero construct, and the Sparkline visual structure for
   presentations. Duarte teaches an adapted version of this material in the
   Stanford GSB course of the same name.
2. Carmine Gallo, *Talk Like TED: The 9 Public Speaking Secrets of the
   World's Top Minds* (St. Martin's Press, 2014). Source of the 18-minute
   rule, the headline / three-message structure, and the Twitter-friendly
   distillation discipline.
3. Steve Jobs, *Stanford Commencement Address*, June 12, 2005. The
   canonical short-form three-stories-and-a-refrain talk. Worth studying
   live, with stopwatch and transcript side-by-side, for pacing and the
   "stay hungry, stay foolish" three-fold close.
4. Sam Leith, *Words Like Loaded Pistols: Rhetoric from Aristotle to Obama*
   (Basic Books, 2012). For rhetorical figures — anaphora, tricolon,
   antithesis, chiasmus — that power applause lines and refrains.
5. Toastmasters International, *Speech Evaluation Guide*. Practical rubric
   for evaluating opening, body, conclusion, vocal variety, language,
   and audience engagement. Useful as a self-edit checklist after the
   second read-aloud pass.
