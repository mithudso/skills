# NC Open File Discovery and Brady/Giglio Obligations

**Educational only, NOT legal advice.** Consult a licensed NC attorney for specific matters.

## Overview

North Carolina uses one of the broadest criminal discovery systems in the country. The Open File statute (G.S. 15A-903, substantially revised in 2004) requires the State to provide the defense with its entire investigative file — not just what the prosecutor considers relevant. This statutory right operates alongside and supplements constitutional Brady/Giglio obligations.

## G.S. 15A-903 — Open File Discovery

### What the State Must Disclose

Upon the defendant's motion, courts must order the State to produce:

**Complete Agency Files** — The entire files of all law enforcement agencies, investigatory agencies, and prosecutor's offices involved in:
- Investigation of the alleged crimes
- Prosecution of the defendant

**File contents include:**
- Defendant's statements
- Co-defendant statements
- Witness statements (oral statements must be documented in writing or recorded, unless they add no significantly new or different information from prior statements)
- Investigating officers' notes
- Results of tests and examinations (including preliminary screening results and bench notes, not just final reports)
- Any other matter or evidence obtained during investigation

**Expert Witnesses (subsection a(2)):** Prosecutors must provide reasonable advance notice and furnish: test/examination reports, CV, expert opinions, and the underlying basis for those opinions.

**Witness Lists (subsection a(3)):** At jury selection, the State must provide written names of expected trial witnesses. Exception: witnesses who face physical harm, substantial economic harm, or other particularized compelling need may be withheld.

### Agency Obligations

Law enforcement and investigatory agencies must supply complete files to the prosecutor on a timely basis for disclosure to the defense. The duty runs from agencies to the prosecutor, and then from the prosecutor to the defendant.

### Defense Enforcement Rights

- Defendants may inspect, copy, and photograph all materials
- Physical evidence may be examined under appropriate safeguards
- Courts have broad authority to order compliance and fashion remedies

### Penalties for Non-Compliance

| Violation | Classification |
|---|---|
| Willful omission or misrepresentation of required evidence (G.S. 15A-903(a)(1) or (c)) | Class H felony |
| Willful omission or misrepresentation under other provisions of the section | Class 1 misdemeanor |

### Scope Limits

- G.S. 15A-903 applies primarily to **superior court** cases (trial-level felonies); district court discovery is more limited
- The statute requires defendant's **motion** to trigger formal disclosure; in practice many prosecutors comply informally
- Oral statements made to prosecutors outside law enforcement presence need not be recorded unless they contain materially new information

## Brady v. Maryland — Constitutional Floor

**Brady v. Maryland**, 373 U.S. 83 (1963): Due process requires the prosecution to disclose evidence **favorable to the accused** when that evidence is **material** to guilt or punishment.

### Materiality Standard

Evidence is material if there is a **"reasonable probability" of a different outcome** had the evidence been disclosed. "Reasonable probability" means the probability is sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict — not just that it might have helped the defense.

**Timing:** Brady applies both pre-trial and at trial. The Supreme Court has held Brady does not require disclosure of impeachment evidence **before a guilty plea** (United States v. Ruiz, 536 U.S. 622 (2002)), but NC Rule of Professional Conduct 3.8(d) imposes a broader ethical duty regardless of plea timing.

### What Qualifies as Brady Material

- Exculpatory evidence (tends to negate guilt)
- Evidence reducing the severity of the offense
- Evidence impeaching the credibility of prosecution witnesses
- Evidence bearing on punishment
- Evidence of third-party guilt

Brady material includes evidence in the possession of **any member of the prosecution team** — police, investigators, forensic labs — not just the prosecutor's office.

### NC Constitutional Dimension

NC courts recognize that defendants also have discovery rights under Article I, § 19 of the NC Constitution (law of the land clause), providing an independent state-law basis for disclosure claims.

## Giglio v. United States — Impeachment Evidence

**Giglio v. United States**, 405 U.S. 150 (1972): Brady's obligation extends to evidence the defense could use to **impeach prosecution witnesses** — evidence that undermines witness credibility.

### Brady vs. Giglio — Key Distinction

| | Brady | Giglio |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | Exculpatory evidence | Impeachment evidence |
| Constitutional basis | Due Process | Due Process |
| Materiality | Same "reasonable probability" standard | Same |
| Relationship | Giglio is a subset of Brady | All Giglio is Brady; not all Brady is Giglio |

Under **United States v. Bagley** (1985), there is no legal distinction between exculpatory and impeachment evidence for materiality purposes — both must be disclosed.

### Types of Giglio Material

Prosecutors must disclose information about witnesses — especially law enforcement witnesses — that could undermine their credibility:

- Criminal history of prosecution witnesses
- Prior acts of dishonesty or untruthfulness
- **Law enforcement misconduct** — false reports, improper conduct, prior disciplinary actions
- Bias or motive to lie (pending charges against cooperating witnesses, payment arrangements, promises of leniency)
- Prior inconsistent statements
- Cooperation agreements with witnesses
- Parole/probation status that creates motive to cooperate
- Racial, personal, or financial bias against the defendant
- Mental health issues affecting reliability

**NC practice note:** Defense attorneys can look back **10 years** when requesting Giglio material on law enforcement witnesses.

### Confidential Informant Files

CI files contain rich Giglio material: reliability history, payment records, pending charges, prior cooperation agreements. Defense strategies for CI file disclosure:

1. **Request in camera review** by the court under G.S. 15A-908 to determine materiality while protecting CI identity
2. **Motion to reveal identity** when the CI's role in the offense is direct or testimonial
3. **Invoke Brady/Giglio directly** — argue the reliability, payment history, and incentives are material
4. **Cite Rule 3.8(d)** — NC's ethical rule requires "reasonably diligent inquiry" and timely disclosure broader than the constitutional minimum

## NC Rule of Professional Conduct 3.8(d)

NC RPC 3.8(d) requires prosecutors to make **timely disclosure** of all evidence known to the prosecutor that **tends to negate guilt or mitigate the offense** — a duty broader than Brady in two important ways:

1. **No materiality filter:** the ethical duty applies to any evidence tending to negate guilt, even if a court might later find it immaterial under Brady
2. **Timing:** the ethical duty to disclose applies throughout the case, including before pleas — unlike the Brady constitutional minimum under Ruiz

## Remedies for Discovery Violations

When the State fails to comply with open file or Brady/Giglio obligations, courts have authority to impose sanctions under G.S. 15A-910:

| Remedy | When Used |
|---|---|
| Order to comply | First response to non-disclosure |
| Exclusion of evidence | Prevent State from using undisclosed evidence |
| Continuance | Allow defense time to use belatedly disclosed material |
| Mistrial | When prejudice cannot be cured by lesser remedy |
| Dismissal | Willful, bad-faith suppression causing severe prejudice |
| New trial (on appeal) | Post-conviction Brady violations meeting materiality |

**Materiality on appeal:** to prevail on a Brady claim on appeal, the defendant must show: (1) the evidence was favorable; (2) the State suppressed it; and (3) prejudice resulted — i.e., the evidence was material. The defendant does not need to show bad faith by the prosecutor.

## Defense Strategy — Open File and Brady/Giglio

### At Discovery Stage
- File a motion for discovery early to trigger G.S. 15A-903 obligations
- Request the complete files of all law enforcement agencies involved
- Specifically request Brady/Giglio material in a written discovery request — creating a clear record for later appellate argument
- Request CI files and move for in camera review if the State resists
- Request the State identify all expert witnesses and provide reports with bench notes

### Reviewing the File
- Look for inconsistencies between officer notes and final reports
- Check for preliminary test results that differ from final conclusions
- Identify all witnesses and check for Giglio material on each
- Compare witness statements for prior inconsistencies
- Request personnel files of law enforcement witnesses with discipline history (Giglio obligation)

### Litigating Violations
- Move to suppress or exclude improperly withheld evidence
- Request a continuance to develop belatedly disclosed material
- Preserve the record meticulously for appellate Brady claims
- Consider Rule 3.8(d) complaint to State Bar for prosecutorial ethics violations
