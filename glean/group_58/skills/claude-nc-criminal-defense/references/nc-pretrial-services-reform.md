# NC Pretrial Services Programs & Cash Bail Reform

> **Educational only, NOT legal advice.** verified-as-of: 2026-06-22

North Carolina's pretrial justice landscape combines a statutory framework that nominally favors non-financial release conditions with a practice reality dominated by secured bonds. County-level pretrial services programs, risk assessment tools, and ongoing reform debates shape how defendants are treated between arrest and trial in NC's 100 counties.

---

## 1. Statutory Framework for Pretrial Policies (GS 15A-535)

GS 15A-535 authorizes each **judicial district** to adopt written pretrial release policies, which must:
- Be issued by the senior resident superior court judge
- Govern how magistrates and judges set conditions in that district
- Be publicly available

**Key statutory preference:** NC law (GS 15A-534) expresses a preference for **non-financial conditions** (written promise, unsecured bond, custody release) over financial conditions (secured bond) for non-violent offenses: but in practice, secured bonds remain the most common condition imposed across NC.

**Effect of local policies:** Local district policies can establish bond schedules, pretrial release presumptions, and supervision referral protocols. They cannot override the statutory release framework but operationalize it.

## 2. NC Pretrial Services Programs — Overview

As of December 2024: [^1]
- **37 of NC's 100 counties** offer some form of pretrial supervision
- Coverage is concentrated in urban and mid-size counties; most rural counties have no formal program
- Programs vary significantly in scope: some offer only risk assessment; others provide supervision, case management, drug testing, and court reminders

### What Pretrial Services Do

| Function | Description |
|---|---|
| **Risk assessment** | Administer a validated tool to score defendant's risk of FTA and new criminal activity |
| **Recommendation to court** | Provide a written recommendation to the judicial official on appropriate release conditions |
| **Supervision** | Monitor defendants released with supervision conditions (check-ins, drug testing, curfews) |
| **Court reminders** | Contact defendants before court dates to reduce FTA rates |
| **Case management** | Connect defendants with social services, housing, treatment |

### Notable County Programs

- **Mecklenburg County:** Uses the Arnold PSA as the actuarial pretrial assessment tool; provides recommendations at first appearance
- **Durham County:** Implemented a locally designed PSA following the Arnold Venture PSA model
- **Orange County:** Uses PSA; provides recommendations to judges on release conditions
- **Judicial Districts 2 and 21:** Have undertaken systematic pretrial reform, tracked by UNC SOG's Criminal Justice Innovation Lab (CJIL)

## 3. Pretrial Risk Assessment Tools in NC

### Arnold Foundation Public Safety Assessment (PSA)

The **PSA** (developed by the Laura and John Arnold Foundation, now Arnold Ventures) is the most widely adopted validated tool in NC: [^1]

**How it works:**
- Does **not** require a defendant interview — all data drawn from court records and criminal history databases
- Scores defendants on two dimensions:
  - **Failure to Appear (FTA) risk** (scale 1–6)
  - **New Criminal Activity (NCA) risk** (scale 1–6)
  - **New Violent Criminal Activity (NVCA) flag** (yes/no)
- Produces a recommendation grid: release, release with conditions, or detention review

**Adoption:** 6 NC counties reported using the PSA as of 2024 [^1]

**Key advantage over interview-based tools:** Removes interviewer bias; faster at first appearance; does not disadvantage defendants who invoke right to silence.

### Virginia Pretrial Risk Assessment Instrument (VPRAI)

An older interview-based tool used by some NC counties before PSA adoption:
- Requires a brief interview with the defendant
- Scores 8 risk factors including current charge, criminal history, employment, and substance use
- Some NC counties using VPRAI were actively planning to switch to the PSA as of 2024

### Other Approaches

Some NC counties use locally developed risk-screening instruments or no formal tool, relying on judicial experience and the factors in GS 15A-534(c).

### Validation and Accuracy Concerns

Risk assessment tools in pretrial settings face ongoing critique:
- **Racial bias concerns:** Tools trained on historical data may perpetuate systemic disparities if underlying arrest and conviction patterns reflect racial bias
- **Predictive limitations:** No tool perfectly predicts individual behavior; risk categories reflect group probabilities
- **Transparency issues:** Some tools are proprietary; defendants may not know their score or the factors used
- **Use as presumption vs. recommendation:** Tools are most appropriate as one input among many, not as determinative of release decisions

## 4. Electronic Monitoring as a Pretrial Condition

Electronic monitoring (EM) is authorized as a pretrial release condition under **GS 15A-531** (house arrest with electronic monitoring) and can be imposed as part of a secured bond. [^2]

### Types

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **GPS ankle monitor** | Tracks real-time location; alerts for zone violations |
| **Radio frequency (RF)** | Detects presence/absence at a fixed location (home); no location tracking |
| **Alcohol monitoring (SCRAM)** | Continuous alcohol monitoring via transdermal sensor |

### Who Supervises

- Department of Adult Correction (DAC) in many counties
- County sheriff's office in some jurisdictions
- Private vendors under contract

### Post-Iryna's Law Role

Iryna's Law (SL 2025-93) elevated house arrest with electronic monitoring as a **mandatory** release condition (with secured bond) for repeat violent offense defendants who cannot be held. This represents a significant expansion of EM use in NC pretrial.

### Practical Considerations for Defendants

- EM is financially burdensome: defendants typically pay daily monitoring fees ($5–$15/day is common)
- Equipment malfunctions can generate false alerts and trigger revocation hearings
- Charging requirements, zone restrictions, and check-in obligations must be strictly followed
- Inability to pay EM fees is not itself a valid basis to revoke pretrial release, though courts vary in practice

## 5. Cash Bail Reform in NC — Context and Debates

### The Reform Argument

Reform advocates (including NC Indigent Defense Services and bail reform coalitions) argue:

1. **Wealth-based detention:** Whether a defendant is held pretrial often reflects financial ability, not risk: two defendants with identical risk profiles may have different outcomes based solely on ability to pay
2. **Presumption of innocence:** Pretrial detention punishes people before conviction; median pretrial detention in NC for those unable to post bond can extend months
3. **Collateral consequences:** Pretrial detention causes job loss, housing instability, and family disruption: which themselves increase recidivism risk
4. **Statutory intent violated:** NC law nominally prefers non-financial release, but in practice secured bonds dominate

### Racial Disparities in NC Bail

- **Black defendants are significantly overrepresented** in the pretrial detainee population nationally and in NC
- Research shows Black defendants receive higher bond amounts and are more likely to be assigned financial conditions than white defendants with comparable charges and histories [^3]
- Racial wealth gaps mean identical bond amounts have disparate impacts
- NC currently lacks a comprehensive statewide pretrial data system, making precise NC-specific disparity measurement difficult — a recognized gap

### The Counter-Argument (and Legislative Direction)

NC's 2025 legislative direction under Iryna's Law moved in the **opposite direction from reform** for violent offenses:
- Eliminated written promises to appear (the most accessible release form)
- Mandated secured bonds or house arrest for violent offense defendants
- Expanded judge-only review (limiting magistrate access to expedited release)
- Reflects public and legislative concern that reform-era releases contributed to reoffending

### Current Landscape (2025)

NC finds itself at an inflection point:
- Progressive judicial districts (Durham, Orange) have implemented risk-assessment-based reforms
- Iryna's Law represents a statewide legislative counter-movement for violent offenses
- Reform advocates focus on data collection, pretrial services expansion, and local policy reform within the existing statutory framework
- Statewide cash bail elimination (as pursued in some states) has no current legislative momentum in NC

### National Comparison

| Jurisdiction | Approach |
|---|---|
| New Jersey | Eliminated cash bail (2017); replaced with risk-based framework |
| California | SB 10 (2018) eliminated cash bail but was overturned by referendum |
| Illinois | Eliminated cash bail (2023) under Pretrial Fairness Act |
| NC | Mixed: local reforms in some districts; Iryna's Law tightens violent-offense bail statewide |

## Anti-Patterns / Common Errors

- **Conflating "pretrial services" with "pretrial supervision":** Risk assessment (producing a recommendation) is distinct from ongoing supervision (check-ins, monitoring) — not all programs offer both
- **Treating PSA scores as determinative:** The PSA produces a recommendation, not a ruling — judges retain full discretion to deviate, and often do
- **Assuming all NC counties have pretrial services:** 63 of NC's 100 counties have no formal pretrial services program as of 2024
- **Missing EM fee burden as a rights issue:** Courts in some jurisdictions have found that inability to pay EM fees cannot itself revoke pretrial release without a separate basis
- **Treating Iryna's Law as "bail reform":** Iryna's Law moved NC in the opposite direction from cash bail reform for violent offenses — framing matters for accurate analysis
