# Social Marketing & Community-Based Social Marketing (CBSM)

> **Spoke of the `applied-psychology` hub** (installed as a standalone skill).

Two tightly related frameworks for campaigns that aim to change *what people actually do* — not just what they know or believe. Social marketing (Andreasen; Kotler & Lee) applies commercial-marketing discipline to public-good behavior objectives. CBSM (McKenzie-Mohr) builds on that with a five-step community program model grounded in behavioral science.

The shared throughline: **behavior change is the product**. Awareness may be a necessary first step, but it is rarely sufficient — and the evidence for awareness-only campaigns producing durable behavior change is weak.

---

## Decision table — which tool fits the situation

| Situation | Primary tool | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Campaign doesn't target a specific behavior | Andreasen benchmark 1: behavior objective | Reframe the goal as a single, observable, measurable behavior |
| Don't know why the audience isn't acting | CBSM Step 2: barrier/benefit research | Run focus groups + surveys before designing the strategy |
| Audience is diverse; one message isn't landing | Andreasen benchmark 3: segmentation | Segment by stage, motivation, or barrier profile; tailor asks |
| The new behavior is easy to intend but forget | CBSM tool: prompts | Place point-of-decision cues where and when the behavior occurs |
| People say they support the cause but don't act | CBSM tool: commitment | Elicit a small, written, public commitment to the specific act |
| People don't know what others are doing | CBSM tool: social norms | Communicate descriptive norms ("Most people in your area have registered") |
| Structural friction is blocking behavior | CBSM tool: convenience | Remove external barriers before addressing motivation |
| Campaign focuses only on individuals | Upstream/midstream distinction | Consider also influencing gatekeepers and policy/environment |
| Piloting vs. scaling | CBSM Steps 4–5 | Pilot with control group, measure, refine, then scale |

## Part 1 — Social Marketing: the benchmark criteria

### What social marketing is (and isn't)

Social marketing is the application of commercial-marketing principles and techniques to influence voluntary behavior of target audiences in order to improve their personal welfare and that of society. It is NOT:

- **Advertising/awareness campaigns** — which target attitudes, not behavior
- **Health communications** — which primarily disseminate information
- **Social media marketing** — the term "social" refers to social benefit, not the medium
- **Propaganda** — which serves the sender's interests, not the audience's

The field was named and shaped by Kotler & Zaltman (1971), systematized by Andreasen (1995, 2002), and elaborated by Kotler & Lee (2008). The NSMC (National Social Marketing Centre, UK) has been the primary practitioner body since 2006.

### Andreasen's six benchmark criteria (2002)

In 2002, Alan Andreasen proposed six criteria that define a genuine social marketing intervention — as opposed to an information campaign using the label. French & Blair-Stevens (2006) subsequently refined and expanded these benchmarks; the combined criteria remain the field's standard.

| Benchmark | What it requires | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Behavior objective** | The program has a clear, specific, observable behavior as its goal — not awareness, attitude, or intention | Goal is "raise awareness of organ donation" rather than "increase donor registrations at DMV" |
| **2. Consumer research** | Formative research (audience insight) shapes design; ongoing research monitors progress | Campaign designed by experts without consulting the target audience |
| **3. Segmentation** | Target audience is defined, and different segments may receive different strategies | One-size-fits-all messaging for heterogeneous populations |
| **4. Exchange / value** | Program offers something the audience values in return for the behavior; perceived benefits ≥ perceived costs | Focus on societal benefit without addressing "what's in it for me?" |
| **5. Competition** | Program accounts for behaviors that compete with the desired action (alternatives, status quo, habits) | Ignores that the competing behavior (inertia, opting out) has its own perceived benefits |
| **6. Marketing mix (4 Ps)** | All four elements addressed: **Product** (the behavior and its benefits), **Price** (barriers and costs), **Place** (access, convenience), **Promotion** (communication) | Promotion-only campaigns that do nothing about price or place |

**Replication note.** These criteria are practitioner-derived and taxonomy-grade, not the result of RCTs. Their value is as a *checklist* that reliably surfaces the most common failure modes of social-change campaigns.

### The 4 Ps applied to behavior (not product)

| P | In commercial marketing | In social marketing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Product** | Physical good or service | The desired behavior and the benefits it delivers; may include tangible goods that enable it (e.g., pledge cards, registration kiosks) |
| **Price** | Monetary cost | All costs the audience perceives: time, effort, embarrassment, habit disruption, social friction — not just money |
| **Place** | Distribution channel | Where and when the audience can perform the behavior; access points, convenience, point-of-decision proximity |
| **Promotion** | Advertising, PR | Messages, channels, and messengers that communicate value and remove informational barriers; the *last* P, not the first |

### Downstream, midstream, and upstream

Andreasen (2006) distinguished three intervention levels; the field now uses a three-tier model:

| Level | Target | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Downstream** | Individual citizens | Persuade a person to register as an organ donor |
| **Midstream** | Influencers and gatekeepers (organizations, health workers, schools, families) | Train hospital staff to make effective donor conversations; engage faith leaders |
| **Upstream** | Policy makers, legislators, institutions | Advocate for opt-out (presumed consent) donation law; require registration at DMV renewal |

A systematic review of social marketing approaches to nutrition and physical activity in early-care settings (PMC5702552) found that **multi-level approaches outperform single-level ones**. Downstream-only campaigns put the entire burden on individual motivation; upstream changes restructure the environment. Best practice for organ donation programs combines all three.

## Part 2 — Community-Based Social Marketing (CBSM)

Developed by Doug McKenzie-Mohr (psychologist, Carleton University), CBSM draws on commitment research (Pallak, Cialdini), social norms literature (Schultz, Goldstein), and environmental psychology to build a step-by-step community program model. Core text: *Fostering Sustainable Behavior: An Introduction to Community-Based Social Marketing* (1st ed. 1999; now in 4th ed.). The model was developed primarily for pro-environmental behavior but has been applied to health, safety, and civic behaviors.

### The five CBSM steps

**Step 1 — Select the behavior**

Identify a specific, observable, end-state behavior — not a general goal like "be more sustainable" or "think about donating." Prioritize by:
- Impact: how much difference does widespread adoption make?
- Penetration: how many people currently do it?
- Probability: how likely is adoption given current barriers?

Organ-donation application: "Enroll in the state donor registry online before leaving this event" is a CBSM-ready behavior. "Support organ donation" is not.

**Step 2 — Identify barriers and benefits**

Use a combination of:
- Literature review of what is already known
- Focus groups (to surface qualitative barriers)
- Surveys (to quantify barrier prevalence across segments)
- Observational research (to identify situational/structural barriers)

Barriers may be **internal** (lack of knowledge, low perceived efficacy, competing norms, fear of family conflict) or **external** (no registration kiosk, registration form is confusing, no prompt at point of decision). External/structural barriers should be removed before motivational appeals are designed — motivation cannot compensate for a broken registration pathway.

Benefits research is equally important: which benefits resonate with each segment? ("Helping my family feel my wishes are known" outperforms "saving lives" in some audience segments for organ donation.)

**Step 3 — Develop the strategy (select and combine tools)**

Strategy is built from the barrier/benefit research. The six primary CBSM tools:

| Tool | Mechanism | Effect-size evidence | Organ-donation application |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Commitment** | Public, written, or pledged commitments to a specific behavior increase follow-through via cognitive-consistency pressure (foot-in-the-door). *Mechanism theory → applied-psychology (references/persuasion-and-influence-psychology.md)* | Medium to large for pro-environmental behaviors (Lokhorst et al. 2013 meta-analysis: d ≈ 0.46 for commitment interventions on energy/water behavior) | Pledge cards at events; "click here to commit to talking with your family"; two-stage online pledge → registration flow |
| **Social norms** | Descriptive norms ("most people in your area have registered") and injunctive norms ("your community expects..."); effectiveness depends on delivery being personal and credible. *Norm persuasion theory → applied-psychology (references/persuasion-and-influence-psychology.md)* | Significant for conservation (Schultz et al. 2007 utility-bill norms: 5.7% reduction in high users); reciprocity + norm messages produced ~1,171 additional donor registrations vs control prompt alone (Robitaille et al. 2021, N ≈ 1M) | "X% of your county has registered — join them"; normative commitment (moral obligation framing) strongly predicts registration intention |
| **Prompts** | Environmental cues placed at the point of decision; should be noticeable, proximal, and self-explanatory | Moderate; effectiveness depends on proximity and salience | QR code at DMV window; sticker on driver's license renewal envelope; in-app prompt at government portal login |
| **Incentives** | Rewards and penalties that shift perceived price; must be weighed against risk of crowding out intrinsic motivation (overjustification) | Variable; most effective when removing cost rather than adding reward; avoid expected, controlling tangible rewards for behaviors with intrinsic value | Remove friction (waive DMV fee for adding donor designation); recognition rather than cash incentives |
| **Communication** | Targeted messages that address the specific barriers of the specific audience segment; two-way channels and feedback | Most effective when paired with other tools; information alone rarely sufficient | Personal contact, social worker conversations, peer-to-peer outreach; feedback on registration campaign progress |
| **Convenience** | Structurally removing external barriers (access, time, effort, pathway complexity) | Often the highest-leverage tool when structural barriers are the primary barrier | Online registration in <2 minutes; kiosk at DMV; adding donation to existing government interactions |

Most effective CBSM programs combine **three to four tools** matched to the primary barriers identified in Step 2. Using a tool without first identifying which barrier it addresses is a common implementation failure.

**Step 4 — Pilot**

Test the intervention with a control group and an intervention group before broad rollout. Measure the target behavior (not just awareness or intention). Use the pilot to:
- Confirm the strategy addresses the actual barriers
- Identify unintended negative effects (e.g., boomerang norms effects when a descriptive norm is *lower* than expected)
- Calibrate effect sizes for cost-benefit projection

**Step 5 — Broad-scale implementation and evaluation**

Scale the piloted, refined program. Ongoing evaluation should:
- Track the target behavior (registrations, not clicks or survey agreement)
- Report results transparently — including null or negative findings
- Celebrate achievements in ways that reinforce community identity and commitment
- Feed findings back into barrier research for the next program cycle

## Part 3 — Why awareness ≠ behavior

This is the most important empirical finding in the field, and the primary reason social marketing exists as a discipline distinct from public communications.

### The evidence

- A 2025 scoping review of social media mental health campaigns (Teng et al., PMC12144482) found stigma and attitude change in 45% of campaigns, but **behavior change in only 13%** — despite high campaign awareness.
- Security awareness programs reliably improve self-reported knowledge yet produce weak behavioral transfer when employees face real phishing attempts — a practitioner-documented gap (Outthink, 2024) consistent with the broader intention-action literature.
- Climate communication research documents high declared concern across populations alongside persistently low rates of pro-environmental behavior adoption — a canonical illustration of the attitude-behavior gap (see awareness-action-gap literature, UNU OurWorld).
- Sheeran's (2002) meta-analysis of 422 studies (N = 82,107) found that a medium-to-large change in intention produces only a small-to-medium change in behavior — **attitude-behavior gaps persist** even when information campaigns successfully shift intentions.

### Why the gap exists

The gap is structural, not a failure of message design:

1. **Behavior is shaped by context, not just cognition.** Habits, environmental cues, social norms, and structural barriers determine behavior more than individual beliefs. *(For social-norms and habit mechanism theory, see persuasion-and-influence-psychology and behavior-change-psychology.)*
2. **Intention ≠ action.** The intention-action gap is one of the most replicated findings in social psychology. People who intend to do something fail to follow through at high rates without a specific plan, a prompt, or a structural change.
3. **Awareness campaigns don't address price, place, or competition.** Even if someone learns the value of registering as a donor, a confusing registration system (high price), no accessible kiosk (poor place), and the status-quo habit (competition) block the behavior.
4. **Awareness can create an illusion of action.** Learning about an issue may satisfy the psychological need to "do something" without producing any behavioral output (moral licensing).

### The social marketing corrective

Social marketing reframes: instead of "how do we inform the audience," ask "what specific behavior do we want, what stops people from doing it, and what would make it easy, normal, and rewarding?" The Andreasen benchmarks and CBSM five steps are both operationalizations of this reframe.

## Part 4 — Worked application: organ-donor registration drives

Organ donation is a well-studied CBSM application domain. McKenzie-Mohr's cbsm.com lists it as a case-study category; Robitaille et al. (2021) ran a field experiment across 1 million road taxpayers.

### Applying the five CBSM steps

**Step 1 — Behavior.** "Complete donor registration in the state/provincial registry" — specific, binary, measurable. Not "support donation" or "learn about donation."

**Step 2 — Barriers and benefits research.** Common barriers found in research:
- **Internal:** Superstitions about death; fear that declaring as a donor affects medical care; family conflict concerns; low perceived efficacy ("one person doesn't matter"); procrastination
- **External:** Registration process unclear or multi-step; no prompt at natural touchpoints (DMV renewal, health portal); registration only at in-person locations

Common benefits by segment:
- Peace of mind (family knows my wishes)
- Community identity / moral duty
- Reciprocity ("if I needed an organ, I'd want one available")
- Legacy / meaning (for some segments, especially after a loss)

**Step 3 — Strategy.**

| Barrier | Tool | Tactic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Procrastination / low salience | Prompt | QR code in DMV materials; government portal banner at login |
| Unclear registration pathway | Convenience | Streamline online registration to <3 steps; add to existing government touchpoints |
| "Others don't do it" misperception | Social norms | "X% of your county is registered — join them" |
| No commitment | Commitment | Pledge-then-register two-stage flow; family conversation pledge card |
| Superstition / fear of care | Communication | Targeted messaging to specific segment; peer messengers from trusted community |

**Step 4 — Pilot evidence.** Robitaille et al. (2021) field experiment (N = ~1 million road taxpayers, UK): persuasive messages paired with registration prompt increased registrations significantly. The reciprocity message ("if you needed an organ, would you want one?") produced ~1,171 additional sign-ups over the control prompt alone. Normative messages also outperformed control.

**Step 5 — Scale.** Abadie & Gay (2006) found that countries with opt-out (presumed consent) donation laws had significantly higher cadaveric donation rates than opt-in countries in a cross-national study — a canonical illustration of how upstream policy changes can outpace downstream behavioral interventions in long-run population impact.

### Segment the audience

Different barriers require different tools:

| Segment | Primary barrier | Tool emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Young adults at license renewal | Inertia / no prompt | Convenience + prompt |
| Older adults concerned about care quality | Fear / misinformation | Communication (trusted messenger) |
| Minority communities with historical medical mistrust | Trust gap | Midstream: community health workers, faith leaders |
| Families of potential donors | Family conflict / no conversation | Commitment (family conversation pledge) |

## Anti-patterns

- **Awareness-only campaigns.** "Raise awareness of organ donation" is not a social marketing objective. It describes an output (people learn something), not a behavioral outcome.
- **Skipping barrier research.** Building a strategy before knowing the primary barriers produces a communication campaign, not a behavioral program.
- **Using one tool for everything.** Commitment alone won't overcome structural friction; prompts alone won't overcome deep cultural barriers. Combine tools matched to identified barriers.
- **Measuring the wrong outcome.** Tracking impressions, website visits, or survey attitudes as proxy for behavior is the most common evaluation failure in social-change campaigns.
- **Boomerang norms.** Descriptive norms backfire when the communicated norm is *worse* than what the audience already does (e.g., "only 20% are registered" can discourage those who were considering it). Always test norms messaging in pilot.
- **Incentives that crowd out intrinsic motivation.** Expected, tangible, controlling incentives for registration can undermine the autonomous motivation that sustains long-term behavior (see behavior-change-psychology / SDT). Prefer removing costs over adding rewards.
- **Downstream-only design.** Individual behavior change is the hardest and most expensive path. Always ask whether upstream policy (opt-out, default enrollment) or midstream gatekeeper engagement could accomplish more.
- **One campaign, one audience.** Segmentation is required because barriers, benefits, and effective tools differ across groups. A faith community's primary barrier is different from a young adult's.
