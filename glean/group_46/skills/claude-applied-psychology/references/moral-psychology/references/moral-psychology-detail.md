# Moral Psychology — Detailed Reference

Deep detail for the seven themes in `SKILL.md`, plus the full citation list. Read
the relevant section before giving a deep or contested answer; keep the honest
replication caveats attached to every claim.

---

## 1. Moral Foundations Theory (MFT)

**Core claim (Graham, Haidt, et al. 2013).** Moral cognition runs on a small set
of innate-but-culturally-tunable foundations — "intuitions come first." The
canonical set:

| Foundation | Adaptive trigger | Virtue / vice | Cluster |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Care / harm** | suffering of offspring; vulnerability | kindness, compassion / cruelty | Individualizing |
| **Fairness / cheating** | reciprocity, cooperation, cheating | justice, proportionality / dishonesty | Individualizing |
| **Loyalty / betrayal** | coalition, group cohesion | patriotism, sacrifice / treason | Binding |
| **Authority / subversion** | hierarchy, dominance/deference | duty, respect / disobedience | Binding |
| **Sanctity (Purity) / degradation** | contamination, disgust | temperance, dignity / degradation | Binding |
| **Liberty / oppression** (proposed) | reactance to domination/bullies | autonomy / tyranny | (cross-cutting) |

- **Individualizing (care + fairness)** protect the individual; **Binding
  (loyalty + authority + sanctity)** bind groups together, sometimes at the
  individual's expense.
- **Political asymmetry.** Liberals rely mainly on care + fairness; conservatives
  use all five more evenly; libertarians weight Liberty highest and score low on
  the others (Iyer et al. 2012). This is the most replicated MFT result and the
  one with the clearest operator payoff.
- **Measurement.** Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-30) → **MFQ-2** (Atari et
  al. 2023), which splits Fairness into Equality and Proportionality and improves
  cross-cultural fit. Moral Foundations Vignettes and the Moral Foundations
  Dictionary (for text analysis) also exist. See moralfoundations.org.

**Contested — flag this honestly.** Factor-analytic support for *five clean
factors* is weak; many studies recover only **two** robust factors (roughly the
individualizing/binding split). Critics argue the foundations are not cleanly
separable, that the set is under-theorized about *why these and not others*, and
that fairness/care do real work for conservatives too once measured well. The
leading rival is **Morality-as-Cooperation (MAC; Curry, Mullins & Whitehouse
2019)**, which derives **seven** cooperative moral domains from the game theory of
non-zero-sum problems — family (kin altruism), group (coordination), reciprocity,
heroism (hawkish bravery), deference (dovish submission), fairness (division), and
property (prior possession) — and claims better theoretical grounding and
cross-cultural evidence than MFT's intuition-list. Treat MFT as a **useful
working vocabulary**, not settled fact.

**Operator use.** When two stakeholders won't converge despite agreeing on the
facts, the fight is usually over **which foundation governs the decision** (e.g.
"this is a fairness issue" vs "this is a loyalty/we-honor-commitments issue").
Name the foundation in play instead of piling on more evidence — evidence aimed at
the wrong foundation slides off.

## 2. Social Intuitionist Model (SIM)

**Source.** Haidt 2001, "The emotional dog and its rational tail," *Psychological
Review* 108: 814–834.

**Core claim.** Moral judgment is produced by **fast, automatic intuition first**;
conscious **reasoning is mostly post-hoc** — generated *after* the judgment to
justify it to others. Haidt's metaphor: reason is the **press secretary**, not the
judge — it explains decisions it did not make.

**The six links** (this is why the model is "social," not purely intuitionist):
1. **Intuitive judgment** — A's intuition → A's judgment (the default path).
2. **Post-hoc reasoning** — A's judgment → A's reasoning (justification).
3. **Reasoned persuasion** — A's reasoning → B's intuition (A's stated reasons can
   nudge B's gut).
4. **Social persuasion** — the mere fact that others hold a view shifts A's
   intuition.
5. **Reasoned judgment** — occasionally reasoning overrides intuition directly
   (rare, effortful).
6. **Private reflection** — A's own reflection triggers a *new* intuition.
Links 3, 5, and 6 are the escape hatch: reasoning has *limited but real* force,
mostly **through other people and over time**, not by winning an argument in the
moment.

**Key evidence — moral dumbfounding.** People maintain a moral judgment ("it's
wrong") while admitting they **cannot give a reason** — they sputter, "I can't
explain it, I just know it's wrong." Classic harmless-taboo vignettes: **Julie and
Mark** (consensual, protected, one-time sibling incest with no harm), the dog the
family eats after it dies, cleaning the toilet with the flag. **Caveat:** the
original 2000 dumbfounding study (Haidt, Björklund & Murphy) was an **unpublished
manuscript / undergraduate-thesis-scale demonstration (n≈30)**; the phenomenon has
since been replicated and refined (e.g. McHugh et al. 2017 confirm dumbfounding is
real but its rate and interpretation are debated). Cite it as **suggestive, not a
landmark finding**.

**Operator use.** Purely rational arguments bounce off moral intuitions because
the intuition came first and reasoning is defending it. To move someone, **reframe
the underlying intuition** (link 3 — give reasons that engage a *different* gut
read) or use **social/relational channels** (link 4 — a trusted peer's stance
moves the intuition more than your logic does). Don't expect to argue a
gut-level moral stance away in real time.

## 3. Dual-Process Theory of Moral Judgment

**Sources.** Greene et al. 2001 (*Science* 293: 2105 — the fMRI trolley study);
Greene et al. 2008 (*Cognition* 107: 1144 — cognitive load); Greene, *Moral
Tribes* (2013); Koenigs, Young, ... Damasio 2007 (*Nature* — VMPFC lesions).

**Core claim.** Two systems compete in moral judgment:
- **Automatic / emotional** responses push toward **deontological** judgments
  ("don't use a person as a means; it's just *wrong* to push him").
- **Controlled / cognitive** ("manual mode") responses push toward
  **utilitarian / consequentialist** judgments ("five lives > one").

**The trolley contrast** (the workhorse stimulus):
- **Switch / impersonal:** divert the trolley to kill 1 instead of 5 — **most
  people approve**. Low emotional engagement; cognitive route dominates.
- **Footbridge / personal:** push a large man off a footbridge to stop the
  trolley, killing 1 to save 5 — **most people refuse**, despite identical
  arithmetic. The up-close, intentional, hands-on harm fires a strong emotional
  alarm.

**Supporting evidence.**
- **fMRI:** personal dilemmas (footbridge) recruit emotion-associated regions
  (mPFC, posterior cingulate, amygdala); impersonal ones recruit
  cognitive-control / working-memory regions (DLPFC).
- **Cognitive load** (2008) **selectively slows** *utilitarian* responses —
  consistent with utilitarian judgment requiring effortful override — but does not
  slow deontological ones.
- **VMPFC-lesion patients** (Koenigs/Greene 2007) — who have blunted social
  emotion — give **more utilitarian** answers on personal dilemmas; so do patients
  on serotonergic manipulation, and people higher in psychopathy/alexithymia.

**Contested — attach these caveats.**
- **Kahane et al. 2015 (*Cognition*).** So-called "utilitarian" sacrificial answers
  mostly track **reduced harm-aversion (Instrumental Harm)**, *not* genuine
  **Impartial Beneficence** (caring equally about all). On the **Oxford
  Utilitarianism Scale (OUS)**, the two subscales dissociate: willingness to push
  the man correlates with *antisocial* traits, not with impartial concern for the
  greater good. So "more utilitarian on trolley" ≠ "more moral / more
  consequentialist in the philosophical sense."
- **CNI model (Gawronski et al. 2017, *JPSP*).** A formal multinomial model that
  decomposes responses into sensitivity to **C**onsequences, sensitivity to moral
  **N**orms, and a general preference for **I**naction (omission bias). Shows the
  classic "utilitarian vs deontological" dichotomy **conflates** at least three
  distinct parameters — e.g. someone who looks deontological may simply have a
  strong inaction bias.
- **Ecological validity.** Trolley problems are artificial, repetitive, and
  emotionally inert; whether they generalize to real moral life is heavily
  criticized (Bauman et al. 2014). Greene's dual-process *architecture* is more
  durable than any specific trolley result.

**Operator use.** When a decision feels viscerally wrong even though the math is
sound (an "efficient" but personal harm — laying off a loyal team via a cold
formula, a technically-fair clawback that feels brutal), expect **deontological
recoil** that no spreadsheet defeats. Lower the *personalness/directness* of the
harm or supply a procedural-justice wrapper (Theme 6), rather than re-presenting
the arithmetic.

## 4. Theory of Dyadic Morality (TDM)

**Source.** Schein & Gray 2018, "The Theory of Dyadic Morality," *Personality and
Social Psychology Review* 22: 32–70. (Builds on Gray, Young & Waytz 2012, "Mind
perception is the essence of morality.")

**Core claim.** Moral cognition is organized by a **cognitive template**: an
**intentional Agent causing harm to a vulnerable Patient (A→P)**. Morality is
fundamentally about **perceived harm to an experiencing mind**; the more an act
fits the dyadic template, the more wrong it feels. This is a **harm-based monist**
account — a direct challenge to MFT's claim that purity/loyalty/authority are
*separate* moral domains. TDM says those "non-harm" violations still get moralized
because people perceive *some* (often invisible or symbolic) harm.

**Key phenomena.**
- **Moral typecasting (Gray & Wegner 2009).** Agents and Patients are seen as
  **opposite roles**: a perceived *moral agent* (doer) is seen as *less*
  vulnerable to harm, and a perceived *moral patient* (victim) is seen as *less*
  capable of doing wrong. Villains feel pain less; victims can do no wrong. This
  warps blame and credit in disputes.
- **Dyadic completion.** Faced with a wrong that has no obvious victim
  ("victimless" taboo acts), people **invent or infer a victim** to complete the
  template — society, God, the self, future generations, "the vulnerable." Mirror
  image: shown a suffering victim, people search for a blameworthy agent.
- **Harm pluralism / continuum.** Moralization isn't binary; acts vary in how
  dyadic they appear, predicting how wrong they seem.

**Contested.** The **MFT-vs-TDM debate** is live and unresolved: is all morality
reducible to harm (TDM), or are there genuinely harm-independent moral domains
(MFT)? Evidence cuts both ways; don't present either as the winner.

**Operator use.** When a customer or stakeholder frames something as a moral
breach with **no concrete victim you can see**, expect **dyadic completion** —
they have cast *someone* as the harmed Patient (their team, their users, "the
relationship," their own integrity) and *you/your company* as the intentional
Agent. Surface **who they perceive as harmed** before you debate intent; arguing
"no one was hurt" attacks a template they will simply re-complete.

## 5. Moral Licensing & Moral Disengagement

Two distinct literatures about how people give *themselves* permission to act badly.

### 5a. Moral licensing / self-licensing

**Source.** Monin & Miller 2001 (*JPSP* 81: 33) — establishing good "moral
credentials" (e.g. first disagreeing with blatant sexism) **licenses** a later
more questionable act (favoring a man for a stereotypically-male job). Two
mechanisms: **moral credentials** (past good acts reframe an ambiguous later act
as non-bad) and **moral credits** (a balance you spend down).

**Effect is small and fragile — state this plainly.** Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg
2015 meta-analysis: overall **d ≈ 0.31**, but with **clear publication-bias
signals**; bias-corrected estimates fall to **d ≈ 0.18 or toward null**. A
**large Registered Replication Report** of a licensing paradigm found an effect
**near zero**. So licensing is a *plausible, weakly-supported* tendency, **not**
a reliable lever. Do not build a plan around inducing it or assuming it.

### 5b. Moral disengagement

**Source.** Bandura 1999 (*Personality and Social Psychology Review* 3: 193) and
Bandura 2002. Eight mechanisms by which people **selectively switch off**
self-sanction and commit harmful acts while keeping a positive self-image. Use
them as a **detection checklist** for how teams talk themselves into bad conduct:

1. **Moral justification** — "we did it for a good cause / to protect the customer."
2. **Euphemistic labeling** — sanitized language ("collateral adjustment,"
   "right-sizing," "managing the narrative" for lying).
3. **Advantageous comparison** — "compared to what competitor X does, this is
   nothing."
4. **Displacement of responsibility** — "I was just following orders / policy."
5. **Diffusion of responsibility** — "it was a group decision / nobody owns it."
   (4 and 5 are often listed together.)
6. **Distortion / disregard of consequences** — minimizing or ignoring the harm
   done ("they'll never notice," "no real impact").
7. **Dehumanization** — stripping the target of human qualities ("they're just a
   number / just churn / not a real partner").
8. **Attribution of blame** — "they brought it on themselves / they should have
   read the contract."

**Operator use.** When a team is sliding toward conduct it would normally reject
(misleading a customer, burying a known defect, an unfair clawback), listen for
these eight verbal tells. Naming the mechanism out loud ("that's euphemistic
labeling — say what we're actually doing") **re-engages** the self-sanction the
language was switching off. This is the most directly actionable item in the skill.

## 6. Organizational Justice (Fairness Perception)

**Anchor.** Colquitt 2001 (*Journal of Applied Psychology* 86: 386) — the
validated **four-factor** structure of justice perception. This is the
**highest-impact operator theme**: fairness is *perceived*, and perception is
driven far more by **process and treatment** than by the outcome itself.

**The four dimensions.**
1. **Distributive justice** — fairness of the **outcome / allocation**. Roots:
   **Adams' Equity Theory** (compare your outcome/input ratio to a referent;
   inequity → tension → restoration). Three allocation **norms** that can each feel
   "fair": **equity** (proportional to contribution — typical in business),
   **equality** (everyone the same), **need** (to each according to need). Disputes
   often come from two sides applying *different* norms.
2. **Procedural justice** — fairness of the **process** that produced the outcome.
   **Leventhal's six rules**: **consistency** (across people and time),
   **bias-suppression**, **accuracy** (good information), **correctability** (appeal
   mechanism), **representativeness** (all affected parties' concerns considered),
   **ethicality**. Plus **voice / process control** (Thibaut & Walker 1975 — being
   *heard* before the decision raises fairness even if you don't get your way).
3. **Interpersonal justice** — **dignity and respect** in how you're treated
   (Bies & Moag 1986). Politeness, no demeaning remarks, taking the person
   seriously.
4. **Informational justice** — **honest, adequate explanations**: candid reasons,
   timely, truthful, tailored. (3 + 4 together were originally **interactional
   justice**.)

**The fair-process effect (the single most useful finding).** A **fair process
raises acceptance of even an UNFAVORABLE outcome.** People will accept a price
increase, a denied feature, a lost escalation, or a tough renewal term they
*dislike* if the process that produced it was consistent, gave them **voice**,
treated them with **dignity**, and was **honestly explained**. Conversely, a
*good* outcome delivered through an arbitrary, voiceless, dismissive, or opaque
process still breeds resentment and erodes trust. Related: **uncertainty
management** (van den Bos) — fair process matters *most* when people are uncertain
or anxious, exactly the escalation/renewal moments operators face.

**Operator use.** When a customer says **"this isn't fair,"** they are usually
*not* only objecting to the dollar amount (distributive) — they are reacting to
**process** (no warning, no voice, inconsistent with last time) and **treatment**
(curt, no real explanation). You often **cannot change the outcome**, but you can
almost always improve the **process and explanation**: give advance notice, let
them be heard, apply the rule consistently, explain candidly, offer an appeal
path. That is the lever that turns "unfair" into "I don't love it, but I get it."
Applies to pricing changes, escalations, negotiations, clawbacks, and missed-SLA
conversations.

## 7. The Is–Ought Problem & the Naturalistic Fallacy

**Sources.** **Hume**, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) — the "is–ought" gap:
authors slide from *is*/*is not* statements to *ought*/*ought not* without
justifying the leap. **G.E. Moore**, *Principia Ethica* (1903) — the
**naturalistic fallacy**: defining "good" in terms of any natural property
(pleasure, what we desire, what we evolved to approve) commits an error, supported
by the **open-question argument** ("X is pleasant — but is it *good*?" remains a
*meaningful, open* question, so good ≠ pleasant).

**Why it's in this skill.** Everything above is **descriptive** moral psychology —
how human moral cognition *actually works*. None of it tells you what is *actually
right*. Knowing that disgust drives purity judgments doesn't make disgust-based
judgments correct; knowing fairness perception is hackable via process doesn't make
it ethical to hack. This is a **hard limit on the ethics of nudging**: you can use
moral psychology to *understand and communicate*, but using it to *manufacture
consent* or steer people past their own judgment is an is→ought overreach and an
ethical line. (For the governance/AI-fairness version of this line — bias, model
fairness, consent, responsible AI — defer to `da-11-ethics-and-privacy`.)

**Operator use.** When someone argues "this is natural / this is how people are
wired, therefore it's fine," that's a naturalistic-fallacy move — flag the gap.
And keep your own influence honest: explain and reframe, don't exploit the
machinery to override informed consent.
