# North Carolina Recording Laws

Educational overview of NC audio and video recording law. NOT legal advice; consult a licensed NC attorney for specific situations.

---

## Overview: One-Party Consent State

North Carolina is a **one-party consent** jurisdiction. Under **N.C. Gen. Stat. § 15A-287**, a person may lawfully record a wire, oral, or electronic communication if:

1. They are a **party to the communication**, OR
2. They have obtained **advance consent from at least one party**

Recording a conversation you are **not** participating in — without any party's consent — is a **Class H felony**.

---

## Core Statute: GS 15A-287 (Electronic Surveillance Act)

**Prohibited conduct** (Class H felony unless an exception applies):
- Willfully intercepting, endeavoring to intercept, or procuring interception of any wire, oral, or electronic communication
- Using a device to intercept oral communications
- Disclosing contents of an intercepted communication
- Using intercepted content

**Key definitions:**
- **Wire communication**: Aural transfer via wire/cable connecting the parties
- **Oral communication**: Words uttered by a person with a **reasonable expectation of privacy**
- **Electronic communication**: Transfer of signals/data by wire, radio, electromagnetic, or optical means (email, texts, VoIP)

**Critical limitation on "oral communication"**: The secret recording ban on in-person conversations only applies when the speaker had a **subjective expectation of privacy** that was **objectively reasonable** given the circumstances. Conversations in public spaces where a person cannot reasonably expect privacy do not meet this threshold.

### Statutory Exceptions (GS 15A-287(b))
- Publicly accessible electronic communications (open radio transmissions)
- Emergency communications intercepts for civil defense/public safety
- FCC enforcement activities
- Communications service providers intercepting in the **normal course of employment** when necessary to render service (GS 15A-287(c))

### Enhanced Government Official Penalty (GS 15A-287(g))
Public officers who knowingly violate the wiretapping statute face:
- Removal from office
- Permanent ineligibility for any elected or appointed public office in NC

---

## Criminal Penalties

| Violation | Classification | Typical Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Unlawful interception/disclosure (GS 15A-287) | Class H felony | 4–39 months (Prior Record Level I → VI); probation typical for first offenders at PRL I |
| Disclosing lawfully intercepted content to obstruct investigation | Class G felony | 8–31 months |
| Negligently divulging surveillance orders | Class 1 misdemeanor | Up to 120 days |

---

## Civil Remedies: GS 15A-296

Any person whose wire, oral, or electronic communication is unlawfully intercepted, disclosed, or used may sue for:

- **Actual damages** (economic loss, emotional distress)
- **Minimum damages**: Greater of $100/day for each day of violation **or** $1,000 (whichever is higher)
- **Punitive damages**
- **Reasonable attorney fees and litigation costs** (mandatory)

**Complete defense**: Good-faith reliance on a court order or on a representation by the Attorney General or a District Attorney.

## Audio Recording: Practical Rules

### Phone Calls
- One-party consent applies to landlines, cell phones, VoIP (Zoom, Teams, FaceTime), and voicemails
- **You must be an active participant** in the call to record without others' consent
- Text messages and emails are "electronic communications" covered by GS 15A-287

### In-Person Conversations
- You may record any conversation you participate in
- Recording without participation is a felony
- Reasonable privacy expectation matters: shouting in a crowded public park → probably no expectation; private office conversation → expectation exists

### Interstate Calls
When a call involves parties in different states:
- NC law applies to NC-side conduct
- If the other party is in a **two-party (all-party) consent state** (CA, FL, IL, MD, MA, PA, WA, etc.), that state's stricter law may also apply
- **Best practice for cross-state calls**: obtain consent of all parties to avoid federal ECPA exposure and out-of-state liability

## Video Recording

### Audio-Only vs. Video-Only vs. Both
- **Video without audio**: Generally lawful as long as the subject has no reasonable expectation of privacy in the location — no separate NC statute restricts video-only recording in public or visible private spaces
- **Audio + video**: The audio component triggers GS 15A-287 one-party consent requirements
- **Covert video in private spaces**: Governed by GS 14-202 (see below)

### GS 14-202 — Secret Peeping / Voyeurism

Amended effective December 1, 2025 by Session Law 2025-70.

| Conduct | Classification |
|---|---|
| Secretly peeping into a room occupied by another person | Class 1 misdemeanor |
| Peeping into a room while **possessing** a device capable of creating a photographic image, with intent to capture | Class A1 misdemeanor |
| Using a device to create a photographic image while peeping, for sexual gratification | Class I felony |
| Secretly capturing images underneath/through clothing ("up-skirt," "down-blouse") | Class I felony |
| Secretly installing a recording device in a room, intending to capture another person | Class I felony |
| Disseminating/publishing images obtained by voyeurism | Class H felony; requires **sex-offender registration** |

**"Room" defined broadly**: bedroom, bathroom, restroom, shower, dressing room, and similar private spaces.

### Security Cameras (Commercial/Residential)
- No NC statute specifically regulates placement of security cameras
- General rule: lawful to record in areas visible from public or where subjects have no reasonable expectation of privacy
- Prohibited: cameras in bathrooms, dressing rooms, bedrooms, or other high-privacy spaces

## Recording Police and Government Officials

### First Amendment Right to Record Police
Recording police officers performing their duties **in public** is a **clearly established First Amendment right** in NC.

Key authority: **Sharpe v. Winterville Police Department** (4th Cir. 2023) — multiple practitioner sources cite this case as affirming a First Amendment right to record police in public within the Fourth Circuit (which covers NC).[^6] Officers may impose **reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions** but may **not** ban recording or retaliate against citizens for recording. Note: the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press considers the 4th Circuit's ruling less definitive than some state courts; consult counsel for contested situations.

**Practical rules:**
- You may record from a public vantage point without interfering with law enforcement operations
- Officers cannot legally seize your phone without a warrant solely because you recorded them
- "Stop recording or I'll arrest you" is not a lawful order in a public setting
- Obstruction charges require actual physical interference, not mere recording

### Government Body Cameras (GS 132-1.4A)
Law enforcement dash cameras and body cameras are **explicitly exempt from the NC Public Records Act** under GS 132-1.4A. Access requires:
- A court order, OR
- Request under a specific statutory procedure (showing personal involvement or legitimate need)

This contrasts with most other government official recordings, which typically become public records under GS 132-1.

### Recording Government Meetings
Under NC's Open Meetings Law, **any person may photograph, film, tape-record, or otherwise reproduce any part of a meeting required to be open** to the public. Governing bodies may regulate equipment placement to prevent "undue interference" but cannot ban ordinary recording of open sessions.

**Closed sessions**: No explicit statutory authority to record; participants risk civil liability for recording confidential matters (personnel, attorney-client, ongoing investigations).

### Government Officials Recording Citizens
Officials face the same one-party consent rules as private citizens, plus the enhanced penalty under GS 15A-287(g): an official who knowingly records without any party's consent faces removal and permanent bar from public office.

## Workplace Recording

### Employees Recording Colleagues/Supervisors
- NC one-party consent: an employee may record **any workplace conversation they participate in**, including with HR, supervisors, or coworkers
- No requirement to notify the other party
- Commonly used to document harassment, discrimination, or wage disputes

### Employer Recording Policies
- Employers may lawfully implement **no-recording policies** and discipline or terminate employees who violate them
- However, blanket no-recording policies may conflict with **NLRA Section 7 rights** (protecting concerted activity including recording to document workplace conditions) — NLRB has found overbroad policies unlawful
- Employers may conduct video surveillance of **common areas and work floors** without notice (no reasonable privacy expectation in public-facing workspaces)
- Employers may **not** place audio or video recording devices in restrooms, locker rooms, or other high-privacy spaces

## Federal Law: ECPA Interaction

The federal **Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA)**, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510–2522, establishes a **federal one-party consent floor** for interstate communications.

- NC law mirrors ECPA's one-party standard for in-state communications
- Federal civil remedy under **18 U.S.C. § 2520**: actual damages, greater of $100/day or $1,000 minimum, punitive damages, attorney fees — identical floor to NC's GS 15A-296
- Federal and state claims may be pleaded simultaneously; courts prevent **duplicative recovery**
- Federal ECPA preempts state law only where state law provides *less* protection (NC's standards are coextensive, not weaker)

| **Federal courts in NC** | Recording devices and cameras **prohibited entirely** |

## Attorney-Client Communications

Recording between an attorney and client raises intersecting layers of law:

### One-Party Consent Still Applies
NC's one-party consent rule governs the *recording act* itself. A client recording their own conversation with their attorney is lawful under GS 15A-287 — the client is a party. The attorney does not need to be told.

### Attorney-Client Privilege Is a Separate Issue
Recording a conversation does not destroy attorney-client privilege — the communication remains privileged if it meets the privilege test (confidential, for legal advice). However:
- If a third party is present and consented to the recording, privilege may be **waived** for that communication
- Sharing the recording with non-privileged parties waives privilege as to that recording
- Courts have found no expectation of privacy inside a courthouse conference room is reduced but not eliminated

### At the Courthouse
- **Outside courtrooms** (hallways, conference rooms, attorney meeting rooms): NC one-party consent applies — you may record conversations you participate in
- **Inside a courtroom during proceedings**: requires presiding judge's permission regardless of whether you are a party (see Court Proceedings section above)
- **Federal courthouse**: all recording devices prohibited

### Ethical Rules for Attorneys
NC Rules of Professional Conduct Rule 8.4 prohibits attorneys from engaging in conduct involving dishonesty or misrepresentation. Bar guidance in some states (not NC specifically as of 2026) has found secret recording by attorneys ethically problematic even where legally permitted. NC has no explicit bar opinion banning attorney self-recording of client communications, but attorneys should consult NC State Bar guidance before recording clients without disclosure.

## AI and Smart Recording Devices (Plaud, Otter.ai, Fireflies)

Wearable and AI-powered recorders (Plaud Note, Plaud Pin, Otter.ai, Fireflies, etc.) that continuously transcribe conversations are subject to the same NC recording laws as any other device. The technology does not change the legal framework.

### Key Rules for AI Recorders

| Scenario | Legal? | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Recording meetings/calls **you participate in** | Yes | One-party consent satisfied by your participation |
| Recording conversations **you are not part of** | No, Class H felony | Device passively capturing others' conversations |
| Recording in a private space without consent | Depends | Location privacy expectation governs; bathroom/locker = felony under GS 14-202 |
| Auto-transcription sent to cloud (Plaud, Otter) | Legal act of recording is separate from storage | Storage/sharing governed by contract/privacy policy, not GS 15A-287 |
| Using AI recorder at courthouse (outside courtroom) | Yes for conversations you're in | Subject to courthouse rules — some courthouses prohibit electronic devices in security areas |
| Using AI recorder inside a courtroom | No, requires judge permission | Same rule as any recording device |

### Practical Guidance for Plaud/AI Recorders
- **Always-on mode**: if the device records ambient conversations of people who didn't know they were being recorded and you are NOT a party to those conversations, that recording is illegal under GS 15A-287
- **Meeting mode**: recording a meeting you attend is lawful; the other participants do not need to be notified under NC law (though professional courtesy and company policy may require notice)
- **Cloud sync**: uploading recordings to Plaud's or Otter's servers is a separate contractual matter — NC law does not restrict this, but attorney-client or work-product content uploaded to third-party servers may waive privilege
- **Courthouse/legal office use**: physically permitted outside courtrooms; check individual courthouse security rules for device restrictions at entry points

| Scenario | Legal? | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Phone call I'm part of, other party in NC | Yes | GS 15A-287 one-party |
| Phone call I'm NOT part of | No, Class H felony | GS 15A-287 |
| Phone call with CA/FL party | Risky, get all-party consent | Multi-state ECPA risk |
| In-person conversation I'm in, public space | Yes | GS 15A-287 + no privacy expectation |
| In-person conversation in private office | Yes (you're a party) | GS 15A-287 |
| In-person conversation I'm NOT in | No, Class H felony | GS 15A-287 |
| Security camera in store/parking lot | Yes (no audio) | No prohibition |
| Camera in bathroom/locker room | No, Class I felony | GS 14-202 |
| Recording police in public | Yes | First Amendment, Sharpe (4th Cir. 2023) |
| Attending city council meeting | Yes | NC Open Meetings Law |
| Requesting police body cam footage | Court order required | GS 132-1.4A |
| Recording at work (conversation I'm in) | Yes (may violate policy) | GS 15A-287; NLRA caveat |

| Plaud capturing ambient convos I'm NOT part of | No, Class H felony | GS 15A-287 |

## Anti-Patterns / Common Mistakes

- **"I'm in NC so I just need one party"** — True for NC-only calls; false for interstate calls with parties in all-party states. Get all-party consent for cross-state calls to be safe.
- **"Video-only cameras are always legal"** — Wrong for private spaces (bathrooms, dressing rooms). GS 14-202 applies regardless of whether audio is captured.
- **"My employer can't stop me from recording at work"** — You have the right to record conversations you participate in under NC law, but employer policies can result in discipline, and NLRB protection only covers concerted activity, not all recording.
- **"I can record police anywhere"** — True in public spaces (sidewalks, parks, public lobbies). In private or semi-private spaces (someone's home, a hospital room, a private business), the property owner's reasonable privacy expectation applies and you may not have a right to enter or remain solely to record. Obstruction charges require actual physical interference with operations, not mere recording.
- **"The recording is secret so I'm safe"** — One-party consent means YOU consenting as a party. Third-party covert recording of others' conversations is always a felony under GS 15A-287.
