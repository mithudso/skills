# NC Involuntary Commitment for Substance Use Disorder
## GS 122C-281 through 122C-288 — Petition, Criteria, Hearing, Rights

> **Educational only — NOT legal advice.** Involuntary commitment law is procedurally complex and fact-specific. Consult a licensed NC attorney or the NC Office of Special Counsel for any specific situation.

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

> **Statute note:** GS 122C-280 is **reserved** and contains no substantive law. The operative involuntary commitment statutes for substance abusers are **GS 122C-281 through 122C-288** (Part 8, Article 5, Chapter 122C — the NC Mental Health, Developmental Disabilities, and Substance Abuse Act of 1985).

## 1. Overview — SUD vs. Mental Health Commitment Tracks

North Carolina runs **parallel involuntary commitment tracks** for mental illness (Part 7, GS 122C-261–279) and substance use disorder (Part 8, GS 122C-281–288). Both tracks share the same evidentiary standard, procedural timeline, and patient rights framework — but are governed by separate statutory provisions and use different forms.[^1]

The SUD commitment track applies when a person's condition is **primarily a substance use disorder** (alcohol, drugs, or other substance dependence/abuse) as defined under GS 122C-3(36), rather than primarily a mental illness. Co-occurring disorders are common; clinical and legal judgment determines which track controls.[^2]

---

## 2. Who May Petition

**Anyone** who has knowledge that a person is a substance abuser who is dangerous to themselves or others may file a petition.[^3]

This includes:
- Family members
- Friends, neighbors, coworkers
- Medical, mental health, or behavioral health professionals
- Law enforcement officers
- Social workers

There is no requirement that the petitioner be a family member or licensed professional. The petition is filed before a **clerk of superior court or magistrate**.

---

## 3. Commitment Criteria — The Two-Part Test

The commitment criteria must be established by **clear, cogent, and convincing evidence** at the district court hearing:[^1][^2]

**Part 1 — Substance Abuser:** The person must be a **substance abuser** as defined in GS 122C-3(36) — someone who has lost the power of self-control with respect to the use of alcohol or other substances, or who is addicted to alcohol or other substances to the extent that their health or safety is threatened.

**Part 2 — Dangerousness:** The person must be **dangerous to self OR dangerous to others**:

- **Dangerous to self:** Has inflicted or attempted to inflict serious bodily harm on themselves, or has threatened to do so and has the means, OR is incapacitated by alcohol or other substances to the degree that immediate care is required.
- **Dangerous to others:** Has inflicted or attempted to inflict serious bodily harm on another, or has threatened to do so and has the means, creating a reasonable probability of harm to others.

Both elements must be independently met — a substance use disorder diagnosis alone is insufficient.[^1]

## 4. Step-by-Step Process

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

### Step 1 — Petition Before Clerk or Magistrate (GS 122C-281)
- Petitioner files a sworn affidavit describing specific facts supporting the belief that the respondent is a substance abuser and dangerous.
- Clerk or magistrate reviews the petition (hearsay and secondhand knowledge admissible at this stage — evidentiary rules do not fully apply).
- If **reasonable grounds** are found: a **custody order** is issued directing law enforcement to take the respondent into custody within **24 hours**.

### Step 2 — Custody and Transportation (GS 122C-281)
- Law enforcement officer takes respondent into custody and transports to a facility for first examination.
- Transportation is provided by the **county where the respondent is taken into custody**.

### Step 3 — First Examination (GS 122C-283)
- A **commitment examiner** (physician or eligible psychologist) must examine the respondent **as soon as possible, no later than 24 hours after custody**.
- The examiner recommends one of:
  - **Inpatient commitment** (proceed to second examination and hearing)
  - **Outpatient commitment** (less restrictive alternative)
  - **Release** (criteria not met; proceedings terminated)

### Step 4 — Second Examination (GS 122C-285)
- If inpatient commitment is recommended: a **different** qualified professional must examine the respondent **within 24 hours of admission** to the 24-hour facility.
- The second examiner also recommends inpatient commitment, outpatient commitment, or release.
- If commitment is recommended: the facility holds and treats the respondent pending the district court hearing.

### Step 5 — Clerk Calendars Hearing (GS 122C-284)
- On receipt of the commitment recommendation, the clerk must:
  - **Assign counsel** (from NC Office of Special Counsel)
  - **Calendar the hearing** (within 10 days of custody)
  - **Notify** respondent, counsel, and petitioner

### Step 6 — District Court Hearing (GS 122C-286)
- Must occur within **10 days** of the person being taken into custody.
- District court judge conducts the hearing (see Section 6 below).

## 5. Special Emergency Procedure (GS 122C-282)

When the standard petition process would cause **dangerous delay** due to violence or immediate risk to life or property, a **special emergency procedure** allows bypassing the first examination:[^1][^4]

**Criteria for special emergency order:**
1. The allegations in the affidavit are true.
2. The respondent is **violent** and requires **physical restraint**.
3. Delay caused by taking the respondent to an examiner would **endanger life or property**.

The clerk or magistrate must find these elements by **clear, cogent, and convincing evidence** at the petition stage.

**Effect:** Law enforcement may transport the respondent **directly to a 24-hour facility** without a first examination. The second examination and hearing requirements still apply.

---

## 6. District Court Hearing (GS 122C-286)

The district court hearing is the core due process protection in the commitment process.[^2][^4]

**Timeline:** Must occur **within 10 days** of the respondent being taken into custody.

**Evidentiary standard:** Clear, cogent, and convincing evidence (stricter than preponderance; less than beyond a reasonable doubt).

**NC Rules of Evidence apply:** Unlike the petition stage, hearsay is subject to objection at the hearing. The respondent's counsel may object to hearsay and improper opinion testimony.

**Respondent's rights at hearing:**
- Right to be **present**
- Right to **testify**
- Right to **call witnesses**
- Right to **cross-examine** all witnesses
- Right to present **independent psychiatric/clinical evidence**

**Possible outcomes:**
- **Commitment order** — inpatient or outpatient (see Sections 8–9)
- **Dismissal** — criteria not met; respondent released
- **Voluntary admission** — respondent may consent to voluntary treatment instead

---

## 7. Patient Rights During Commitment

Throughout the involuntary commitment process, respondents retain significant rights:[^2][^4]

| Right | Details |
|---|---|
| **Appointed counsel** | NC Office of Special Counsel provides state-appointed attorneys; respondent may also retain private counsel |
| **Least restrictive treatment** | Commitment must be to the least restrictive appropriate treatment setting |
| **Independent evaluation** | Right to request an independent clinical evaluation |
| **Participation in hearing** | Full evidentiary rights at district court hearing |
| **Treatment refusal** | Right to refuse certain treatments, except when necessary to prevent imminent serious harm |
| **Discharge** | Right to discharge as soon as a less restrictive treatment is appropriate |
| **Appeal** | Right to appeal commitment order (see Section 11) |
| **42 CFR Part 2 protections** | SUD treatment records from a Part 2 program remain confidential throughout; see `nc-sud-confidentiality.md` |

---

## 8. Length of Commitment and Renewal

**Initial commitment order:** Up to **90 days** (inpatient).[^2]

**Renewal:** Continued commitment requires **additional district court hearings** with the same evidentiary standard — clear, cogent, and convincing evidence. There is no fixed maximum total duration; each renewal period is adjudicated individually.

**Outpatient commitment:** Initial period up to **180 days** (see Section 9).

**Involuntary commitment order form:** AOC form SP-306 (GS 122C-287) is the court order for substance abuse commitment; last updated July 2024.[^4]

---

## 9. Outpatient Commitment as an Alternative

NC law allows **outpatient commitment** as a less restrictive alternative to inpatient treatment:[^3]

- Can be recommended by the commitment examiner at Step 3 or Step 4.
- Can be ordered by the district court judge at the hearing.
- Requires the respondent to comply with outpatient treatment conditions (counseling, medication, drug testing, etc.) while living in the community.
- Violation of outpatient commitment conditions can result in a custody order returning the respondent to a 24-hour facility.
- Initial outpatient commitment period: up to **180 days**.
- Outpatient commitment is distinct from **Assisted Outpatient Treatment (AOT)** under NC mental health law, though both serve a similar function.

---

## 10. Relationship to Mental Health Commitment (GS 122C-261 et seq.)

The mental illness commitment track (Part 7, GS 122C-261–279) and the SUD commitment track (Part 8, GS 122C-281–288) are parallel but distinct:[^1]

| Feature | Mental Health Track (Part 7) | SUD Track (Part 8) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary condition | Mental illness (GS 122C-3(21)) | Substance abuser (GS 122C-3(36)) |
| Petition statutes | GS 122C-261 | GS 122C-281 |
| Same timeline | Yes (24hr examination, 10-day hearing) | Yes |
| Same evidentiary standard | Yes (clear, cogent, convincing) | Yes |
| Same patient rights | Yes | Yes |
| AOT (outpatient) | GS 122C-271 et seq. | GS 122C-285 et seq. |

**Co-occurring disorders:** When a person has both a mental illness and SUD, clinicians and legal teams must determine which condition is primary. Courts may use the more applicable track or, in practice, both petitions may be filed. This is an area requiring careful clinical and legal judgment.

---

## 11. Appeal Rights (GS 122C-288)

A respondent who has been involuntarily committed under the SUD track has the right to **appeal** the district court's commitment order:[^2]

- Appeal goes to the **NC Court of Appeals** (not superior court).
- Appellate review examines whether the record supports the findings of fact and whether the commitment criteria were correctly applied.
- The NC Office of Special Counsel provides appellate representation to respondents who cannot afford private counsel.

---

## 12. Practical Notes

- **Emergency department boarding:** Despite statutory timelines requiring examination within 24 hours, psychiatric bed shortages result in average ED stays of approximately **65 hours** in practice — a significant gap between law on paper and lived experience.[^2]
- **Voluntary admission alternative:** At the hearing, a respondent may choose to consent to voluntary admission to a treatment facility, avoiding a formal commitment order. Voluntary status may be preferable for treatment engagement.
- **Family petitioners:** Family members often contact LME-MCOs or local mental health crisis services before filing a petition. Crisis services may facilitate voluntary treatment, avoiding the involuntary commitment process entirely.
- **Medicaid continuation:** Medicaid benefits continue during inpatient SUD commitment; Tailored Plans (launched July 1, 2024) now cover SUD inpatient treatment for members with severe SUD.

## 13. Anti-Patterns / Common Misunderstandings

**"GS 122C-280 governs SUD involuntary commitment"**
No — GS 122C-280 is **reserved** and contains no substantive law. The operative statute is GS 122C-281 through 122C-288.[^1]

**"NC uses a 72-hour hold for SUD"**
NC does not technically use a "72-hour hold" framework. The actual timeline involves: custody + up to 24 hours to first examination + up to 24 hours to second examination + hearing within 10 days of custody. The "72-hour" term reflects general psychiatric hold terminology from other states.[^2]

**"Only family or doctors can file a commitment petition"**
No — any person with knowledge that someone is a substance abuser and dangerous may petition. There is no requirement that the petitioner be related to or professionally involved with the respondent.[^3]

**"A commitment order automatically means long-term institutionalization"**
No — the initial commitment order is for up to 90 days (inpatient) or 180 days (outpatient). Continued commitment requires additional court hearings. Discharge must occur as soon as a less restrictive setting is appropriate.[^2]

**"The commitment process is the same as for mental illness"**
Procedurally similar, but legally distinct tracks. The criteria differ (mental illness definition vs. substance abuser definition), and different statutory provisions apply. Co-occurring cases require careful determination of primary condition.[^1]
