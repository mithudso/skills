## 1. Overview — NC's Model Approach

North Carolina's approach to opioid litigation settlements has been recognized as a national model. Rather than diverting funds to the state general fund, NC negotiated a **Memorandum of Agreement (MOA)** that sends the majority of settlement funds **directly to local governments** — the counties and municipalities closest to where the crisis is felt.[^1]

NC has collected settlements from multiple defendants (Johnson & Johnson, the "Big Three" distributors — McKesson, Cardinal Health, AmerisourceBergen — Walmart, Walgreens, CVS, Teva, Allergan, Endo, Kroger, and others). As of 2024–2025, NC's total expected opioid settlement receipts are in the hundreds of millions of dollars over 18 years.

---

## 2. NC Memorandum of Agreement (MOA)

The **NC MOA** (signed by NCDHHS, NC Department of Justice, and participating local governments) is the governing document for opioid settlement fund distribution and use in NC.[^1][^2]

**Key MOA requirements:**
- Funds must be used for **opioid abatement** — purposes directly addressing the opioid epidemic
- Local governments must adhere to spending limitations (Exhibit A or Exhibit B strategies)
- Transparency and reporting obligations (feeding into CORE-NC)
- Collaborative strategic planning is incentivized (unlocks Exhibit B spending)

---

## 3. Fund Allocation — 85/15 Split

The NC MOA directs:
- **85%** of opioid settlement funds → NC's 100 counties and 17 qualifying municipalities (direct payments)
- **15%** → NC General Assembly, directed to NCDHHS for state-level abatement initiatives[^1]

This is a distinctive feature of the NC model — most states retain a larger share at the state level. Sending 85% directly to local governments was designed to ensure resources reach communities most affected by the opioid crisis.

**County allocation formula:** Within the 85%, amounts are distributed among counties based on a formula that accounts for the county's relative share of opioid impact metrics (overdose deaths, treatment need, etc.). The ncopioidsettlement.org website publishes county-specific payment schedules.

## 4. Allowable Uses — Exhibit A and Exhibit B

Settlement funds must be spent on **opioid abatement purposes**. The MOA provides two tiers of allowable uses:[^1][^2]

### Exhibit A — High-Impact Strategies (Available to All)

Any local government may immediately fund strategies from **Exhibit A**, a curated short list of evidence-based high-impact uses:

- **Collaborative strategic planning** — engaging the community in developing an opioid response plan
- **Evidence-based addiction treatment** — MAT, ASAM-aligned treatment services
- **Recovery support services** — peer support, recovery coaching, recovery community organizations
- **Recovery housing support** — funding for certified recovery residences
- **Employment-related services** — job training, transitional employment for people in recovery
- **Early intervention** — programs targeting at-risk populations before dependency
- **Naloxone distribution** — community distribution programs, first responder supplies
- **Post-overdose response teams** — follow-up services after non-fatal overdose
- **Syringe Service Programs** — see `nc-harm-reduction-laws.md` for legal framework
- **Criminal justice diversion programs** — deflection and drug treatment court support
- **Addiction treatment** — general treatment funding

### Exhibit B — Broader Strategies (Requires Planning Process)

Counties that complete a **collaborative strategic planning process** may also fund a broader list from **Exhibit B** — taken from the national settlement agreements. This includes additional prevention, education, and harm reduction strategies beyond those in Exhibit A.

## 5. State (NCDHHS) Spending (15%)

The 15% state share is directed to the NC General Assembly for appropriation to NCDHHS.[^1]

**FY 2023–2024 state spending examples:**
- ~$3.7 million to NCDHHS for grants to entities addressing opioid overdose epidemic impacts
- ~$5.2 million to UNC for competitive grants for opioid abatement research and development

**FY 2024–2025 state spending examples:**
- ~$4.5 million to NCDHHS for grants to entities
- ~$5.5 million to UNC for opioid abatement research

State funds have also supported the CORE-NC platform and statewide data infrastructure for opioid response.

## 6. County and Municipal Spending (85%)

Counties receive their allocations over the 18-year settlement payment period. Counties may:
- Spend funds directly on Exhibit A/B programs
- Contract with community organizations, LME-MCOs, treatment providers, or recovery community organizations
- Accumulate funds for larger capital investments (within limits)

**Spending examples reported across NC counties (illustrative):**
- Naloxone purchase and distribution through local health departments
- Funding recovery coaches and peer support specialists through treatment courts
- Recovery housing support (NCARR-certified residences) — see `nc-recovery-housing-law.md`
- Post-overdose response team staffing
- MAT access expansion — supporting OBOT practices and OTP access in underserved counties

## 7. CORE-NC — Transparency and Accountability

**CORE-NC (Community Opioid Resources Engine for North Carolina)** is the transparency and accountability platform for NC opioid settlement funds.[^1]

**What CORE-NC provides:**
- Settlement information and payment schedules by county
- Spending plans submitted by local governments
- Past spending reports
- Place-based data dashboards: overdose rates, treatment capacity, naloxone distribution, etc.
- Resources for local governments on allowable uses and planning

CORE-NC is designed to allow residents, advocates, researchers, and policymakers to track whether opioid settlement funds are being used effectively.

## 9. Anti-Patterns / Common Misunderstandings

**"Opioid settlement funds go to the state general fund"**
No. The NC MOA specifically prohibits using settlement funds for general governmental purposes. 85% goes directly to counties/municipalities for opioid abatement, and the 15% state share must also be used for opioid abatement purposes through NCDHHS.[^1]

**"Counties can spend settlement funds on anything related to health"**
No. Funds are restricted to opioid abatement purposes under Exhibit A or (with planning) Exhibit B. General health expenditures do not qualify. Counties risk clawback or disqualification from future payments for improper spending.[^2]

**"NC has received all its settlement funds already"**
No. Most national opioid settlements pay out over 10–18 years. Counties are receiving annual payments; the total amounts are not available upfront. Check ncopioidsettlement.org for the payment schedule specific to each county.

**"Small counties don't receive enough to do anything meaningful"**
Small counties with fewer overdose metrics receive smaller allocations, but they can pool resources through regional collaborations, apply for NCDHHS opioid abatement grants (from the 15% state share), and access state-funded programs through their LME-MCO.[^2]
