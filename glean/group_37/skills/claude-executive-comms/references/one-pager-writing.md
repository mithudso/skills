# one-pager-writing

## Overview

One-pagers are the highest-impact document format in business: a single page that earns a meeting, secures a budget, aligns a partner, or briefs an exec in under three minutes. The format's discipline — fit on one page, lead with the point, design as visually as you write — forces clarity that longer documents allow you to dodge.

This skill covers the five canonical one-pager species and the rules that apply to all of them: account brief, project pitch, product one-pager, sales-enablement one-pager, and the Amazon-style 1-pager / PR-FAQ opener.

## Core Concepts

### 1. The no-second-page discipline

A one-pager fits on one page. That is the entire constraint. The moment you flow to page 2, you have lost the format and the reader's contract — they will not read page 2, and the document is now a half-finished two-pager. Everything else in this skill exists to enforce this rule.

Practical effects:
- Cut by 30-50% from your first draft. Always.
- Drop full sentences for fragments where fragments work.
- Move evidence to an appendix link, not a footer.
- If three things won't fit, you have three things — not one.

The one-pager is a forcing function, not a layout. Honor the constraint.

### 2. Above-the-fold visual hierarchy

The reader scans before they read. The first 4-6 seconds tell them whether to keep reading. What lives above the fold (top third of the page on screen, top quarter on print):

1. **Headline** — the one-line value prop, written in the reader's language, not yours.
2. **Subhead or value-prop sentence** — what changes for the reader if they say yes.
3. **The "if you only read one line" sentence** — usually identical to the subhead, set off visually (bold, callout box, color tint).
4. **A visual anchor** — chart, screenshot, diagram, photo, or large pull-quote. The reader's eye lands here second.

If a reader scrolls past the fold without engaging, the rest of the page is wasted ink.

### 3. The "if you only read one line" pattern

Every one-pager has one sentence the reader must take away. Find it. Bold it. Place it above the fold. Repeat it in different words at the end.

Test: if a reader reads only that one sentence, do they understand what you want them to do, fund, or decide? If no, the sentence is too soft. Rewrite until yes.

```
Bad:  "Our new initiative aims to improve customer engagement
       through innovative cross-functional collaboration."

Good: "Cutting onboarding from 12 days to 3 will unlock $2.4M in
       Year 1 retention. We need $180K and 2 engineers for 6 weeks."
```

### 4. Headline + value-prop pattern

The two-line opening that earns the next 60 seconds of attention.

- **Headline**: the noun phrase the reader cares about. The thing. The pitch in 5-8 words.
- **Value prop**: the verb the headline performs in the reader's world. What changes for them.

```
Headline:    Atlas Migration Acceleration Program
Value prop:  Cut customer migration time from 90 days to 30,
             at no incremental cost to your team.
```

The headline names the thing. The value prop is the promise. Together they form a two-line elevator pitch.

### 5. The Amazon 1-pager and the PR/FAQ lineage

Amazon's narrative-memo culture (banned PowerPoint in 2004; replaced with 1- and 6-page narrative memos read silently for 20-30 minutes at the start of each meeting) has shaped one-pager craft across tech for two decades. The 1-pager is the front door of the longer PR/FAQ (Press Release + Frequently Asked Questions) document used in Amazon's Working Backwards process.

The Amazon 1-pager / PR-style opener:

1. **Heading**: the product or initiative name.
2. **Subheading**: who it's for, in their language.
3. **Summary paragraph (3-4 sentences)**: what it does, who benefits, why now.
4. **Problem paragraph**: the customer's status quo, written from their POV.
5. **Solution paragraph**: how this changes the status quo, with a measurable outcome.
6. **Quote (optional)**: an executive or customer making the value claim concrete.
7. **Call to action**: what the reader does next.

Two non-negotiables in the Amazon tradition: written as full sentences (not bullets); written as if the product already launched (future-perfect tense forces concreteness).

### 6. Bullets vs paragraphs: the choice

A one-pager is rarely all-bullets or all-prose. The choice is per-section.

**Use paragraphs when:**
- The reader needs to see how ideas connect, not just enumerate them
- You're describing a customer's emotional / strategic status quo
- The Amazon 1-pager opener (problem → solution → outcome) — Amazon tradition says full sentences
- Tone matters and bullets would feel transactional

**Use bullets when:**
- Listing 3-7 parallel items where order is enumerative not narrative
- Scannable facts: who, when, cost, risk, status
- The reader is an exec who triages
- A table would be cleaner but won't fit

Heuristic: paragraphs for the upper half (the narrative), bullets for the lower half (the facts and the ask). Mixing them in the same section is usually a sign you haven't decided what that section is doing.

### 7. Section budget for a single page

A single page on US Letter or A4, 10-11pt body type, 1.15 line height, 0.75" margins gives you roughly 550-650 words of usable real estate. Budget like this:

- **Headline + value prop**: 20-30 words
- **Above-the-fold callout**: 15-25 words
- **Body — problem / context**: 80-120 words
- **Body — solution / approach**: 100-150 words
- **Body — outcomes / proof**: 80-120 words
- **The ask**: 30-50 words
- **Sidebar / facts box** (optional, often a table or 5-bullet block): 60-100 words
- **Footer (owner, date, version, link to longer doc)**: 10-15 words

Anything above 700 words is a two-pager pretending. Cut.

### 8. Design-vs-writing collaboration

A one-pager is half writing, half design. Treat both as load-bearing.

If you have a designer:
- Deliver the headline, value prop, and one-line-summary as final-text before layout starts. These don't move during design.
- Hand off body copy with hierarchy marked: H1, H2, callout, body, caption, footer. Let the designer decide weights, sizes, color.
- Ship a "facts box" / sidebar separately from the body — designers can place it left or right and choose contrast.
- Final-pass review together: does the reader's eye land on the headline first, the visual anchor second, the ask third? If no, redesign.

If you're the designer too:
- Pick one accent color. Two max. Anything more starts to look like a flyer.
- One visual anchor, not three. The eye needs a primary landing pad.
- Typography: one type family with weight contrast (regular vs bold) beats two type families fighting each other.
- Whitespace is not wasted space. A breathing layout reads faster than a packed one.
- Print test: print at 100%. If you can't read the body comfortably arm's length away, the type is too small.

### 9. The five canonical species and when to pick each

- **Account brief** — written for an internal audience to brief them before a customer meeting. Top: account name, owner, ARR, status. Middle: top 3 risks, top 3 opportunities. Bottom: the ask of the reader (intro, exec sponsor, escalation).
- **Project pitch** — written to get funding or headcount. Top: the bet in one sentence. Middle: problem, approach, why now, why us. Bottom: the ask (dollars, people, timeline).
- **Product one-pager** — written for stakeholders evaluating a product. Top: what it is, who it's for. Middle: top 3 capabilities, target outcomes. Bottom: pricing or positioning, link to longer materials.
- **Sales-enablement one-pager** — written for sellers to use in front of buyers. Top: buyer-language value prop. Middle: 3-5 talking points + objection handling. Bottom: links to demos, decks, references.
- **Amazon 1-pager / PR-FAQ opener** — written for internal alignment on a new product or initiative. Top: PR-style headline + subhead. Middle: problem → solution → outcome as prose. Bottom: customer quote + call to action.

Pick the species before you start writing. They are not interchangeable.

### 10. The ask, the owner, the next step

Every one-pager ends with three lines that often get dropped because the writer ran out of room:

1. **The ask** — what specific thing do you need from the reader. Not "support" or "alignment" — a verb with an object.
2. **The owner** — who runs this, with name, role, contact. Even if the reader knows you.
3. **The next step** — when, where, what format. "Decision needed by Fri Aug 14. Meeting on Tue 10am. Reply with go / no-go."

These three sentences are the entire reason the one-pager exists. Cut earlier sections to keep these. Never cut these to keep earlier sections.

## Templates and Examples

### Template: Account brief

```
=================================================================
[ACCOUNT NAME]  |  Brief for [Reader Role]
ARR: $X.XM  •  Renewal: [date]  •  Owner: [name]  •  Updated [date]
=================================================================

ONE-LINE STATUS  >>>  [Account] is at [color/state] heading into
[event/cycle]. The single most important thing the reader should
know is [X].

CONTEXT
[80-120 words on relationship history, current posture, recent
events. Be specific. Name names, dates, dollar amounts.]

TOP 3 RISKS                        TOP 3 OPPORTUNITIES
1. [risk + dollar impact]          1. [opp + dollar potential]
2. [risk]                          2. [opp]
3. [risk]                          3. [opp]

THE ASK
What we need from [reader]: [one-sentence verb-object ask].

NEXT STEP
[Meeting / decision / intro], owned by [name], by [date].

Longer dossier: [link]
```

### Template: Project pitch

```
[Project Name]
[Subhead value prop — what changes for the company if we do this]

>>> If you read one line: [the single bold sentence the reader
    must walk away with]. <<<

THE PROBLEM
[80-120 words. Specific, measurable, dated. Not "engagement is
low" — "free-to-paid conversion fell from 4.1% to 2.7% over Q2".]

THE APPROACH
[100-150 words. The bet. What we'll do, in concrete steps, with
a "why this and not the obvious alternative" sentence.]

EVIDENCE / WHY NOW
- [Data point with source]
- [Comparable that worked elsewhere]
- [Cost of inaction this quarter]

THE ASK
$[X] and [Y] engineers for [Z] weeks. Decision needed by [date].

Owner: [name, role, email]   |   Full plan: [link]
```

### Template: Amazon-style 1-pager (PR opener)

```
[Product / initiative name]
[One-line subhead: who it's for, written in their language]

[Summary paragraph: 3-4 sentences. What it does, who benefits,
why now. Written as if it already launched. Future-perfect tense.]

The problem today
[80-120 words. The status quo from the customer's POV, in their
voice. No insider jargon.]

What changes
[100-150 words. The new state of the world. Concrete. Measurable.
Names the metric that moves.]

"[Customer or exec quote, 1-2 sentences, attributed.]"
— [Name, role]

Next step: [what the reader does. Read the FAQ. Approve the
launch. Greenlight the build. Specific.]
```

### Template: Sales-enablement one-pager

```
[Product]  for  [Buyer Persona]
[Buyer-language value prop — not feature language]

The 3 things buyers care about
1. [Outcome 1]   →   [Capability that delivers it]
2. [Outcome 2]   →   [Capability that delivers it]
3. [Outcome 3]   →   [Capability that delivers it]

Top objections + responses
- "[Objection]" → [One-line response with proof]
- "[Objection]" → [One-line response with proof]
- "[Objection]" → [One-line response with proof]

Proof points
- [Customer name / outcome / metric]
- [Customer name / outcome / metric]

Pricing / packaging: [link]
Demo: [link]   |   Reference list: [link]   |   Deck: [link]
```

### Example: an above-the-fold zone done right

```
=================================================================
ATLAS MIGRATION ACCELERATION PROGRAM
Cut customer migration time from 90 days to 30, at no extra cost.

|--------------------------------------------------------------|
|  If you read one line: this unlocks $4.2M in deferred ARR    |
|  across 14 strategic accounts, with no new headcount needed. |
|--------------------------------------------------------------|

[chart: time-to-migrate distribution before/after, 60% width]
[5-fact callout box, 40% width]
=================================================================
```

The reader scans: name → promise → headline outcome → visual proof + facts. Four seconds. Before reading a single body sentence, they know whether to keep going.

## Anti-Patterns

- **The 1.5-pager** — runs onto a second page by half a paragraph. Cut.
- **The dense wall** — no whitespace, no hierarchy, 10pt body in every section. Reader bails.
- **The mystery headline** — "Project Phoenix: A New Approach". The reader has no idea what changes for them. Rewrite into value-prop language.
- **The ask buried at line 47** — the entire point of the page is the ask. Lead with it or callout-box it.
- **The "alignment" ask** — "We're seeking alignment on..." No verb, no object. Say what you want them to do.
- **The five-color rainbow** — every section a different color, three icon styles, two fonts. Looks like a flyer, not a brief.
- **The PowerPoint-on-one-page** — bulleted top-to-bottom with no narrative, no visual anchor, no "if you only read one line" sentence.
- **Past-tense product description in an Amazon opener** — "we built X" → write it as if X already launched and customers are using it. Future-perfect forces concreteness.
- **The acronym soup** — internal jargon, internal product codenames, internal team names. If the reader isn't on your team, none of these land.
- **The missing owner line** — the page is anonymous; nobody knows who to ask. Cuts your follow-through to zero.
- **The "deck-on-paper"** — the doc is a slide deck exported to PDF and shrunk. Decks are decks; one-pagers are documents. Different format, different physics.

## Decision Heuristics

**Which species is this?**
- Briefing internal people on an external account → account brief
- Asking for money/people → project pitch
- Introducing a product to evaluators → product one-pager
- Arming sellers with talk-tracks → sales-enablement one-pager
- Aligning a team around a new product idea → Amazon 1-pager / PR-FAQ opener

**Bullets or paragraphs?**
- Narrative section (problem, approach) → paragraphs
- Enumerative section (facts, risks, opps, asks) → bullets
- Amazon-style narrative opener → always full sentences
- Sales-enablement → bullets win for scannability

**Should this be a one-pager or a deck?**
- Read async, no presenter, exec triage → one-pager
- Presented live with discussion → deck
- Needs a paper trail and a decision → one-pager
- Needs a story arc with builds → deck

**Should this be a one-pager or a longer doc?**
- The decision can be made in 3 minutes → one-pager
- Needs technical detail or appendix → write the long doc, put a one-pager on top of it
- Distributed across multiple teams who all need different sections → multiple one-pagers, not one long doc

**What goes above the fold?**
- Name of thing
- One-line value prop in reader's language
- "If you only read one line" callout
- One visual anchor (chart, screenshot, diagram)
- Nothing else

**Where do I cut to fit on one page?**
- Cut the second-best example
- Cut the longest paragraph by 30%
- Move evidence into a linked appendix
- Replace prose with a 3-row table if it carries the same facts
- Replace a section heading with a bolded inline phrase
- Never cut the ask, the owner, or the next step

**Should I work with a designer?**
- High-stakes external doc (board, partner, top customer) → yes, always
- Internal briefing for a familiar audience → no, a Google Doc with disciplined formatting is fine
- Sales-enablement doc that will be reused → yes, designer makes it durable
