## 1. Overview — Why SUD Records Get Special Protection

Federal law treats substance use disorder treatment records with **heightened confidentiality** beyond ordinary HIPAA protections. The rationale: fear that treatment records could be used against patients in criminal prosecutions or employment decisions has historically deterred people from seeking help. Congress created Part 2 to remove that deterrent.[^1]

42 CFR Part 2 (originally adopted under the Comprehensive Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism Prevention, Treatment, and Rehabilitation Act of 1970 and the Drug Abuse Office and Treatment Act of 1972) governs **"Part 2 programs"** — federally-assisted programs that provide SUD treatment, counseling, or referral services.[^1]

---

## 2. Who is Covered by 42 CFR Part 2

**Part 2 programs** are:
- Any individual or entity that holds itself out as providing (and actually provides) alcohol or drug abuse diagnosis, treatment, or referral for treatment, AND
- Is **federally assisted** — receives federal funds (directly or indirectly via Medicaid/Medicare), holds a DEA registration, or is tax-exempt under IRS § 501(c)(3), OR is authorized/licensed/certified by the federal government.[^1]

Virtually all licensed NC SUD treatment providers, OTPs, and community mental health centers that take Medicaid are covered. Small private-pay practices without any federal connection may not be.

**Who is NOT covered by Part 2 (but may be by HIPAA):**
- General medical hospitals disclosing records about a patient's SUD to another treating provider (if the hospital is not itself a Part 2 program)
- Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs) in most circumstances
- Records of individuals who are merely identified as having a SUD in a non-Part-2 program context

---

## 3. Part 2 vs. HIPAA — Key Differences

| Feature | HIPAA | 42 CFR Part 2 (post-2024) |
|---|---|---|
| **Treatment, Payment, Healthcare Ops (TPO)** | Allowed without consent | Requires patient consent — but 2024 rule now allows ONE consent covering all future TPO disclosures |
| **Re-disclosure by recipients** | PHI may be re-disclosed by HIPAA-covered recipients | Recipients who got records under TPO consent may re-disclose under HIPAA rules — EXCEPT for use in legal proceedings against the patient |
| **Law enforcement / criminal investigations** | Generally restricted; various exceptions apply | Much stricter — cannot be disclosed to investigate or prosecute patient without written consent OR specific court order |
| **Subpoenas / administrative demands** | Standard administrative subpoenas can compel disclosure | A standard subpoena, warrant, or official request CANNOT compel Part 2 disclosure — a specific Part 2 court order is required |
| **SUD/Psychotherapy notes** | Psychotherapy notes require separate consent | SUD counseling notes require separate consent (new in 2024) |
| **Breach notification** | HIPAA Breach Notification Rule applies | 2024 rule applies HIPAA Breach Notification Rule to Part 2 records |
| **Enforcement** | Civil and criminal penalties | 2024 rule aligned penalties with HIPAA civil/criminal enforcement |
| **Patient accounting of disclosures** | Right exists under HIPAA | 2024 rule added same right for Part 2 disclosures |

**Bottom line:** Part 2 is STRICTER than HIPAA in criminal/law enforcement contexts. Even after the 2024 harmonization, Part 2 records remain off-limits for use against patients in legal proceedings without consent or a qualifying court order.[^2][^3]

---

## 4. Consent Requirements Under Part 2

### Pre-2024: Per-Disclosure Consent
Before 2024, each disclosure required a separate, specific written consent from the patient.

### Post-2024: Single TPO Consent Permitted
The 2024 final rule (effective April 16, 2024; compliance required February 16, 2026) allows a **single consent** covering all future uses and disclosures for **treatment, payment, and healthcare operations (TPO)**.[^2]

**Required consent elements (GS 122C-53 alignment):**
- Name/description of recipient(s)
- Purpose of the disclosure
- How much and what kind of information will be disclosed
- Patient's right to revoke consent and method for doing so
- Expiration date or event (or statement that consent does not expire)
- Patient's signature and date

**Prohibited combinations:**
- A single consent CANNOT combine TPO consent with consent for use in **civil, criminal, administrative, or legislative proceedings** against the patient.
- Consent for **SUD counseling notes** must be **separate** from all other consent.[^2]

---

## 5. SUD Counseling Notes — Extra Protection

**New in 2024:** Analogous to HIPAA's psychotherapy notes protection.[^3]

**Definition:** SUD counseling notes are notes recorded (in any medium) by a Part 2 program provider who is an SUD or mental health professional documenting or analyzing the contents of conversation during a **private, group, joint, or family counseling session** — maintained **separately** from the rest of the patient's record.

**Special rules:**
- Cannot be disclosed under a general TPO consent — require their **own separate consent**.
- Cannot be combined with consent for any other use.
- Subject to the same court-order restrictions as other Part 2 records.

**Practical note:** Providers must decide whether to create and maintain SUD counseling notes separately. If they don't, the content in a combined treatment note is governed by the standard Part 2 rules — not the heightened counseling note rules.

---

## 6. Permitted Disclosures WITHOUT Patient Consent

Even under Part 2, certain disclosures are allowed without consent:[^1][^2]

| Permitted Without Consent | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Medical emergencies** | Where the patient's prior informed consent cannot be obtained and the disclosure is necessary to meet a bona fide medical emergency |
| **Communications within a program** | Among Part 2 program personnel having a need for the information in the performance of duties |
| **Qualified service organizations** | Entities providing services to the program (under a written agreement) |
| **Research** | Under specific research protocol conditions with IRB oversight |
| **Audit and evaluation** | Government agencies conducting program audits; must maintain confidentiality |
| **Public health — de-identified data** | Disclosure to public health authorities, provided records are de-identified per HIPAA standards (new in 2024) |
| **Crime on program premises** | Patient information may be disclosed to law enforcement about a crime committed ON program premises or against program personnel — limited to circumstances of the incident, patient name/address/last known location |
| **Court orders** | Under qualifying Part 2 court orders (see Section 7) |

## 7. Court Orders and Law Enforcement Disclosure

### Standard Subpoenas Do NOT Work

A **general subpoena, search warrant, administrative demand, or official request** does NOT authorize disclosure of Part 2 records. This is a critical difference from most medical records.[^4]

### Part 2-Specific Court Orders (42 CFR § 2.61–2.67)

A court may authorize disclosure of Part 2 records only if it finds **good cause**:[^4]

1. Other ways of obtaining the information are **not available or would not be effective**, AND
2. The **public interest and need** for disclosure **outweigh the potential injury** to the patient, the provider-patient relationship, and treatment services.

### Criminal Investigation/Prosecution — Higher Standard

For disclosures to allow criminal investigation or prosecution OF THE PATIENT, the court must additionally find:[^4]

- The crime involved is **extremely serious** — causing or directly threatening loss of life or serious bodily injury (examples: homicide, rape, kidnapping, armed robbery, assault with a deadly weapon, child abuse).
- The records sought are **material** to the investigation.
- There is no reasonable alternative.

**Even with a valid court order:** The records may still only be used for the specific purpose and by the specific person named in the order.

### NC Integration — GS 122C-53

Under NC law, **GS 122C-53(a)** parallels Part 2's consent requirements. A facility may disclose confidential client information to specified persons with written client consent valid for a specified time period. Unauthorized disclosure is a **Class 3 misdemeanor** with a fine not exceeding $500.[^5]

**Session Law 2023-95** (effective October 1, 2023) amended GS 122C-53(a) to align with HIPAA Subpart E of Part 164, CFR Title 45 — meaning a HIPAA-compliant authorization now satisfies the NC consent requirement for behavioral health record releases.[^5]

---

## 8. 2024 Final Rule — What Changed

**Federal Register:** 89 FR 12472 (February 16, 2024). Effective April 16, 2024. **Compliance deadline: February 16, 2026.**[^2]

### Summary of Key Changes

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| Single TPO consent permitted | Reduces consent burden; patients can sign once for all future TPO disclosures |
| SUD counseling notes defined and protected | New heightened protection analogous to HIPAA psychotherapy notes |
| Re-disclosure under HIPAA permitted (with limits) | Recipients can redisclose under HIPAA — but NOT for proceedings against the patient |
| De-identified data to public health permitted | New without-consent pathway for public health disclosures |
| Penalties aligned with HIPAA | Civil and criminal HIPAA enforcement authorities now apply to Part 2 violations |
| HIPAA Breach Notification Rule applies | Covered entities must report breaches of Part 2 records same as HIPAA breaches |
| Accounting of disclosures right added | Patients can request accounting of Part 2 record disclosures |
| Restriction request right added | Patients can request restrictions on certain disclosures |
| Patient Notice aligned with HIPAA NPP | Part 2 Patient Notice requirements harmonized with HIPAA Notice of Privacy Practices |

### What Did NOT Change

- **Criminal proceedings protection:** Records still cannot be used in legal proceedings against the patient without written consent or a qualifying Part 2 court order.[^2]
- **Standard subpoena prohibition:** A subpoena, warrant, or official demand alone still does not compel Part 2 disclosure.
- **Core program coverage:** The definition of a "Part 2 program" is unchanged.

## 9. NC State Law — GS 122C-52 and 122C-53 (S.L. 2023-95)

**GS 122C-52** establishes the **right to confidentiality** for clients of NC behavioral health facilities — covering mental health, developmental disabilities, and SUD services.[^5]

**GS 122C-53** governs **when facilities may disclose** confidential client information:
- Written consent required, specifying recipient and purpose
- Post-S.L. 2023-95 (effective Oct. 1, 2023): HIPAA-compliant authorization satisfies the NC consent requirement
- Penalties for unauthorized disclosure: **Class 3 misdemeanor**, fine up to $500

**Interaction:** For records from Part 2 programs, BOTH federal Part 2 AND NC GS 122C-52/53 apply. The MORE RESTRICTIVE standard controls. In practice, Part 2's restrictions on law enforcement disclosure typically exceed NC's baseline requirements.

---

## 10. Patient Rights Under Part 2

**Post-2024 final rule:**
- Right to receive a **Patient Notice** (analogous to HIPAA Notice of Privacy Practices) explaining how records may be used/disclosed
- Right to **revoke consent** at any time
- Right to an **accounting of disclosures**
- Right to **request restrictions** on certain disclosures
- Right to be free from having records used against them in legal proceedings without consent or a qualifying court order

## 11. Anti-Patterns / Common Misunderstandings

**"A subpoena is enough to get SUD treatment records"**
No. A standard subpoena, even from a prosecutor or grand jury, does NOT authorize disclosure of Part 2 records. A specific Part 2 court order under 42 CFR § 2.61–2.67 is required.[^4]

**"Part 2 and HIPAA are basically the same now"**
No. The 2024 rule brought Part 2 closer to HIPAA for TPO purposes, but Part 2 remains stricter in the criminal/law enforcement context — records still cannot be used against patients in legal proceedings without consent or a qualifying court order, even if the records were lawfully received under a TPO consent.[^2]

**"If a patient signs a HIPAA authorization, that covers Part 2 records too"**
No (with limited exceptions). Part 2 consent requires specific elements beyond a standard HIPAA authorization. However, after S.L. 2023-95 in NC, a HIPAA-compliant authorization meets NC GS 122C-53 state law requirements — but does not automatically meet Part 2 federal requirements for all purposes.[^5]

**"The 2024 final rule means providers must comply now"**
The final rule became effective April 16, 2024, but the **compliance deadline is February 16, 2026**. Some providers have updated early; others are still implementing. Policies and procedures still being revised.[^2]

**"SUD records are protected by HIPAA's psychotherapy notes rules"**
Not exactly — HIPAA's psychotherapy notes rules protect mental health therapy notes. Part 2 separately protects SUD treatment records, and the 2024 rule added an ANALOGOUS (but distinct) protection for SUD counseling notes. They are parallel regimes, not the same rule.[^3]
