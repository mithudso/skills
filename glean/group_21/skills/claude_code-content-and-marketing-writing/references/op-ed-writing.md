# Op-ed writing

## Overview

The op-ed — short for "opposite the editorial page," a 1970 New York Times innovation — is the **shortest serious argument form in public writing**. In ~750 words you must make a single argument that a previously-uninterested reader believes by the end. No section headers. No bullet lists. No charts. No second draft once it's filed. Just a thesis, the case for it, the case against it, and a reason the reader should do something about it now.

The hardest constraint is also the most clarifying: **one argument**. A piece that tries to make two arguments makes neither. A piece that catalogues five concerns is a blog post. A piece that summarizes everyone's view is a news analysis. An op-ed picks a hill and dies on it.

This skill is for writing the artifact — the 750-word piece you submit to an editor. It is distinct from a **whitepaper** (no business pitch, no 8,000-word format), a **founder/shareholder letter** (no internal-stakeholder framing, no "as our CEO I want to say"), a **company blog post** (no time-pressed argument, no public-debate posture), and **executive thought-leadership ghostwriting** that is really PR with extra steps. If what you're writing is really an ad for your company, it will not be published as an op-ed at a serious outlet.

## Core concepts

### 1. Word count and the 750-word discipline

Standard target: **750 words**. New York Times caps at 800. Washington Post caps at 800. Most regional dailies sit at 600–700. Industry trades (TechCrunch, Stat, Defense One) range from 700 to 1,200, but the discipline is the same.

The word count is the constraint that makes the form work. At 750 words you cannot dump every consideration. You must pick: one thesis, two or three supporting beats, one counter-argument, one call to action. Anything else gets cut. A 1,500-word draft is the wrong unit of work — get to 1,000, then cut to 750, and the piece will be better at every cut.

### 2. The single thesis

The thesis is one sentence. It is a claim someone could disagree with. It is the sentence the reader should remember a week after reading. Tests for a strong op-ed thesis:

- **Disagreeable.** "Schools should teach reading" is not a thesis; nobody disagrees. "Schools should drop phonics and return to whole-language instruction" is a thesis.
- **Singular.** "We need to fix housing, healthcare, and education" is three theses. Pick one.
- **Provable in 750 words.** "Climate change is real" is too big. "California's wildfire insurance market will collapse in 18 months without state intervention" fits.
- **Not a question.** "Should we rethink immigration?" is not a thesis. "We should expand the H-1B cap by 50%" is.
- **Front-loaded.** The thesis appears in the first paragraph, often the last sentence of the first paragraph. The Times explicitly requires this: "Clearly state the issue and your thesis in the opening paragraph."

### 3. "Why now" — the news peg

Op-eds are not evergreen. Every published op-ed has a reason it ran *this week*, not next month. The "why now" — sometimes called the news peg — is the most common reason editors reject otherwise-good pieces. Acceptable pegs:

- **A news event.** A bill introduced, a court ruling, an election result, a corporate announcement, a disaster, a death.
- **An anniversary.** Five years since the pandemic, ten years since a landmark case.
- **A data release.** A new study, a government report, a quarterly metric.
- **A cultural moment.** A book release, a film, a viral incident.
- **A counter to recent coverage.** "Last week the Times argued X. Here is why X is wrong." (Use sparingly; editors at the outlet you're countering are reading this.)

Important distinction: the op-ed "why now" is **why this argument matters this week**, not "why I'm releasing this product this week" (that's a launch post's why-now — see release-blog-and-launch-narrative). If your why-now is "because my company just shipped something," you are pitching the wrong form to the wrong outlet.

The why-now usually appears in paragraph 2 or 3, after the thesis. It can be implicit if the news event is unmissable.

### 4. The authority paragraph — "why I'm qualified to say this"

Every op-ed earns the reader's attention by establishing why this writer, on this topic, is worth reading. The authority paragraph is usually 1–3 sentences, placed in paragraph 2 or 3, sometimes folded into the byline tagline. It answers: what do you know that the general reader doesn't?

Forms of authority editors accept:
- **Direct professional experience.** "I spent fifteen years as an immigration attorney representing H-1B applicants."
- **Subject of the issue.** "I am one of the 8 million Americans whose student loans are now in default."
- **Documented expertise.** "I have published three peer-reviewed studies on the failure rates of these systems."
- **Reporting access.** "I have spent the last six months interviewing the engineers who designed the system."

Forms of authority that fail:
- **Generic credentials.** "I am a thought leader in this space."
- **Title without specifics.** "As a CEO, I want to say..."
- **No authority claim at all.** The reader has no reason to weight your view.

The authority paragraph is also the place to **disclose conflicts**. If your company has a stake in the outcome you're arguing for, say so. Editors will catch undisclosed conflicts and pull the piece; readers who find out later will discount everything you've ever written.

### 5. Structure: the 8-paragraph skeleton

A working 750-word op-ed almost always lays out roughly as follows. Paragraph counts are approximate; paragraphs in op-eds run 60–120 words.

1. **Hook + thesis** (80–120 words). One concrete image, anecdote, or fact that opens the door; the thesis in the last sentence.
2. **Why now** (60–100 words). The news peg or precipitating event.
3. **Authority** (40–80 words). Why the reader should listen to you. Can be folded into paragraph 2.
4. **Evidence beat 1** (80–120 words). The first reason the thesis is correct, with specific support.
5. **Evidence beat 2** (80–120 words). The second reason, ideally orthogonal to the first.
6. **Counter-argument and rebuttal** (100–150 words). The strongest objection, named and answered.
7. **Stakes / consequence** (60–100 words). What happens if we ignore this.
8. **Call to action / kicker** (50–100 words). The specific thing the reader (or a policymaker, or an industry) should do, plus a closing image or line.

The Washington Post's editorial guidance: provide your whole argument before counter-arguments. The OpEd Project teaches the same: don't lead with what your opponents say. You earn the right to handle their argument by making yours first.

### 6. Counter-argument acknowledgment

The piece must name the strongest objection to your thesis and answer it. Not the weakest objection — the strongest. Editors at serious outlets check for this. A piece that doesn't name the obvious counter-argument reads as either naive or dishonest, and gets rejected.

Two failure modes:

- **Strawman counter.** Picking a weak version of the opposing view to knock it down. Readers recognize this and disengage.
- **Both-sides-ism.** Treating the counter so generously that you've now made the opposing case better than your own. The op-ed is an argument; the counter exists to be answered, not to be performed.

The right form: state the counter clearly enough that someone holding it would say "yes, that's my view." Then explain — with evidence, not just assertion — why it's wrong, incomplete, or outweighed.

### 7. Evidence selection

Evidence in an op-ed is brutally selective. You have room for 2–4 evidence claims at most. Each one must be:

- **Concrete.** A number, a named case, a specific policy, a documented incident. Not "many experts" or "studies show."
- **Recent.** Data from the last 1–3 years unless you're making a historical argument.
- **Citable.** Trish Hall's rule from her years as the NYT op-ed editor: every factual claim must be checkable. Fact-checkers at major outlets will check, and one bad number kills the piece.
- **Argument-shaped.** Evidence that supports the thesis. Not throat-clearing, not context for its own sake.

Hall also reminds writers — backed by persuasion psychology — that facts alone don't change minds. **Pair every evidence beat with a stake or an emotional referent**. Not "premiums rose 23%" but "premiums rose 23% — meaning the average family in Sacramento now pays more for insurance than for their mortgage."

### 8. Voice and register

Op-eds are written in **first person, conversational-but-controlled prose**. Not academic ("This essay shall argue..."). Not corporate ("It is our perspective that..."). Not Twitter ("So basically..."). The register sits between a strong newspaper feature and a smart letter to a friend who happens to disagree with you.

Specific moves:
- **First person, used sparingly.** "I" appears 2–4 times in a typical piece. Mostly in the authority paragraph and the kicker.
- **Short sentences for emphasis. Longer ones for argument.**
- **Direct verbs.** Not "There is a need to consider whether..." but "We should consider..."
- **No jargon.** A reader in your field is not the target reader. A smart non-specialist is.
- **No hedging escalators.** "It might possibly be the case that perhaps..." reads as either fear or padding. Make the claim or cut it.
- **No headers, no bullet lists.** The form is continuous prose. If you can't make the argument flow as prose, the argument isn't there yet.

### 9. The call-to-action close

Every op-ed ends with a **specific actionable claim** about what should happen next. Not "we must have a conversation." Not "let us hope." Actionable means:

- A policy that should pass or fail.
- A practice an industry should adopt or abandon.
- A vote a reader should cast.
- An institution that should change a specific procedure.
- A piece of evidence the reader should now go look at.

The kicker — the final sentence — usually does one of three things: it returns to the hook of paragraph 1 (callback), it crystallizes the thesis in one sharp line, or it raises the stakes one last time. The line the reader keeps is the closing line; don't waste it on housekeeping.

### 10. Pitch and submission mechanics

The piece exists to be published; the submission is part of the craft.

- **Target one outlet at a time.** Op-ed submissions are exclusive by default. Submitting to multiple outlets simultaneously is a fast way to be blacklisted.
- **Submit the full piece, not a pitch.** Most major outlets (NYT, WaPo, WSJ, Atlantic guest essays) want the finished piece in the email body. Some allow a 2-sentence pitch above it.
- **Subject line.** "Op-ed submission: [4-7 word headline]." Editors scan inboxes; clarity beats cleverness.
- **Bio.** 25–40 words at the bottom. Name, title, affiliation, one credential relevant to the topic, a recent book or piece if applicable.
- **Disclosure.** Any financial or institutional stake in the outcome.
- **Expect silence.** NYT receives ~2,000 op-eds per week and prints maybe 12. Standard expectation is no response within 3 business days = pass. Then you can submit elsewhere.
- **One round of edits.** If accepted, the editor will send edits. Argue for the ones that matter; accept the ones that don't. Heavy edits are normal at every major outlet.

## Templates and examples

### Op-ed skeleton (with word counts)

```
[¶1 — Hook + thesis | 80-120 words]
  - Concrete image, recent event, or counter-conventional fact (1-2 sentences)
  - The thesis in 1 sentence (the LAST sentence of ¶1)

[¶2 — Why now | 60-100 words]
  - The news peg
  - Brief stakes-setting

[¶3 — Authority | 40-80 words]
  - Why the reader should listen to YOU on this
  - Disclosure if relevant

[¶4 — Evidence beat 1 | 80-120 words]
  - Specific data point or case
  - Paired with a stake the reader can feel

[¶5 — Evidence beat 2 | 80-120 words]
  - Different angle on the thesis
  - Specific support

[¶6 — Counter-argument | 100-150 words]
  - The strongest objection, stated fairly
  - The answer, with evidence (not assertion)

[¶7 — Stakes / consequence | 60-100 words]
  - What happens if we don't act
  - Forward-looking

[¶8 — Call to action + kicker | 50-100 words]
  - Specific actionable claim
  - Closing line: callback OR crystallizing line OR raised stakes
```

### Sample hook patterns

- **The concrete-fact opener.** "Last Tuesday, the state of California paid more in wildfire-insurance subsidies than it collected in property tax from the affected counties combined."
- **The anecdote opener.** "When my client received her H-1B denial letter in March, she had been working at her company for eleven years."
- **The contrarian-claim opener.** "Every honest economist I know agrees on one thing about minimum wage research. They just won't say it in public."
- **The first-person-stake opener.** "I have spent the last six months trying to renew my flood insurance. I have failed."

### Sample call-to-action lines

- "The committee meets again on June 12. It should vote no."
- "The fix is not complicated. It requires three sentences in the next FAA reauthorization bill."
- "Until that happens, the rest of us are paying for a system that doesn't work."
- "Read the dissent. It will be the majority opinion within ten years."

### A worked thesis-to-skeleton example

**Topic:** Database security audits at small companies.

**Thesis (rejected):** "Database security is important."  → not disagreeable.

**Thesis (better):** "Small SaaS companies should be required by their cyber-insurance carriers to run quarterly database audits."  → disagreeable, specific, actionable.

**Why now:** Recent breach at [named company] traced to an un-audited database. Insurance industry meets next month to revise underwriting standards.

**Authority:** "I have audited 200+ production databases at small SaaS companies in the last three years."

**Evidence beats:** (1) Audit findings data — what % of un-audited dbs have critical flaws. (2) Cost comparison — quarterly audit vs. one breach.

**Counter:** "This is regulatory overreach by insurance companies." Answer: Insurance is private; carriers already underwrite for fire suppression, why not this.

**Stakes:** Without the change, the next breach is statistically certain.

**CTA:** Insurance industry should adopt the standard at the June meeting.

## Anti-patterns

- **The "we need a conversation" piece.** Ends with a call for dialogue instead of an actionable claim. Tells the editor and the reader that the writer has no actual position.
- **The five-thesis piece.** Tries to argue about housing, healthcare, education, climate, and democracy in 800 words. Picks none; persuades on none.
- **The disguised press release.** "Our company has been working on this issue for years..." Reads as marketing; gets rejected on sight at any serious outlet.
- **The credential-stack opener.** "As a Harvard-trained economist with twenty years on Wall Street..." Authority belongs in paragraph 2 or 3, not paragraph 1. Paragraph 1 is for the hook and thesis.
- **The strawman counter.** Picks a fringe version of the opposing view. Smart readers spot it instantly.
- **The both-sides-ism collapse.** Makes the counter-argument so well that you've defeated your own thesis.
- **The throat-clearing intro.** "In recent years, much has been written about..." Cut the first paragraph; the piece almost always starts in paragraph 2.
- **Hedging escalators.** "It might be the case that perhaps we should consider whether possibly..." Cut every hedge that doesn't carry actual uncertainty.
- **The data dump.** Eight statistics in two paragraphs. Pick the two that matter; ditch the rest.
- **The evergreen piece.** No news peg. Editors reject these almost without reading. If you can't say why this needs to run *this week*, it doesn't.
- **The "Some say... others say..." structure.** That's news analysis, not opinion. An op-ed has a writer who has a view.
- **The headers-and-bullets piece.** That's a blog post or a memo. Op-eds are continuous prose.
- **The 1,400-word "short" version.** If you can't get it to 800, you don't have one argument; you have two.

## Decision heuristics

- **Is this really an op-ed?** If it's an ad for your product, write a launch post. If it's for internal stakeholders, write a founder letter. If it's exhaustive, write a whitepaper. If it's a take with no policy claim, write a Substack.
- **Can the thesis be stated in one sentence that someone could disagree with?** If not, sharpen the thesis before drafting.
- **Why this week?** If you can't answer in one sentence, the piece is not yet ready.
- **What's the strongest counter?** Name it. Write it down. If you can't answer it, the thesis is wrong or you need more reporting.
- **Who's the target outlet?** NYT, WaPo, regional daily, industry trade — each has slightly different conventions. Read three recent op-eds from your target outlet before drafting.
- **Are you the right author?** If the byline doesn't carry credibility on the topic, the piece won't run. Consider partnering with a co-author whose authority lands.
- **Is there a CTA?** If the piece ends in "we must reflect," you don't have an op-ed. You have a sigh.
- **Have you cut it to 750?** A 750-word version of a 1,200-word draft is almost always sharper. Try it before defending the longer one.
- **Where's the kicker?** The last sentence is the line readers remember. If yours is housekeeping, rewrite it.
