# Profile writing

## Overview

A journalistic profile is not a biography, a hagiography, a resume in prose, or a Q&A. It is a **narrative reported piece** that uses reporting, scene, anecdote, and selected detail to reveal something true about a person — usually something the subject themselves would not, or could not, articulate. The reader finishes a great profile knowing the subject in a way the subject's own social-media bio could never convey.

Three pieces of writing define the modern profile tradition and are still worth study every time you sit down to write one: Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (Esquire, 1966) for what scene-based reporting looks like at its peak; Janet Malcolm's "The Journalist and the Murderer" (The New Yorker, 1989) for the ethics of the form; and the body of work Walt Harrington did at the Washington Post Magazine and codified in *Intimate Journalism* — the discipline of "deep, not wide" reporting on ordinary lives.

This skill is for the **finished prose**. If you need to plan or run the interview itself, use `interview-and-conversational`. If you're writing a customer story for marketing purposes, use `case-study-writing`.

## Core concepts

### 1. The profile is not the subject's biography

A common amateur mistake: chronological recitation. Born here, went to school here, took first job here, married, had kids, got promoted. This produces a Wikipedia entry, not a profile. The profile selects from a life — often from a single recent moment in a life — and uses that selection to reveal character. Talese followed Frank Sinatra for three months and never got the interview. The piece is about what surrounded Sinatra, not what Sinatra said. It works because the *constellation* of details reveals the man.

The biographer's question: what happened? The profile writer's question: **what is this person actually like, and how do I show it?**

### 2. The five-part structure

Most profiles published in *The New Yorker*, *The Atlantic*, *Esquire*, the *Washington Post Magazine*, and *New York Magazine* follow a variation of this five-part skeleton:

1. **Lead (lede).** A scene, anecdote, or paradox that introduces the subject in a specific moment. Usually present-tense or recent-past. 1–3 paragraphs.
2. **Nut graph (nutgraf).** The "why this story now" paragraph. Answers: who is this person, why are we reading about them, what's the larger context. 1 paragraph, usually after the lede but sometimes delayed 4–5 paragraphs in a "delayed nut" structure.
3. **Context / background.** What the reader needs to know about the world the subject operates in — the industry, the historical moment, the precedent. 2–4 paragraphs.
4. **Biography and present moment.** Selected biographical material — *not* a full chronology, just the parts that explain the present. Interleaved with scenes from current reporting. The bulk of the piece (often 60-70% of word count).
5. **Kicker.** The closing scene or quote. Often a callback to the lede that lands differently because of what the reader now knows. 1–3 paragraphs.

The structure is not a formula to follow rigidly — it's a load-bearing skeleton you can reshape but should not omit. If a piece has no nut graph the reader doesn't know why they're reading. If it has no kicker the piece dribbles out.

### 3. Lede patterns

Three dominant opening patterns, each suited to different subjects:

**In-scene lede.** Open inside a moment. The subject is doing something — walking into a room, working on something, mid-conversation. The reader is dropped into observation without narration. This is Talese on Sinatra at Jilly's; this is Walt Harrington at a barbershop. Best when you have access and concrete sensory detail. Bad when you don't have access and are tempted to invent.

**Anecdotal lede.** Open with a story — a discrete past event that illustrates something about the subject. "When she was twelve, she..." or "The day her company nearly collapsed, she..." Compresses biography into a parable. Best when one story does the work of fifty.

**Paradoxical lede.** Open with the contradiction at the heart of the subject. "He has spent thirty years arguing against the institution he now runs." Good for subjects whose tension *is* the story. Risk: it can read as cleverness if the body doesn't earn it.

A fourth pattern, the **delayed lede**, mixes any of the three with a deferred nutgraf — the reader is in scene or in anecdote for 600+ words before the explanation arrives. Used by *The New Yorker* and *The Atlantic*. High-skill move; novices should not attempt.

What never works: opening with the subject's birth, their resume, or their official title. "John Smith, the 47-year-old CEO of Acme Inc., is an unusual leader" is the death-rattle of profile prose.

### 4. The nutgraf and its placement

The "nut graph" — a term from *The Wall Street Journal* in the 1980s — is the paragraph that tells the reader what the piece is about and why they should keep reading. In a 4,000-word *New Yorker* profile, you may not get to the nutgraf for 800 words. In a 1,200-word newspaper profile, the nutgraf is usually paragraph 2 or 3. The rule is: **the nut comes early enough that the reader doesn't bail, late enough that the lede has done its work**.

A nutgraf almost always answers:
- Who is this person? (One-sentence positioning.)
- Why now? (What just happened that makes them newsworthy.)
- What does this piece argue or reveal? (The implicit thesis.)

It does *not* answer:
- The whole story (that's the body).
- Why you, the writer, decided to write this (that's a memoir).

### 5. Show in scene, summarize in transit

Walt Harrington's craft rule, paraphrased from *Intimate Journalism*: **render the important moments as scenes; summarize everything in between**. A profile is not a transcript. The reader doesn't need to know every meeting you sat in on; they need to be in the room for the meeting where the subject lost their composure.

Practical application: pick 3–5 scenes that do narrative work — they reveal character, advance the implicit argument, or escalate the stakes. Render those in close, sensory, present-tense detail. Everything else — the years between scenes, the context, the historical setup — is summary prose. The rhythm of a profile is **scene → summary → scene → summary → scene → kicker**. If you find yourself summarizing what should have been a scene, you didn't report enough. If you find yourself scening what should be summary, you're padding.

### 6. The interview-to-prose distillation

The single biggest amateur mistake: putting too much interview material in the piece. A 90-minute interview yields perhaps 50–80 quotable utterances. A great profile uses 4–8 of them. The rest becomes context the writer absorbs and renders in their own voice.

Discipline:

- **Quote sparingly.** Direct quotes should do work no paraphrase could do — voice, idiosyncrasy, surprise, contradiction, signature phrase. If a quote could be paraphrased without loss, paraphrase it.
- **Never start a paragraph with a long quote.** The reader needs you, the writer, to frame the quote first.
- **Don't dialogue-dump.** Long stretches of Q&A back-and-forth almost always belong in summary or in a separately-published interview transcript.
- **Action over speech.** What the subject *does* in your presence beats what they *say* about themselves nine times out of ten.

Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" famously contains almost no direct quotes from Sinatra. The piece is a tour de force of action and observation.

### 7. The reporting requirement

A profile cannot be written from public sources. If you have not been in a room with the subject — or, failing that, have not spent significant time with people who have — the resulting piece will read as thin no matter how well-written. Profile writing is a **reported form**. Minimum table stakes for a serious profile:

- 1–3 long-form interviews with the subject (90+ minutes each).
- 5–15 interviews with people who know the subject (family, colleagues, rivals, mentors).
- Direct observation of the subject in their normal environment.
- Document review — letters, emails, prior published work, court filings, financial disclosures where relevant.
- Fact-checking discipline (see concept 9).

If you can't get the subject — the Talese "write-around" — you can still do the piece, but you must triple the reporting from people around them. A write-around profile of a subject with no surrounding cooperation is a hatchet job in disguise.

### 8. The ethical posture

Janet Malcolm opened *The Journalist and the Murderer* with the most-quoted sentence in profile ethics: "Every journalist who is not too stupid or too full of himself to notice what is going on knows that what he does is morally indefensible." Malcolm's point — overstated for effect — is that the profile relationship is **structurally asymmetric**. The subject grants access believing one story will be told; the writer often tells a different story. There is no fully ethical exit from this asymmetry, but there are ground rules:

- **No deception about the assignment.** The subject should know who the publication is, what the piece is about, and that quotes are on the record unless explicitly agreed otherwise.
- **No fabricated composites.** Don't merge two people into one character. Don't invent dialogue. Don't reorder events to improve drama.
- **No quote-clean-up that changes meaning.** Light grammatical edits to spoken English are conventional. Inventing words, changing tense to alter meaning, or surgically removing context is not.
- **No surprise ambushes in the final piece.** If you discovered something material the subject would want to address, give them the chance. (Standard newsroom practice: a "no surprises" callback before publication.)
- **No quote approval.** You can fact-check specific claims with the subject; you do not let them rewrite the piece.

Malcolm's harder claim — that the writer is always *betraying* the subject — is worth sitting with even when you reject it. The form runs on access that is granted in trust, and the prose can never fully respect that trust without becoming a press release.

### 9. Fact-checking discipline

In the *New Yorker* / *Atlantic* / *Harper's* tradition, every factual claim in a profile is checked. Names (spelling, middle initial). Dates. Places. Quoted statements (against recording or notes). Numbers (revenue, ages, distances, costs). Descriptive claims about appearance, clothing, room contents. Anecdotal claims (was the subject *actually* at the place they said they were on the date they said?).

For a serious profile:

- Tape every interview. Transcribe (or pay a service).
- Keep a fact log: every claim → source. Two independent sources for any contested claim.
- For claims about people other than the subject, contact those people for confirmation or comment.
- For damaging claims, give the subject the opportunity to respond before publication.
- After the piece is drafted, have a fact-checker (or yourself, in a fresh pass) verify every name, date, place, and quote independently.

If the publication doesn't have fact-checkers (most don't, outside a small set of legacy magazines), you are the fact-checker. The reader can't tell the difference, but the libel lawyer can.

### 10. The kicker

The kicker — the closing scene or paragraph — is the line the reader remembers. Three patterns:

- **Callback.** The lede scene returns, but the reader now understands it differently. Talese ends "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" with Sinatra leaving a casino at dawn — a mirror of the opening, but loaded with everything the reader has absorbed.
- **Crystallizing quote.** A single line from the subject that compresses the piece's argument. Rarely the most "important" thing the subject said — usually a quiet line you nearly cut.
- **Forward-look.** A small concrete detail of the subject's near future — a flight they're about to take, a meeting they're about to walk into. Implies continuation; the story doesn't end, the piece does.

What doesn't work as a kicker: a summary paragraph ("So who is John Smith? He is many things..."), a writer's interpretation moment, a quote that's already been used in the piece.

## Templates and examples

### Profile skeleton (≈3,500-word feature)

```
[Lede — 200-400 words]
  Scene or anecdote. Subject in motion. Sensory detail.
  No biographical exposition yet.

[Nutgraf — 100-200 words]
  Who. Why now. What this piece reveals.

[Context — 300-500 words]
  The world the subject operates in.
  The historical or industry backdrop.

[First biographical pass — 400-700 words]
  Selected origin material. Not chronological recitation —
  the 2-3 formative moments that explain the present.

[Scene 2 — 300-500 words]
  Subject in action again. Different setting from the lede.

[Second biographical pass — 400-700 words]
  Career middle, key turning points.

[Tension / complication — 400-600 words]
  The contradiction, the critic, the unresolved thing.
  Other-voice reporting lives here.

[Scene 3 — 300-500 words]
  The hardest scene to get. Often the most revealing.

[Forward-look paragraph — 100-200 words]
  Where the subject is going next.

[Kicker — 100-300 words]
  Callback or crystallizing image. The line the reader keeps.
```

### Skeleton lede (in-scene)

> The first thing he does, every morning, is open the same window. The view hasn't changed in thirty-two years — the parking lot, the dry-cleaner across the street, the two dumpsters — and he hasn't either, in his own telling. The rest of the building has. The company he runs used to fit on one floor of this building. Now it occupies four, and the dry-cleaner is gone, and the dumpsters belong to him.

(Establishes: present-tense observation; specific sensory detail; implicit tension between "hasn't changed" and the reality that everything has; setup for a nutgraf about what the company became.)

### Skeleton nutgraf

> Smith, 58, took over a struggling regional logistics firm in 1994 and turned it into the third-largest carrier in the Pacific Northwest. He has done it, his colleagues say, by being almost pathologically resistant to change — a posture that has saved the company twice and nearly killed it once. This year, with the industry tilting toward automation he has openly resisted, his board is asking him a question they have not asked before: whether the same instinct that built the company can keep it from losing the next decade.

(Establishes: who, age, position, the news peg, the implicit thesis, the conflict.)

### Skeleton kicker (callback)

> He closes the window before he leaves. The parking lot, the empty dumpster pad, the building across the street that will, in six months, be torn down. He locks the office at 6:14 — the same time as every other night, the same time as the year he bought the company — and walks down four flights of stairs because the elevator, in his telling, makes him soft.

(Calls back to the opening; reader now reads "the same time as every other night" with the knowledge of what the board is asking him.)

## Anti-patterns

- **The hagiography.** Profile reads as press release. Every quote from the subject is laudatory; no critical voice appears. Loss of credibility happens around word 400.
- **The hit piece.** Profile reads as prosecution. Every detail bends toward the predetermined verdict. Subject's voice is reduced to incriminating snippets. Equally damaging to credibility.
- **The resume in prose.** "After graduating from State University in 1991, Smith took his first job at..." Chronological recitation with no scene, no character, no argument.
- **The Q&A in disguise.** Long stretches of "I asked him about X. He said: '[300 words].' I asked him about Y. He said: '[300 words].'" That's a transcript, not a profile.
- **The writer in the foreground.** "When I first met Smith, I expected..." Used sparingly, the writer's presence is honest. Used throughout, it makes the piece about the writer.
- **The unattributed psychoanalysis.** "Smith is a deeply lonely man." On whose authority? Either show it in scene or attribute it to someone who would know.
- **The unmoored quote.** A perfect quote floating in the middle of the page with no setup, no scene, no narrative reason to be there. Quote does work; quote in vacuum is filler.
- **The exit-interview ending.** Closing line is a summary paragraph or a polite send-off. Earn the kicker.
- **Composite characters / invented dialogue.** Fabrication, full stop. Career-ending if discovered.
- **The thin reporting tell.** Adjectives doing the work that reporting should do. "Smith is a complex, demanding, mercurial boss" tells you the writer didn't get the scene that would show it.

## Decision heuristics

- **Who is this piece for?** Magazine readers will tolerate a 600-word delayed lede; newspaper readers want the nutgraf in paragraph 2. Match the channel.
- **What's the news peg?** If there isn't one — "I just find them interesting" — the nutgraf will collapse. Find the present-tense reason this piece exists now.
- **How much access did you get?** A lot → in-scene lede. A little → anecdotal lede plus other-voice reporting. None → write-around or don't write it.
- **Is there a contradiction at the center?** If yes, that's the spine of the piece. The lede sets it up; the body explores it; the kicker lands it.
- **How many scenes do you have?** Three is the floor for a 3,000+ word piece. If you have fewer, report more before writing.
- **Are you tempted to write a hagiography or a hit piece?** That's a tell that you've over-identified with the subject (or with their adversary). Step back. Find the third voice.
- **Have you fact-checked?** Names, dates, quotes, descriptive details. If you can't show your fact log, you're not done.
- **Does the kicker call back to the lede or compress the argument?** If not, you have a closing paragraph, not a kicker.
