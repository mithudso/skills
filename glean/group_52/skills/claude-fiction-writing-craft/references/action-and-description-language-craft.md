# Action & Description Language Craft

Action prose fails by default because the easiest way to write a fight, chase, or tense physical sequence is to narrate it the way you'd write stage directions in a script: subject, verb, object, next subject, verb, object. "He raised his fist. He swung it at her jaw. She ducked. She stepped back." Every sentence is true, every movement is accounted for, and the passage is still lifeless, because a real body in a real crisis does not experience itself as a sequence of discrete, evenly-weighted actions. It experiences itself as a rush of partial, overlapping sensation: a smell that arrives before the sight does, a muscle that fires before the decision to fire it registers, a sentence-fragment of panic that cuts across the movement instead of following it. Five levers fix this, and they are the same five levers regardless of whether the scene is a swordfight, a car chase, a bar brawl, or someone frantically defusing a device: choose verbs that carry the force of the action inside the word itself; vary sentence length so the rhythm of the prose enacts the pacing of the event; strip out the mechanical blow-by-blow choreography that a screenplay needs and a novel doesn't; ground the scene in senses other than sight; and let interiority interrupt the action instead of pausing to summarize it. The sixth lever, filter words, is really a special case of the first four: it's the habit of narrating perception ("she saw," "he felt") instead of narrating the thing perceived, and cutting it tightens almost every other technique in this file.

## Core Concepts

### 1. Strong, specific verbs vs. weak verb + adverb constructions

A verb that already contains the manner of the action does more work in less space than a generic verb propped up by an adverb. "He walked quickly" and "he strode" describe the same action, but "strode" spends one word where the other spends two, and it doesn't invite the reader to question whether "quickly" was really the important detail. The same logic applies inside action beats: "hit" is a placeholder verb that needs qualification ("hit hard," "hit clumsily") to mean anything specific, while "pounded," "cracked," "grazed," or "slammed" already tell the reader how much force was involved and often what it sounded or felt like. This is also the substance of Stephen King's advice in *On Writing* that adverbs are a symptom of an underlying weak verb choice — he compares them to dandelions: one on the lawn looks harmless, but they multiply, and a manuscript that leans on adverbs to do a verb's job reads as unrevised.[^1]

- BEFORE: "He walked angrily across the room and hit the table hard with his hand."
- AFTER: "He stormed across the room and slammed the table."
- When to use: any moment where a generic verb (walk, hit, look, move, go) is doing duty for a more specific, already-loaded verb. Don't over-correct into a thesaurus crawl — one precise verb per beat is the goal, not a rare word in every sentence.

### 2. Sentence length and rhythm as a pacing control

Sentence length is a dial, not a decoration: short sentences read fast and land hard, long sentences slow the reader down and create a sense of continuity or reflection, and a run of clauses joined by "and" can mimic a body moving through several actions in one unbroken motion. In high-tension passages, craft guidance converges on keeping the *average* sentence short — some editors suggest averaging under twenty words during a fight or chase — but with variation, because a page of uniformly short sentences produces a machine-gun monotony that exhausts the reader instead of gripping them.[^2] Ursula K. Le Guin's *Steering the Craft* frames this as a matter of sound: prose has to be tested by ear, and pace and forward momentum come from rhythm, not from any single sentence in isolation — the job of a narrative sentence is to propel the reader into the next one.[^3]

- BEFORE (monotone short): "He ducked. He rolled. He came up swinging. She blocked. He staggered."
- AFTER (varied rhythm): "He ducked, rolled, came up swinging — and she blocked it cold, a single flat crack of forearm on forearm that sent a jolt clean to his shoulder. He staggered back, breath ragged, boots skidding on wet stone."
- When to use: shorten sentences at the moment of impact or decision (the punch itself, the swerve, the shot); lengthen them slightly in the half-second before or after, where a character registers what just happened or braces for what's next. Reserve the shortest sentences and fragments for the single most important beat in a passage — if everything is short, nothing reads as urgent.

### 3. Avoiding stage-direction / choreography prose

Stage-direction prose is what happens when a writer tracks every micro-movement of a body the way a screenplay's action line does — because a screenplay has to, since the camera and the actors need literal instructions, but a novel doesn't, because the reader isn't staging the scene, they're experiencing it. The tell is a passage that could be read aloud as literal blocking notes: "Nathan stretched his right arm and extended an index finger" when the sentence only needs to say "Nathan pointed."[^4] The fix is to narrate the meaningful gesture and the felt experience of the movement, not the anatomy of how the gesture was physically executed, and to resist using action beats purely as filler between lines of dialogue.[^5]

- BEFORE: "She reached her right hand toward the doorknob, curled her fingers around it, twisted her wrist, and pulled the door open toward herself."
- AFTER: "She wrenched the door open."
- When to use: any time a passage names more anatomical steps than the reader needs to picture the outcome. Ask what the reader actually needs to know — the result and its emotional weight, not the choreography — and cut to that.

### 4. Multi-sensory grounding beyond vision

Default description defaults to sight because sight is the sense writers (and readers) reach for first, but a body under stress is flooded with input from every channel at once — sound, smell, touch, temperature, taste, and proprioception (the felt sense of where your own limbs are and how they're moving), plus the vestibular sense of balance. Craft guidance on sensory writing consistently flags proprioception and balance as the most neglected senses in fiction, and recommends deliberately writing a beat where a character can't rely on sight — blinded by dust, darkness, blood in the eyes — specifically to force the other senses onto the page.[^6] Layering two or three senses into a single beat, rather than one sense per sentence, is also what makes a passage read as a unified physical experience instead of a checklist.[^7]

- BEFORE: "He saw the alley was dark and narrow, with trash along the walls."
- AFTER: "The alley narrowed to a throat of wet brick; something rotten cut through the cold, and his shoulder scraped stone before his eyes had adjusted enough to place the wall."
- When to use: at scene-openings and at the moment a character's other senses would plausibly outrun their vision — close combat, darkness, panic, disorientation, being struck in the head. Don't force all five senses into every paragraph; two well-chosen non-visual details usually outperform an exhaustive sensory inventory.

### 5. Balancing action beats with interiority

A sequence that is pure external action reads as choreography no matter how well the verbs and rhythm are handled, because the reader has no reason to care who wins; a sequence that stops for extended internal reflection kills the momentum the short sentences were built to create. The working craft heuristic is to keep internal thought fragments as short and reflexive as the physical beats around them — "Don't fall," "Knife," "Get up" — rather than full sentences of analysis, and to use them as connective tissue between physical beats rather than as a pause from them.[^8] A useful mechanical check some writing coaches use is a rule of three: don't run more than two sentences of the same type (pure action, pure interiority, dialogue) in a row before breaking the pattern, since unbroken runs of one mode are what produce a flat, list-like rhythm.[^9]

- BEFORE (no interiority — pure choreography): "He blocked the strike. He countered. She sidestepped. He missed."
- AFTER (interiority woven in): "He blocked the strike — *too slow, she's faster than you thought* — and countered wide. She was already gone, sidestepped clean, and his fist closed on nothing but cold air."
- When to use: insert interiority at the beat where a character's plan changes, fails, or succeeds — the moment that carries stakes — not at every beat. In the fastest, most physically overwhelming instant of a sequence, cut interiority to nothing and let pure sensation carry the line; that absence is itself a pacing choice.

### 6. Filter words and why removing them tightens prose

Filter words are the verbs of perception — saw, heard, felt, noticed, realized, wondered — that insert a narrating layer between the reader and the thing being perceived. "She saw the door was open" reports that a character had a perception; "the door was open" delivers the same fact from inside the character's experience, without reminding the reader that they're watching someone else watch something.[^10] Chuck Palahniuk's widely circulated essay "Nuts and Bolts: 'Thought' Verbs" makes the related, stronger claim that a class of interior-state verbs — thinks, knows, understands, realizes, believes, wants, remembers, imagines, desires, loves, hates — should almost always be replaced with the concrete sensory or behavioral detail that would let a reader infer the interior state themselves, rather than being told it directly.[^11] In action prose specifically, filter words are doubly costly: they add a word to sentences that are supposed to be lean, and they blunt the immediacy that short, direct sentences are trying to create.

- BEFORE: "She felt the floor shake and heard glass breaking somewhere behind her, and she noticed she was already running."
- AFTER: "The floor shook. Glass broke somewhere behind her. She was already running."
- When to use: cut filter words by default in any close-POV action passage; keep them only when the fact that perception itself is uncertain, delayed, or mistaken is the point of the sentence ("she thought she heard footsteps, but it was only rain").

## Anti-Patterns

- **Over-choreographed fight scenes.** Every strike, block, and foot placement is itemized in sequence, producing a passage that reads like a martial-arts instruction manual rather than an experience. Fix: cut to the two or three beats that carry stakes or change the outcome, and compress or omit the connective movement between them.
- **Adverb-crutch action verbs.** "He hit her weakly," "she ran quickly," "he grabbed roughly" — a generic verb propped up with an adverb instead of replaced by a verb that already contains that meaning. Fix: for every adverb+verb pair in an action passage, ask whether a single stronger verb exists (grazed, sprinted, wrenched) and use it; keep the adverb only when no single verb captures the same nuance.
- **Monotone sentence rhythm during action.** A run of same-length, same-structure sentences — usually all short — that starts strong but flattens into a droning cadence the reader tunes out. Fix: read the passage aloud; wherever three sentences in a row have roughly the same length and shape, lengthen or restructure one of them, and reserve the shortest fragments for the single most important beat.
- **Sensory tunnel-vision (visual-only description).** The scene is fully blocked out visually — what everyone is standing where, doing what — with no sound, smell, temperature, or bodily sensation, so the passage reads like a description of a photograph rather than a lived moment. Fix: for each major beat, add at least one non-visual sensory detail, prioritizing sound and touch/proprioception in physical action, and consider a pass where you deliberately black out the character's vision to force the other senses onto the page.
- **Filter-word buildup.** Repeated use of saw/heard/felt/noticed/realized across a passage, which quietly re-inserts narrative distance into a scene that is supposed to feel immediate. Fix: search the passage for these verbs and delete the frame, promoting the perceived detail to the main clause; keep a filter word only where the uncertainty or delay of perception is itself the information being conveyed.
