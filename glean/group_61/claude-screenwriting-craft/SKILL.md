# Screenwriting & Dramatic Writing Craft

Hub for the craft of writing for the screen, film and television. Screen
writing is a distinct medium from prose: it is a blueprint for an audiovisual
experience, read by a small set of industry professionals before it is ever
seen by an audience, and everything on the page must ultimately be filmable.
That constraint (see `screenplay-format-and-externalization`) is the root of
almost every convention in this hub, from format rules to scene economy.

## When to use this skill

See `whenToUse` above. Typical asks: "how do I format a scene heading",
"my script reads too talky, how do I externalize this", "which beat sheet
should I use for this screenplay", "is the one-page-one-minute rule real",
"what's the difference between a spec and a shooting script", "how do I
structure a TV pilot", "how do I adapt this novel into a screenplay".

## When NOT to use this skill

See `whenNotToUse` above and the SKIP line in the description. If the ask
names a novel, short story, or manuscript with **no screen/production
intent**, route to `fiction-writing-craft`. If the ask is specifically about
adapting prose *into* a screenplay, that adaptation craft lives here
(`tv-structure-and-adaptation`), not in `fiction-writing-craft`.

### Screen vs. stage (brief note, not built out)

Stage plays share the externalization constraint (no narrator, no interior
monologue) but differ from screenplays in format and scene economy: stage
dialogue typically carries more of the storytelling load because the camera's
close-up, cut, and visual-metaphor toolkit isn't available, a playwright
leans harder on language and blocking to do what a screenwriter would do with
an image or an edit. Stage format also has no slug-line/scene-heading
convention comparable to INT./EXT., since a single set often stands for an
entire act. This hub does not research playwriting craft in depth; treat this
paragraph as the boundary marker, not a starting point for stage-specific work.
