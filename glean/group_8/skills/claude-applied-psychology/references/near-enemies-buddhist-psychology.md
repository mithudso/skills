# Near Enemies & Far Enemies in Buddhist Psychology

The Visuddhimagga's counterfeit-emotion framework: each of the four brahmaviharas (divine
abidings) has **two enemies · one near, one far**. The near enemy resembles the virtue and
corrupts it from inside; the far enemy is its direct opposite and cannot coexist with it.
Per-claim confidence: **FACT** (3+ independent sources incl. primary text), **QUALIFIED** (2
or bounded), **TENTATIVE** (single-source/contested).

**The single most useful correction this reference carries:** most popular near/far-enemy
lists diverge from the canonical text. What circulates as "the" list is a family of teacher
glosses. Both layers are worth knowing · the canon for precision, the glosses for how the
framework is actually taught.

## 1. Textual origin · what is canonical and what is not

- Locus classicus: **Visuddhimagga chapter IX (Brahmavihāra-niddesa) §§98–101**, Buddhaghosa
  (5th c. CE): "And here each one has two enemies, one near and one far" (Ñāṇamoli
  translation).[^1] Pali: near enemy = **āsannapaccatthika**, far enemy =
  **dūrapaccatthika**.[^2] · **FACT**.
- The defining images: the near enemy "behaves like a foe who keeps close by a man, and it
  easily finds an opportunity"; the far enemy is "like a foe ensconced in a rock
  wilderness."[^1] The often-quoted "**like an enemy masquerading as a friend**" line is NOT
  Buddhaghosa · it is the sub-commentary (Dhammapāla, Vism-mhṭ 309), quoted in Ñāṇamoli's
  footnote. Nearly every modern quotation misattributes it.[^1] · **FACT**.
- **The frame is commentarial, not sutta.** The terms do not appear in the Pali suttas.[^3]
  But the *content* is canonical: the far enemies come from the "escapes" formula (D III
  247–48 · metta escapes ill will, karuṇā cruelty, muditā boredom, upekkhā greed), and three
  of the four near enemies are quotations of **MN 137's** "grief / joy / equanimity **based on
  the home life**" (gehasita) triad.[^1][^4] Buddhaghosa's innovation was the near/far
  *frame*, not the content. · **FACT**.
- Parallel commentarial counterfeit-detection exists elsewhere: the Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha
  tradition's "direct/indirect enemies" (metta: indirect = pema, personal affection); the
  Nettippakaraṇa commentary's "delusion disguised as equanimity… indolence is also likely to
  pretend to be equanimity."[^5] · **QUALIFIED**. Whether the schema predates Buddhaghosa
  (e.g. in the Vimuttimagga) is unverified. · **TENTATIVE**.

## 2. The four canonical pairs (Ñāṇamoli wording · verify against this, not pop lists)

| Abiding | Near enemy (canonical) | Shared aspect | Far enemy (canonical) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mettā (loving-kindness) §98 | **Greed** (rāga) | "both share in seeing virtues" | **Ill will** |
| Karuṇā (compassion) §99 | **"Grief based on the home life"** (gehasita domanassa) | "both share in seeing failure" | **Cruelty** (vihiṃsā) |
| Muditā (gladness) §100 | **"Joy based on the home life"** (gehasita somanassa) | "both share in seeing success" | **"Aversion (boredom)"** (arati) |
| Upekkhā (equanimity) §101 | **"Equanimity of unknowing based on the home life"** (aññāṇupekkhā) | "both share in ignoring faults and virtues" | **Greed AND resentment** (two far enemies) |

All four verified against the primary text plus independent secondary confirmations.[^1][^6]
— **FACT**. Companion "failure clauses" (Vism IX.93–96) feed the modern glosses: metta "fails
when it produces (selfish) affection [sineha]"; compassion "fails when it produces sorrow";
gladness "fails when it produces merriment"; equanimity "fails when it produces the equanimity
of unknowing."[^1]

**Where pop lists diverge (all FACT):**
- "Pity" as compassion's near enemy is a teacher gloss (Kornfield-lineage) of "grief based on
  the home life"; closer glosses are "grief," "personal distress," "worldly sadness." English
  "pity" imports condescension the canon doesn't emphasize; the canonical emphasis is
  overwhelm/sorrow.[^7]
- Muditā's canonical **far enemy is boredom (arati), not envy**. Envy (issā) is what muditā's
  *function* opposes ("being unenvious"), which is how it colonized the far-enemy slot in
  Kornfield/Fischer/Wikipedia-style lists.[^1][^6]
- "Attachment" as love's near enemy merges §98's rāga (greed) with §93's sineha and the
  Saṅgaha's pema.[^1][^5]
- "Indifference" as equanimity's near enemy compresses "ignorance-based unknowing equanimity
  of the household life" — stronger than casual not-caring; Bhikkhu Bodhi: upekkhā is
  "indifference only to the demands of the ego-self," not unconcern for others.[^8]
- The structure is asymmetric: upekkhā has two far enemies; no single "the" list exists beyond
  Vism IX.98–101.

## 3. The discernment mechanism

- **Why near enemies fool you (canonical logic):** the near enemy shares the virtue's
  *cognitive aspect* · the same object-orientation (seeing virtues / failure / success /
  ignoring both) with a different underlying root. Because it "keeps close by," it "easily
  finds an opportunity"; the virtue "should be well protected from it."[^1] · **FACT**.
- **The far enemy needs no such vigilance** because it *cannot co-occur* with the virtue: "it
  is not possible to practice loving-kindness and feel anger simultaneously" — practice may
  proceed "free from fear" of it. The far enemy is defined by co-occurrence impossibility, not
  mere oppositeness. This reverses the intuitive threat-ranking: **the counterfeit, not the
  opposite, is the practical danger.**[^1] · **FACT**.
- **Practitioner markers for real-vs-counterfeit** (convergent teacher heuristics · FACT as to
  what is taught; the markers are heuristics, not canon):[^7][^9]
  - *Separation vs connection* · pity "sets up a separation… affirming and gratifying to the
    self"; near enemies "mistakenly separate us from life" (Kornfield).
  - *Self-reference* · sentimentality is "more self-referential; we've lost sight of the other
    human being" (Lama Tsomo); genuine upekkhā is "freedom from all points of self-reference"
    (Bodhi).
  - *Contraction / conditionality / fear* · attachment "grasps, demands, aims to possess";
    indifference "stems from fear, is a withdrawal."
  - *For whom is this?* · idiot compassion: "you're trying to get away from YOUR feeling of
    I-can't-bear-to-see-them-suffering · you're doing it for yourself" (Chödrön).
  - *Overwhelm* · near-enemy grief is "being shattered, debilitated by the suffering we see,"
    vs compassion as "tender readiness of the heart… without despair."
  - *Sequence-watching* · the far enemy can arise OUT of the virtue: Mahasi Sayadaw's
    snake-and-frog example (compassion for the frog flips into wanting to strike the
    snake).[^10] · **QUALIFIED**.
- **Testing instructions:** interrogate the state · "Is this loving-kindness or affection?";
  "Is there something I am clinging to or avoiding?"; canonical functional test (DN 21):
  cultivate the version of the state under which skillful qualities grow.[^9] · **FACT**
  (instruction sets), DN 21 linkage **QUALIFIED**.

## 4. Modern teachers and list drift

- **Kornfield** is the main popularizer (in print by late 1980s; *A Path with Heart* 1993).
  His pairs: love/attachment; compassion/pity + despair "another enemy"; joy/comparison-and-
  jealousy; equanimity/indifference. Organizing frame: near enemies separate, genuine abidings
  connect.[^7] · **FACT**.
- **Salzberg** (*Lovingkindness* 1995; Lion's Roar/Tricycle) · desire/grasping vs metta,
  jealousy/comparison vs muditā (exact published list wording not directly retrieved —
  **QUALIFIED**).[^11] **Brach**: "attachment masquerades as love," "indifference can
  masquerade as equanimity"; upekkhā's shadow as dissociative distance.[^12] **Fronsdal/IMC**:
  teaches the full framework and *extends* it · "the near enemy of friendship is flattery. A
  near enemy of an ideal is that which looks like the ideal but actually detracts from
  it."[^9] **Chödrön** names idiot compassion "the third near enemy of compassion"; **Fischer**
  gives frivolity for muditā; Tibetan-lineage lists (Lama Tsomo) give sentimentality/
  hypocrisy variants.[^7][^13] · **FACT** that lists vary substantially by teacher and
  lineage; the modern list is generative, not fixed.
- **Extension beyond the brahmaviharas** is established practice: Kornfield's "Near Enemies of
  Awakening"; Wallis's *Near Enemies of the Truth* (2023) applies it to spiritual platitudes,
  adding "if a Near Enemy is near enough, it can be a Temporary Ally for a beginner."[^14]
  · **FACT**.

## 5. Secular / scientific crossover (adjacent · keep attribution direction clear)

- **Empathic distress vs compassion (Klimecki/Singer):** longitudinal fMRI training studies —
  empathy training increased negative affect (anterior insula + aMCC); compassion training
  reversed it, increasing positive affect via a non-overlapping network (mOFC, pgACC, ventral
  striatum). They propose renaming "compassion fatigue" → "**empathic distress
  fatigue**."[^15] · **FACT** as to findings; note one research group generated the core
  studies, and the mapping onto karuṇā's near enemy is made by dharma-side writers, not the
  scientists. · **QUALIFIED**.
- **Bloom's *Against Empathy*** invokes the Buddhist distinction (via Goodman's "sentimental
  compassion" vs "great compassion"); the empathy/compassion split is itself disputed in
  psychology (compassion-requires-empathy critiques).[^16] · **FACT** (both sides documented).
- **Idiot compassion** (Trungpa; popularized by Chödrön): conceptualized do-gooding without
  intelligence · enabling; term possibly borrowed from Gurdjieff's "compassionate idiot"
  (**QUALIFIED**, one detailed source).[^13]
- **Spiritual bypassing** (Welwood 1984): "this tendency to try to avoid or prematurely
  transcend basic human needs, feelings, and developmental tasks" (1984 wording; his later
  gloss: using spiritual ideas/practices to sidestep unresolved emotional issues); entered
  peer-reviewed counseling literature (Cashwell 2007). Adjacent counterfeit-spirituality concept, not a brahmavihara
  teaching.[^17] · **FACT**.

## 6. Anti-patterns · misstatements this reference must correct

1. Quoting "an enemy masquerading as a friend" as Buddhaghosa → it's Dhammapāla's
   sub-commentary.
2. "Envy is the far enemy of sympathetic joy" stated as canon → canonical arati (boredom);
   envy is the function's opposite.
3. Treating the framework as sutta → it's commentarial; content is canonical (MN 137, the
   escapes formula), the frame is Buddhaghosa's.
4. Treating any one teacher's list as "the" list → lists drift by lineage; cite which layer
   you're using.
5. "Upekkhā's near enemy is not caring" → canonical near enemy is *ignorance-based* unknowing
   equanimity; and upekkhā ≠ indifference (Bodhi, Ṭhānissaro, Anālayo all warn against the
   rendering).
6. Citing Klimecki/Singer as if the scientists equate empathic distress with the near enemy of
   compassion → the mapping is dharma-side commentary on the science.
