# Social Influence Theory: Norms, Social Proof, Conformity, Obedience

Why "what others do" moves people — the mechanism, the two distinct channels, and
the honest limits of the famous studies.

## The two channels (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955)
The foundational distinction. Conformity has **two separable causes**:
- **Informational social influence** — accepting others' behavior/opinions as
  **evidence about reality**, especially under uncertainty. Produces **genuine
  private acceptance** ("if everyone swerves here, there must be a pothole — I now
  believe there's a pothole").
- **Normative social influence** — conforming to others' **expectations** to gain
  approval and avoid rejection. Often produces **public compliance without private
  change** ("I'll go along in the meeting, but I haven't actually changed my mind").

This maps onto the persuasion-durability question: informational influence changes
the attitude; normative influence changes only the visible behavior while the
social pressure lasts.

## Descriptive vs injunctive norms — Focus Theory (Cialdini, Reno & Kallgren, 1990)
Two kinds of norm, each driving behavior through a *different* channel:
- **Descriptive norm** — what people **actually do** ("most teams have upgraded").
  Works via **informational** influence: others' behavior is taken as evidence of
  the smart/adaptive choice.
- **Injunctive norm** — what people **approve/disapprove of** ("upgrading is
  considered best practice; running EOL versions is frowned on"). Works via
  **normative** influence: the motivation is social approval.

### The focal principle (the operational key)
Norms only steer behavior when they are **focal** — salient in attention at the
moment of action. A norm the person isn't currently thinking about doesn't move
them. So *activating* the right norm at the decision point is the whole game.

### The descriptive-norm backfire (a critical failure mode)
A well-intended descriptive-norm message can **backfire** by advertising the
undesirable behavior as common: "Many organizations are *still* on the legacy
version" normalizes staying on legacy. The fix: state the norm you **want**
("teams like yours are moving to X"), and if the bad behavior is common, lean on
the **injunctive** norm (disapproval) instead — and consider pairing it so the two
norms align rather than conflict.

## Social proof — the mechanism, not a trick
Social proof is just **informational influence under uncertainty**: when unsure,
people treat others' behavior as diagnostic of the correct behavior. It is
strongest when (a) the situation is **ambiguous**, (b) the others are **similar**
to the perceiver, and (c) there are **many** of them. "Companies like yours chose
X" is informational social influence aimed via a descriptive norm.

## The classic demonstrations — and their modern caveats
These are the canonical evidence for normative pressure and authority. Cite them as
**demonstrations of mechanisms under specific conditions**, never as literal
predictions of how a given stakeholder will behave.

- **Asch conformity (1951/1956).** ~**One-third** of judgments conformed to a
  unanimous-but-wrong majority on an obvious line-length task; ~75% of participants
  conformed at least once. Critical moderators:
  - A **single dissenting ally** collapses conformity dramatically — unanimity is
    the active ingredient.
  - Conformity is **culturally and historically variable** (Bond & Smith's
    meta-analysis: higher in collectivist cultures; declined in some eras).
  - Most conformity here was **normative** (people knew the right answer but went
    along publicly).
- **Milgram obedience (1963).** ~**65%** of participants administered the maximum
  shock under an authority's prods. Caveats that matter:
  - **Ethically and methodologically contested** (Perry's archival work; variable
    scripts; debriefing concerns).
  - **Burger (2009)** ran an ethically-constrained partial replication (the
    "150-volt solution," stopping at the *point of no return*) and found obedience
    rates only **slightly below** Milgram's — i.e., the effect is **not era-bound**;
    people today are roughly as obedient. But situational factors (proximity of
    authority, presence of a disobedient model, legitimacy of the authority) swing
    obedience enormously. A modeled refusal sharply reduces it (echoing Asch's
    ally).

The defensible reading: **situational pressure and authority are powerful**, and
they're **highly conditional** — dissent, distance, and questioned legitimacy break
them. The "humans are blind sheep" caricature is not supported.

## Operator translation
- For **durable** adoption, drive **informational** influence (genuine acceptance),
  not just normative pressure (which evaporates when you stop pushing).
- Make the **desired** behavior the **focal descriptive norm among similar peers**,
  and align an **injunctive** signal with it. Never accidentally normalize the
  behavior you're fighting.
- Recruit a **visible dissenter/ally** when you need to break a stuck consensus —
  one credible internal champion saying "we should move" can collapse a conforming
  majority, exactly as Asch's ally collapses conformity.
- Treat "everyone's doing it" as **strongest under uncertainty and similarity** —
  it's weak when the audience is confident or sees the referents as dissimilar.
