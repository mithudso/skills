# Replication Status — What Survived, What's Contested, What Failed

**Read this before citing any decision/social-psych effect to a customer or in a written
recommendation.** Psychology went through a replication crisis: the Open Science
Collaboration (2015, *Science*) reproduced only ~36% of 100 studies, and social psychology
fared worse (~25%). Many Labs 2 and other large pre-registered efforts confirmed the pattern.
Some celebrity effects did **not** survive. Citing a debunked effect as fact destroys credibility.

Rule of thumb: **core judgment-and-decision-making (JDM) findings are robust; several adjacent
social-psychology effects are not.**

---

## Robust — safe to rely on

These replicate well and/or have strong meta-analytic and real-world support:
- **Anchoring** — one of the most replicable effects in the field.
- **Framing effects** (gain/loss) — robust.
- **Probability weighting / overweighting of small probabilities** — robust; foundational to prospect theory.
- **The disposition effect, default/status-quo effects** — strong field evidence (e.g., enrollment, organ-donation defaults).
- **Present bias / hyperbolic discounting** — robust; large meta-analytic support (quasi-hyperbolic β-δ).
- **Planning fallacy / optimism in forecasts** — well supported.
- **Availability, representativeness, confirmation, hindsight, overconfidence** — core effects, broadly supported (effect sizes vary by paradigm).

## Contested / nuanced — state carefully, don't overclaim

### Loss aversion (λ ≈ 2)
**Real in many settings, but NOT a universal context-free constant.** Gal & Rucker (2018),
"The Loss of Loss Aversion," argue it's far more context-dependent than the "losses hurt 2×"
slogan implies. Evidence: gains can loom larger than losses at **small magnitudes** (Harinck
et al., 2007); people **over-predict** the emotional sting of losses vs what they actually feel
(Kermer et al., 2006); and several phenomena once "explained by" loss aversion (endowment
effect, status-quo bias) have competing explanations. Defenders counter that critics used wrong
scales, but follow-ups found loss aversion still absent in some cases even with the recommended
measures (Mukherjee et al., 2017).
- *How to use it:* treat loss aversion as a **hypothesis to test for this customer/context**
  (more likely at higher stakes and for endowed goods), not a guaranteed lever. Don't assert
  "losses hurt exactly twice as much" as a law.

### Dual-process System 1/System 2
A **useful model, not literal brain architecture.** The strict two-systems boxes are widely
criticized (better read as a continuum of automaticity; "the false dilemma"). The *phenomena*
(fast intuitive vs slow deliberate processing) are real and useful; don't reify "two systems"
as neuroanatomy.

### Choice overload (the "jam study")
Fragile. The original effect (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000) did not hold up in a large meta-analysis
(Scheibehenne et al., 2010; mean effect ≈ 0) and depends heavily on moderators. Don't assume
"more options always hurt"; curate and provide a default rather than citing choice overload as a law.

## Failed / abandoned — do NOT cite as established

### Power posing
The claim that expansive postures raise testosterone, lower cortisol, and improve outcomes did
**not** survive. Ranehill et al. (2015) found no hormonal/behavioral effects in a larger sample;
Simmons & Simonsohn (2016/2017) p-curved the literature and found no evidential value once
selective reporting is accounted for. **Co-author Dana Carney publicly disavowed the effect**
("I do not believe 'power pose' effects are real"). At most, a small *felt-power* self-report
effect may remain; the embodied/hormonal/performance claims are dead. Do not advise a customer
or colleague to "power pose."

### Social priming
Effects like the **Bargh elderly-walking-slow study** (1996) largely failed to replicate (Doyen
et al., 2012, and others). Kahneman himself wrote a 2012 open letter warning the social-priming
field faced a "train wreck." Treat dramatic social-priming claims as **not established**. (This
is why the "Priming" element of MINDSPACE is the weakest — see `references/choice-architecture.md`.)
Note: this is distinct from robust *semantic/perceptual* priming in cognitive psychology, which is fine.

### Ego depletion
The idea that willpower is a depletable resource drained by prior self-control did **not** hold
up: a large pre-registered multi-lab replication (Hagger et al., 2016, ~23 labs) found an effect
near zero, and the area is now seen as having both a replication and a *conceptual* crisis. Do
not build advice on "they're out of willpower / decision fatigue" as if it were settled science.
