# Skills-Based Hiring and Verified Credentials

## Overview

Skills-based hiring is the practice of screening and selecting candidates on demonstrated
competencies rather than proxies for competency — chiefly a four-year degree. It is real, it is
growing, and it is also substantially oversold in employer press releases. The honest picture
requires holding two facts simultaneously: employer *policy* has shifted fast (job postings dropping
degree language, public commitments from major employers) while employer *practice* (who actually
gets hired) has shifted far more slowly. A resume strategy built only on the press-release version
of this trend will misallocate effort — chasing badges and micro-certifications that recruiters
at most large employers still weight below a degree or a name-brand employer, while ignoring the
places (tech, skills-testing platforms, specific "leader" employers) where the shift is genuinely
substantive.

This reference covers:
1. The actual scale of skills-based hiring adoption vs. the policy-practice gap
2. Reading degree-optional job-posting language as genuine vs. boilerplate
3. Presenting verified skills assessments (LinkedIn, HackerRank/Codility, badges) without looking gimmicky
4. Which certifications move the needle in tech hiring, tier by tier
5. Portfolio-as-verification vs. credential-as-verification — which skills-based employers trust more
6. How skills-testing platforms change what a resume needs to do
7. Anti-patterns
8. Quick reference

## 1. The Adoption-Practice Gap

### What employers claim

Employer self-reported adoption of skills-based hiring has climbed sharply: 81% of U.S. employers
reported using skills-based hiring practices in 2024, up from 73% in 2023 and 57% in 2022; a 2025
TestGorilla survey put the figure at 85%.[^1] NACE's Job Outlook 2026 survey (183 respondents, 170
of them NACE employer members, fielded August–September 2025) found 70% of employers use
skills-based hiring for entry-level roles, up from 65% the year before — most heavily during
interviewing (87% of those employers) and screening (65%).[^2] The same survey shows GPA screening
in retreat as a proxy: 73% of employers screened by GPA in 2019; only 42% do in the 2026 survey.[^2]

On postings specifically: as of 2025, 53% of employers had publicly removed degree requirements
from at least some job postings, up from 30% the year before (TestGorilla), and Indeed's own
listings data shows only about 18% of U.S. job postings still list a degree requirement, down from
roughly 20% in 2018.[^1][^3] IBM (via its "New Collar" program, one of the earliest and most
extensive skills-based initiatives), Google, Delta Air Lines, and Bank of America are commonly
cited examples of large employers that eliminated four-year-degree requirements for a substantial
share of roles.[^1]

### What actually happens to hiring outcomes

This is the gap worth stating plainly to a job seeker, because it changes strategy. The
authoritative source here is **"Skills-Based Hiring: The Long Road from Pronouncements to
Practice"** (Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School's Project on Managing the Future
of Work, Matthew Sigelman, Joseph B. Fuller, and Alex Martin, February 2024) — a study of 11,300
roles at large firms, tracked at least one year before and after degree requirements were
removed.[^4]

Its findings:
- Only about 3.6% of roles removed degree requirements in the study window.
- Removing a degree requirement produced, on average, only a **3.5 percentage-point** increase in
  the share of non-degreed hires for that role.
- Scaled across the economy, the researchers found **fewer than 1 in 700 hires** in the prior year
  was attributable to this shift toward skills.
- Among companies that publicly announced degree-requirement removal, **45% changed in name
  only** — no measurable difference in who actually got hired.

The same study, however, identified a genuine minority: **37% of firms were classified as "Skills-
Based Hiring Leaders,"** and those firms saw an average **18% increase** in non-degreed hires —
real, structural change, driven by also restructuring sourcing channels, assessment criteria, and
manager incentives, not just deleting a line from the JD. Employers cited as leaders in that
cohort include Koch Industries, Walmart, Apple, General Motors, Target, Cigna Healthcare, Tyson
Foods, ExxonMobil, and Yelp.[^4]

The predecessor study, **"The Emerging Degree Reset"** (Burning Glass Institute / HBS, Fuller et
al., February 2022, analyzing 51 million job postings back to 2014), documented the posting-level
side of this: 46% of middle-skill and 31% of high-skill occupations had a "material degree reset"
between 2017 and 2019, and 63% of those resets were structural (pre-dating the pandemic labor
crunch) rather than cyclical. Even so, 37% of middle-skill jobs showed no reduction in degree
screening, which the authors calculate still excludes roughly 15.7 million workers from the
candidate pool for those roles. Their framing has become the standard shorthand for this whole
debate: **"Jobs do not require four-year college degrees. Employers do."**[^5]

### The practical implication for a job seeker

1. **Treat "skills-based hiring" as employer-specific, not sector-wide.** A JD that says "or
   equivalent experience" tells you almost nothing about whether *this* employer is one of the 37%
   of leaders or one of the 45% that changed in name only. Section 2 covers how to read the signal.
2. **Tech, government, and a specific set of named large employers are the real frontier.** Over 16
   U.S. states have dropped degree requirements for government positions outright — public-sector
   adoption has been more structural than corporate self-reports suggest.[^1]
3. **A non-degreed candidate should not assume a dropped-requirement posting is an open door.**
   Lean harder on the verification signals in Sections 3–5 (assessments, certifications, portfolio)
   precisely because the degree screen, even when nominally removed, often still operates
   informally in the recruiter's head or the ATS's structured fields.
4. **A degreed candidate should not assume skills-based hiring is irrelevant to them.** Recruiters
   increasingly search ATS databases by skill keyword and assessment result, not just by degree
   field — Section 6 covers why this changes resume structure even for degree-holders.

## 2. Reading Degree-Optional Language

"Bachelor's degree or equivalent experience" appears on a large share of postings, and it means
different things depending on who put it there and why.

**Signals it's genuine:**
- The surrounding requirements are specific and map to daily tasks ("3+ years building REST APIs in
  a production Node.js environment") rather than generic filler — task-specific language tends to
  come from the hiring manager, who cares what you can do, not where you learned it.[^6]
- The posting uses "preferred" rather than "required" for the degree line specifically.
- The employer is a named skills-based-hiring adopter (see Section 1) or is in tech, where degree
  requirements have eroded furthest.
- The role is non-regulated — most software, data, marketing, operations, and sales roles have no
  legal or licensing reason to require a specific degree.

**Signals it's boilerplate / legal cover:**
- The line is generic and appears verbatim across every posting at the company regardless of role.
- Hiring managers have openly described this pattern: HR or legal adds "Bachelor's degree or
  equivalent experience" as a defensible, quantifiable screening criterion in case a rejected
  candidate later challenges the decision — not because the hiring manager cares.[^6]
- The requirement sits alongside other generic catch-alls ("strong communication skills," "ability
  to work in a fast-paced environment") that read as templated across the org, not written for this
  specific req.

**Where it's likely to be enforced literally regardless of phrasing:** healthcare, engineering
(licensure-gated roles), finance (certain compliance and audit functions), and government
contracting, where a specific credential may be a genuine legal or contractual requirement rather
than a proxy.[^6]

**Practical rule:** Don't self-select out based on the degree line alone. If your experience covers
the task-specific requirements, apply — the discretion to accept "equivalent experience" rests with
the employer, and career-advice sources are unanimous that candidates screen themselves out of
qualifying roles far more often than employers screen them out.[^6] But do not over-invest in a
single posting's degree-optional framing as proof the employer is a skills-based-hiring "leader" —
the 45%-in-name-only finding from Section 1 argues for calibrated optimism, not certainty.

## 3. Presenting Verified Skills Assessments

### LinkedIn Skill Assessments

LinkedIn reports that candidates who complete a skill assessment are **30% more likely to get
hired**, and a passing score (70%+) unlocks a verified badge on the profile.[^7] The mechanism
matters more than the badge itself: recruiters using LinkedIn Recruiter can filter searches to show
only candidates with a verified badge in a given skill, so the primary value is **discoverability**
— a candidate without the badge may never surface in the initial search, regardless of actual
skill.[^7] Scores below 70% are never shown publicly and can be deleted before a three-month
retest cooldown, so there is no public downside to attempting one. Badges expire after one year,
requiring a retake to stay current.

**How to use them on a resume, not just LinkedIn:**
- Do not paste a bare score or percentile onto a resume — it is not portable or verifiable outside
  LinkedIn's system and reads as an odd, unearned flex.
- If the skill is central to the target role, a single line in a Certifications/Skills section —
  "LinkedIn Verified Skill: Python (2026)" with a profile link — is acceptable and low-risk. It is a
  door-opener, not a differentiator; interviewers move past it quickly into deeper evaluation once
  in the room.[^7]
- Treat it as a supplement to, never a substitute for, demonstrated experience or a portfolio.
  Recruiters interviewed on the subject are explicit that badges are "good to have but not a deal
  breaker."[^7]

### HackerRank / Codility scores

There are two categorically different things people mean by "my HackerRank score," and conflating
them is the most common mistake:

1. **A private, employer-administered assessment score** (the test you took as part of one
   company's application pipeline). This is a relative, percentile-based internal recruiting metric
   set by that employer against that specific candidate pool — it has no external meaning and is
   not something you list on a resume at all.[^8]
2. **A public HackerRank/Codility Skills Certification** (badges like "Problem Solving
   (Intermediate)," "Python (Advanced)") completed independently on the platform, plus a public
   profile. These function as a portable, linkable credential and are reasonable to reference.[^8]

**Presentation rule:** never write a bare number ("HackerRank: 87/100") — it's meaningless to a
reader outside that one pipeline and looks like padding. If you have a genuinely elite rating on a
standardized competitive-programming site (Codeforces, LeetCode, TopCoder), that carries more
external credibility than a HackerRank percentile, because it reflects a global, standardized
rating rather than one employer's custom test.[^8] Format as: "HackerRank Certified: Problem
Solving (Advanced) — [link]" in a Certifications section, not inline in a bullet.

### General rule for any verified-assessment credential

Frame with context and a link, not a raw number. A reader cannot interpret "92%" or "Level 3"
without knowing the scale, the difficulty curve, or the candidate pool it was normed against.
Linking lets a skeptical recruiter verify it themselves in ten seconds — which is the entire point
of a *verified* credential over a self-reported skill claim.

## 4. Certification Tiers in Tech Hiring

Hiring managers do not treat all certifications the same, and the honest guidance requires ranking
them, not listing them as interchangeable "credentials."

### Tier 1 — Vendor cloud certifications (highest resume-parsing and recruiter-recognition value)

AWS, Azure, and GCP certifications carry the most consistent hiring-manager recognition among
technical certifications, and measurable salary effects: AWS-certified professionals report
salaries averaging **$20,000 higher** than non-certified peers in comparable roles, and certified
cloud-security professionals with CCSP report roughly **19% higher pay**.[^9] Within the AWS
ladder specifically, the **AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Associate** is the single most
widely recognized entry point — largest job-posting footprint, no prerequisites, and hiring
managers treat it as the default proof of baseline cloud competence.[^9]

**But certifications alone are read as weak signal.** The recurring theme across hiring-manager
commentary: a certification "is the beginning of demonstrating knowledge, not the end" — a resume
with one AWS Solutions Architect Associate cert plus three production-quality GitHub projects
outperforms a resume with five certifications and no evidence of having built anything.[^9] Treat
a cloud cert as necessary-but-not-sufficient: pair it with a project, a portfolio, or work
experience that used it.

### Tier 2 — Bootcamp completion certificates

Regarded skeptically on their own. Multiple markets have emerged specifically to patch this
credibility gap — lightweight, low-cost skill-verification products positioned explicitly as
supplements for bootcamp graduates, marketed as "a CV supplement for self-taught or bootcamp
grads" or "a cheap benchmark before booking a vendor exam."[^9] The existence of that market is
itself evidence that a bootcamp certificate by itself is not viewed as sufficient proof of skill by
hiring managers. Data-analyst comparisons make the gap concrete: analysts entering with a Google or
IBM certification start their careers earning **$10,000–$15,000 more** than bootcamp graduates
without a credential attached.[^9] The combination that works: bootcamp (or self-teaching) →
demonstrable project work → a relevant vendor certification layered on top, not a bootcamp
completion certificate presented as the terminal credential.

### Tier 3 — Online-course completion certificates (Coursera, Udemy, LinkedIn Learning, etc.)

Lowest tier of the three for hiring-manager weight when presented as a standalone line. These
verify attendance/completion, not competence, and carry no standardized difficulty bar — a
completed Udemy course and a passed vendor certification exam are not interchangeable signals, and
listing many of them reads as padding rather than expertise. Structured, well-known Google Career
Certificates are a partial exception — they get referenced in job postings and function closer to
Tier 1–2 territory because of brand recognition and a defined skill scope — but generic
course-completion certificates from unbranded platforms should generally not occupy resume space
except in a Skills/Continuing Education line, never framed as equivalent to a vendor certification
or degree.[^10]

### Cross-tier synthesis

| Credential type | Standalone resume weight | Best paired with |
|---|---|---|
| AWS/Azure/GCP associate-level cert | High | A project or role that used it in production |
| Advanced/specialty vendor cert (e.g., AWS Solutions Architect Professional, security certs) | High | Years of matching experience — overqualification without experience reads oddly |
| Bootcamp completion certificate | Low alone | A relevant vendor cert + 2-3 portfolio projects |
| Public HackerRank/Codility skill badge | Moderate, mainly for discoverability | Portfolio or GitHub work showing the same skill applied |
| Generic online-course completion cert | Low | Should rarely stand alone; fine in an aggregated "continuing education" line |
| Google/IBM/Microsoft named Career Certificates | Moderate-high (brand recognition) | Same as vendor certs — pair with applied work |

## 5. Portfolio vs. Credential as Verification

When forced to choose which single form of proof to prioritize, the data leans toward the
portfolio — but the honest answer is "it depends on employer type," and both signals compound
rather than substitute for each other.

**Evidence for portfolios:** 76% of hiring managers in Resume Genius's hiring-manager survey say
self-taught skills and portfolio work can outweigh formal education, and in a related framing from
the same research, 79% said self-taught skills or a strong portfolio can carry *equal* weight to a
degree, while 82% said certifications specifically can be as valuable as a bachelor's degree, and
86% said they value relevant experience over formal education outright (survey of roughly 1,000
U.S. hiring managers; treat exact percentages as directional given self-reported survey
methodology).[^11] Separately, 71% of employers agree the quality of a candidate's portfolio
influences their hiring decision.[^12]

**GitHub specifically is context-dependent, not universally weighted.** How much a GitHub profile
matters depends heavily on company type: tech-first and open-source-friendly companies sometimes
weight it more than the resume itself, while traditional companies treat it as a bonus signal at
best.[^12] The practitioner consensus: keep a small number of polished, well-documented public
projects with clear READMEs explaining context and decisions, rather than a large number of
half-finished repos — most recruiters still weigh the traditional resume more heavily and see
GitHub as a supplementary signal of practical ability, not a replacement.[^12]

**Certifications still function, but as confirmation rather than primary qualifier.** In a
portfolio-first hiring model, a recognized certification works best when it *reinforces*
demonstrated ability and gives structured coverage of skills an employer expects to see named
somewhere — it compensates for a gap in formal education or refreshes a degree that predates
current tech requirements.[^12] No single signal wins outright: 92% of employers in industry
survey data believe skills-based hiring is more effective at identifying talented candidates than
resumes alone, and 89% believe it is more predictive of on-the-job success — but the same body of
research shows outcomes lag intent (see Section 1's 1-in-700 finding), which is the practical
reason to present multiple corroborating signals — portfolio, certification, and quantified resume
achievements — rather than betting on any single one.[^12]

**Practical resolution for the resume:**
1. Lead with quantified achievement bullets (the core resume craft — see the parent
   `resume-and-cv-writing.md` reference).
2. Link a portfolio or GitHub in the header for roles where one is standard practice (engineering,
   design, data, writing).
3. List certifications in a dedicated section, tiered per Section 4, never presented as
   interchangeable with the portfolio.
4. Do not let credential-collecting substitute for building something a reader can actually look
   at — the recurring hiring-manager complaint across every source above is credentials without
   evidence of applied work.

## 6. Skills-Testing Platforms and the Resume

Skills-testing platforms are changing what employers ask a resume to do at all — several are
explicitly designed to make the resume a secondary or even bypassed input.

**Indeed Assessments** (launched 2018 out of Indeed's acquisition of Interviewed) let employers
screen candidates on job-specific skills rather than relying on resume text. Indeed's own data:
candidates who completed an assessment sent by a prospective employer were **30% more likely** to
get a positive response, and employers using the tool saw close to a **30% reduction in time to
hire**.[^13] Indeed later extended this to job seekers directly — anyone can take one of 50+
psychologist-designed skills assessments and attach the results to their Indeed resume/profile,
independent of any specific application, explicitly framed as addressing the finding that
candidates (women in particular, per Indeed's internal research) tend to undersell their skills in
resume text alone.[^13]

**Vervoe** and similar job-simulation platforms build assessments on the actual tasks a role
requires rather than resume claims or trivia-style tests, explicitly marketed on the premise that
"skills-based evaluation removes the advantage of a polished CV or a prestigious university — the
work is the proof."[^14] Vendors in this space claim substantial screening-time reductions from
routing around resume/phone-screen stages entirely for high-volume roles.

**What this means for resume strategy:**
1. **Complete the platform's assessment when a JD routes through one** (Indeed Assessments, Vervoe,
   HackerRank, Codility, custom take-home tests). At many employers using these tools, the
   assessment result is now a stronger signal in the pipeline than the resume text — treat it as
   the primary artifact for that application, not an optional add-on.
2. **A resume applying to a skills-testing employer should still be strong on its own**, because
   the resume determines whether you're invited to take the assessment in the first place at most
   pipelines — the assessment doesn't replace the resume screen, it follows it.
3. **Where a platform lets you attach a self-initiated assessment to your public profile** (Indeed's
   job-seeker-facing assessments, LinkedIn Skill Assessments), doing so before applying — rather
   than waiting to be invited — increases discoverability in employer searches, per the mechanism
   described in Section 3.
4. **Do not assume every employer using an ATS is also using a skills-testing layer.** ATS parsing
   (see `ats-resume-optimization.md`) and skills-assessment platforms are different systems that
   may or may not be integrated at a given employer; a resume still has to survive the parse
   regardless of whether an assessment follows.

## 7. Anti-Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Listing a raw private employer HackerRank/Codility score on a resume | Not portable, not verifiable outside that one pipeline; reads as padding |
| Bare LinkedIn Skill Assessment percentage with no link | Unverifiable without a link; the badge itself, not the number, is the signal |
| Presenting a bootcamp completion certificate as a terminal credential | Hiring managers read bootcamp-only certs as weak signal absent a paired vendor cert or projects |
| Stacking five+ online-course completion certificates in a Certifications section | Reads as credential-collecting, not expertise; course completion ≠ competence |
| Assuming a "degree or equivalent experience" posting means the employer is a skills-based-hiring leader | 45% of public degree-requirement removals showed no change in actual hiring (Burning Glass/HBS 2024) |
| Self-selecting out of a role over a generic degree line | Career-advice consensus: candidates screen themselves out of qualifying roles more often than employers screen them out |
| Treating a cloud certification as sufficient without any applied project or work experience | "Evidence, not experience" — certs pass exams, projects prove application |
| Embedding portfolio images or a resume-style GitHub contribution graph in the resume PDF | Breaks ATS parsing and bloats file size (see `ats-resume-optimization.md`) |
| A large, unpruned GitHub with many half-finished repos as "portfolio" | Practitioner consensus favors a few polished, documented projects over volume |
| Treating certifications and portfolio work as interchangeable | They verify different things (structured knowledge vs. applied ability); present both, don't substitute one for the other |
| Ignoring an employer's skills-assessment invitation because "the resume already covers it" | At skills-testing employers, the assessment often outweighs resume text in the pipeline |
| Assuming skills-based hiring is uniform across industries | Adoption and follow-through vary sharply by employer, sector, and whether the employer is a documented "leader" vs. name-only adopter |

## 8. Quick Reference

**On the adoption gap:** Employer claims (81-85% using skills-based hiring) run far ahead of
hiring outcomes (fewer than 1 in 700 hires attributable to dropped degree requirements, per Burning
Glass/HBS 2024). Don't let a degree-optional posting alone signal genuine openness — check whether
surrounding requirements are task-specific (likely real) or generic boilerplate (likely HR/legal
cover).

**On verified assessments:** Link, don't quote a bare number. LinkedIn badges and public
HackerRank/Codility certifications are door-openers for discoverability, not differentiators once
in the interview room. Never list a private, employer-administered assessment score.

**On certifications:** Tier them. AWS/Azure/GCP associate-level certs (especially AWS Solutions
Architect Associate) carry real recruiter recognition and salary premiums; bootcamp completion
certificates need a paired vendor cert or project to avoid reading as padding; generic online-course
completion certificates carry the least standalone weight of the three.

**On portfolio vs. credential:** Portfolio work is trending toward being the more trusted signal,
especially at tech-forward and smaller employers (76-79% of hiring managers say self-taught
skills/portfolio can equal or outweigh formal education) — but certifications still function as
structured confirmation, especially at larger or more traditional organizations. Present both;
don't bet on either alone.

**On skills-testing platforms:** When a JD routes through Indeed Assessments, Vervoe, HackerRank, or
similar, treat the assessment as the primary artifact for that application — but the resume still
gates whether you're invited to take it.
