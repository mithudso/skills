# Performance review writing

## Overview

Performance reviews are not new information — they are **synthesis**. The cardinal rule from Camille Fournier and almost every modern people-management reference is "no surprises": anything that appears in a written review should already have been said in a one-on-one. The written review's job is to organize patterns, name impact, and create a record.

This skill covers three closely-related writing tasks:

1. **Manager-side reviews** of a direct report.
2. **Self-reviews** written by an employee about their own work.
3. **Peer / 360 feedback** written about a colleague.

All three share core mechanics: evidence-based claims, the SBI structure, growth-area framing instead of "weakness," and balanced narrative that distinguishes behavior from character. The differences are in voice, audience, and the specific traps each form falls into.

## Core concepts

### 1. The "no surprises" rule

If a written performance review contains feedback the employee is hearing for the first time, the manager has failed earlier in the year, not in the review itself. Camille Fournier states this directly in *The Manager's Path*: "If you're giving negative feedback in the course of a performance review, it shouldn't be a major surprise to your employee."

**Implication for writing:** Before writing, audit the year's feedback. If you're about to write something the employee hasn't heard, either (a) deliver it in a one-on-one *before* the review is submitted, or (b) cut it and address it next cycle. The review is the consolidation.

### 2. SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact) — Center for Creative Leadership

SBI is the canonical feedback structure. It anchors a claim to observable evidence.

- **Situation** — When and where the behavior occurred. Anchors the receiver in a specific context.
- **Behavior** — What the person actually did. Observable, factual, non-interpretive. No adjectives, no character claims.
- **Impact** — What followed from the behavior. The effect on people, projects, or outcomes.

**Optional extension (SBI-I):** Add a second I for **Intent**. Asking about intent — rather than assuming it — turns one-way feedback into dialogue and reduces defensiveness.

**Example (negative):**
> *Situation:* In the design review for the refund system on March 12. *Behavior:* You interrupted the junior engineer three times in the first ten minutes and finished her sentences twice. *Impact:* She stopped contributing to the discussion, and we lost her input on the rollback design — which I think we needed. *Intent:* I want to understand what you were trying to do in that meeting.

**Example (positive):**
> *Situation:* During the Q2 incident on April 8. *Behavior:* You took on the comms role within five minutes of joining the bridge, wrote three customer-facing updates, and kept the timeline document current. *Impact:* The CSMs were able to respond to customers without paging engineers, and the post-incident review came together two days faster than usual.

### 3. The "behavior + impact + recommendation" structure for review bullets

Most performance review systems expect short bullets, not long SBI essays. A compact, evidence-rich format:

```
[Behavior or accomplishment] + [Impact] + [Recommendation / next step]
```

**Weak:** "Sarah is a great team player and is always willing to help."

**Strong:** "Sarah led the cross-team migration of the billing service in Q2, partnering with three teams and coordinating four deployment windows; the migration shipped two weeks ahead of plan with zero customer-visible incidents. **Next step:** I'd like to see Sarah take on a bigger system design role in Q4 — the ledger redesign would be a natural fit."

### 4. Growth-area framing (not "weakness")

"Weakness" is character-coded; "growth area" is behavior-coded and forward-looking. The shift is not a euphemism — it changes what the bullet *does*. A weakness names a flaw; a growth area names an opportunity and points to action.

**Weakness phrasing (avoid):** "Tom is too quiet in meetings."

**Growth-area phrasing (use):** "In larger forums (5+ people), Tom contributes less than I think he could. He has the strongest understanding of the search service on the team, but in the Q3 architecture reviews his ideas often landed in the followup chat rather than in the meeting itself. **Growth focus for next cycle:** Tom and I have agreed he'll lead two of the next four architecture reviews, with me as backup. The goal is for him to anchor the conversation in real time."

Note how the strong version includes (a) specific situations, (b) the observed behavior, (c) the cost of the pattern, and (d) an agreed next step.

### 5. Radical Candor — Kim Scott's Caring Personally + Challenging Directly

Kim Scott's framework maps feedback on two axes: how much you care personally about the person, and how directly you challenge them.

- **Radical Candor:** Care + Challenge. The target.
- **Ruinous Empathy:** Care without challenge. Soft, vague, no real feedback. The most common manager failure mode.
- **Obnoxious Aggression:** Challenge without care. Direct but cold; reads as personal attack.
- **Manipulative Insincerity:** Neither. Politicking, hedging.

**Implication for written reviews:** the failure mode in writing is almost always Ruinous Empathy — burying real feedback in praise sandwich, hedging with "sometimes" and "occasionally," and leaving the reader unclear about what specifically needs to change. The fix is to be specific *and* warm: name the behavior, name the impact, and make clear you're invested in the person.

A reviewable test: if the employee read your bullet and asked "what specifically should I do differently," could you point at the answer in the bullet?

### 6. Camille Fournier's patterns from *The Manager's Path*

- **Lead with strengths, not weaknesses.** "When employees believe their manager sees the good things they do, they'll be more open to hearing about areas where they might improve."
- **Use concrete examples or cut the point.** "If you can't use a concrete example to support a point, ask yourself if the point is something you should be communicating in the review."
- **Negative feedback goes in private (one-on-one or written review).** Positive feedback can be public.
- **Most of your time should go to talents and achievements.** If most of the review is about correcting weaknesses, the relationship feels like continuous criticism and the employee disengages.

### 7. Evidence-based claims with concrete examples

Every assertion in a performance review should be tied to a specific example. Sweeping claims without examples are the single biggest source of perceived unfairness in reviews and the most common signal that a manager has written a biased or undercooked review.

**Weak:** "She has strong communication skills."

**Strong:** "When the platform migration ran into the dependency issue on the search team, she wrote the four-paragraph summary that unblocked the cross-team decision. The document was forwarded to leadership unchanged."

Lattice and 15Five both ship this rule as a top tip: ask "what were the three most impactful ways this person contributed?" rather than "how well did this person contribute?" Concrete examples reduce subjectivity and reduce bias.

### 8. The rating-vs-narrative split

Most systems pair a rating (1-5, Exceeds/Meets/Below, or a level-fit signal) with narrative paragraphs. The rating is a label; the narrative is the case.

**Common failure:** Rating and narrative are inconsistent. A "Meets" rating paired with three paragraphs of glowing accomplishments and one sentence about a growth area reads as if the rating is being suppressed. The opposite — "Exceeds" paired with mostly developmental feedback — reads as inflation.

**Fix:** Write the narrative first. Then choose the rating that the narrative supports. If the rating is constrained by calibration (see next concept), edit the narrative to match honestly — don't pad the narrative to justify a rating it doesn't support.

### 9. Calibration language

Most large organizations calibrate ratings across managers to reduce inflation and bias. When a manager's preferred rating is moved during calibration, the written review may need to be rewritten.

**Practical language for calibrated reviews:**

- "Relative to the broader L4 cohort this cycle, X's impact landed in the upper-middle range."
- "X had a strong year. Within this calibration cycle, the strongest performers shipped at a scope or visibility one level above. X's path to that next level is..."

**Avoid:** Phrases that read as defensive ("I argued for a higher rating but the committee...") — these undermine the review and the calibration system. The review is the company's document; calibration is part of the system.

### 10. Self-review traps

The two opposite failures of self-reviews are **false modesty** (underselling) and **inflation** (overselling). Most managers report false modesty as the more common problem and the more costly one.

**False-modesty traps:**

- Hedging with "I tried to" and "I worked on" instead of "I led" and "I shipped."
- Crediting the team for outcomes you personally drove.
- Listing the work without claiming the result.
- Omitting accomplishments because they "weren't a big deal."

**Inflation traps:**

- Sole credit for team outcomes.
- Vague "I'm a strong communicator" claims with no example.
- Strategic-language inflation: "transformed," "revolutionized," "100% improvement" without a baseline.

**The fix for both:** evidence-based bullets. State the outcome, state your role, name the team's role separately. "I led the migration; the team of four shipped it" beats both "I migrated the system" (overclaim) and "We migrated the system" (underclaim).

### 11. Peer / 360 feedback specificity

Peer feedback is often the least developed writing in the review packet. The most useful peer feedback is **specific to behaviors the manager wouldn't observe directly** — how the person shows up in working sessions, how they respond to feedback, how they pair, how they handle disagreement.

**Weak peer bullet:** "Always willing to help. Great teammate."

**Strong peer bullet:** "When I was stuck on the rate-limiter design in May, I dropped a half-formed doc into our channel at 8am and asked for thirty minutes. He read it before standup, came in with three specific questions that exposed the issue I hadn't seen, and stayed for an hour to walk through the alternative. I shipped a better design because of that conversation."

### 12. Bias-checking before submission

Performance reviews are a documented source of demographic bias. Common patterns: women and underrepresented minorities receive more vague, personality-focused feedback ("abrasive," "not a good fit," "needs to smile more") and less specific, technical feedback. Senior men receive more "stretch" language ("ready for the next level"); senior women receive more "support" language ("doing well in current scope").

**Self-check before submission:**

- Are claims tied to behaviors and outcomes, or to personality?
- Could I substitute another person's name in this bullet? If yes, the bullet isn't specific enough.
- Have I used different language for different people on the same team for the same observed behavior?
- Have I disproportionately commented on style (communication, presence, attitude) versus substance (technical impact, project outcomes)?

## Templates and examples

### Template: manager-side review bullet (BIR = Behavior + Impact + Recommendation)

```
[Behavior — what they did, with situation context]
[Impact — what changed because of it, for whom]
[Recommendation — what to do more of, less of, or differently next cycle]
```

### Example: full manager-side review section (for a senior engineer)

```
## Strengths

**Technical leadership on the refund pipeline rebuild.** Sarah led the
refund pipeline migration in Q1-Q2, coordinating with four teams and
managing the rollback strategy for two production cutovers. The migration
shipped two weeks ahead of plan, and we had zero customer-visible incidents
during the cutover windows. The post-migration design review was used by
two other teams as a template for their own migrations. **Continue:**
Take on more cross-team technical leadership — the ledger redesign in Q4
would be a strong fit.

**Mentorship of junior engineers.** Sarah onboarded two new hires this
year. Both shipped their first production change within their first month,
and both rated her onboarding as the most valuable part of their first
quarter in the team retro. **Continue:** I'd like Sarah to formalize the
onboarding doc she's built and to lead the next new-hire's first 90 days.

## Growth areas

**Written design communication.** Sarah's design conversations in pairs
and small groups are excellent — clear, specific, fast. In writing,
particularly for designs distributed asynchronously to senior staff, the
documents tend to under-explain the tradeoffs that Sarah has already
worked through verbally. The Q3 ledger RFC went through three rounds of
revision because reviewers couldn't reconstruct why specific alternatives
had been rejected. **Focus for next cycle:** I've asked Sarah to write a
"considered alternatives" section in every design doc, including the
specific reason each alternative was rejected. We'll review the first two
together before broader distribution.

## Rating: Exceeds Expectations.

Sarah's year has been a clear "Exceeds" — major delivery on the refund
pipeline, strong mentorship outcomes, and growth into cross-team
leadership. The written-communication growth area is real but it's a
narrow refinement of an already-strong skill, not a gap. The path to
Staff-level promotion next cycle runs through the ledger redesign and
through closing the written-communication gap.
```

### Example: self-review bullets (avoiding false modesty)

**False modesty:**
> "I worked on the refund system migration with the team this year. It went pretty well overall. I learned a lot and helped where I could."

**Calibrated self-review:**
> "I led the refund system migration in Q1-Q2. I owned the design, coordinated with four teams on deployment windows, and wrote the rollback runbook the team still uses. The migration shipped two weeks ahead of plan with zero customer incidents. My team of three engineers carried the implementation; my contribution was design, planning, and stakeholder management. **What I'd do differently:** I underestimated the cross-team review cycle by about three weeks. Next time I'd start the design-review process before completing the detailed design, in parallel rather than serial."

### Example: peer / 360 feedback (specific and behavioral)

```
## What works well about working with Tom

Tom's strongest contribution to my work this year was on the search
re-indexing project. I dropped a half-baked doc in our channel at 8am
asking for thirty minutes of his time. He read it before standup, came
in with three questions I hadn't seen — specifically about the
back-pressure behavior under shard rebalancing — and stayed for an
hour walking through an alternative design. I shipped a meaningfully
better system because of that single conversation, and I've gone back
to that pattern (drop a doc, ask for 30 minutes) with him three more
times since.

## What I'd ask Tom to consider

In larger architecture-review meetings, Tom contributes less than I
think he could. He has the deepest knowledge of the search system on
our team, but in the Q3 reviews his strongest observations came in the
followup chat rather than in the meeting itself. I think the team would
benefit from him anchoring the discussion in real time. Specific
suggestion: pre-write a one-paragraph position before the meeting and
lead with it in the first five minutes.
```

### Anti-example: the "praise sandwich" that buries the feedback

**Don't write:**
> "Maria is a wonderful teammate and a hard worker. She sometimes can be a bit late on deliverables but overall she's been a great addition to the team and I really appreciate her contributions."

This is Ruinous Empathy in writing — the real feedback ("late on deliverables") is buried in vague praise, has no specific example, and gives Maria no idea what to change.

**Rewrite (Radical Candor in writing):**
> "Maria's collaboration on the search project was the highlight of her year — particularly the cross-team review she ran in May. **Growth area: schedule discipline.** Three of Maria's six committed deliverables this cycle slipped by 2-4 weeks (the September dashboard, the October A/B framework, and the December roadmap). In two of the three cases I learned about the slip when the deadline arrived. **Next cycle:** Maria and I have agreed to weekly delivery checkpoints with explicit "still on track / at risk / slipped" status. Goal: 5/6 on-time delivery next cycle, with proactive escalation on the sixth."

## Anti-patterns

1. **The surprise review.** Negative feedback in writing that the employee has never heard verbally. Violates the no-surprises rule.
2. **The vague adjective.** "Strong communicator," "team player," "self-starter." Cut or replace with evidence.
3. **The praise sandwich that buries the feedback.** Real feedback hidden between two pieces of vague praise. Ruinous Empathy in written form.
4. **The character claim.** "He's arrogant," "She lacks confidence." Cut. Replace with behavior + impact.
5. **The performance-coded bias word.** "Abrasive," "shrill," "needs to smile more," "intense," "emotional." Disproportionately applied to women and underrepresented groups. Cut and rewrite around behavior.
6. **The rating-narrative mismatch.** "Meets" rating with three paragraphs of "Exceeds"-level accomplishments, or vice versa. Resolve the inconsistency.
7. **The recycled self-review.** Last year's bullets with this year's dates. Distinguishable in 30 seconds.
8. **The "I'm not good at self-promotion" disclaimer in a self-review.** The disclaimer doesn't help; the evidence does.
9. **The peer feedback that says only "great teammate."** Add specific behavior. If you can't, write the manager and explain why you can't.
10. **The promotion packet that confuses scope with impact.** "Led 5 projects" without naming the outcome of each is scope theater.
11. **The growth area that names a personality trait instead of a behavior.** "Tom is shy" is a personality claim. "Tom contributes less in 5+ person meetings than he does in pairs" is a behavior claim.
12. **The defensive calibration mention.** "I argued for a higher rating but the committee..." Undermines the review and the calibration system.

## Decision heuristics

- **Could the employee point to specific situations behind each claim?** If not, add examples or cut the claim.
- **Is the strongest growth area the *first* thing the employee will hear?** If yes, restructure — lead with strengths.
- **Have I used the same level of specificity for praise and for growth areas?** Asymmetry signals avoidance.
- **Is each claim about behavior or about character?** Behaviors are actionable; character claims are not.
- **Would I be comfortable reading this aloud to the employee?** If not, rewrite the parts that fail this test.
- **Does the rating match the narrative?** If not, fix one to match the other (usually fix the narrative).
- **Could this bullet be applied to anyone on the team?** If yes, it isn't specific enough.
- **Have I checked for demographic-bias-coded words?** "Abrasive," "intense," "emotional," "needs to be more confident" — search for these and rewrite around behavior.

## Quick reference: phrases to use and avoid

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| "Strong communicator" | "Wrote the four-paragraph summary that unblocked the X decision" |
| "Team player" | "Stepped into the on-call rotation when Y was on leave, owned three incidents" |
| "Weakness in [X]" | "Growth area for next cycle: [behavior] in [context]" |
| "Sometimes / occasionally" | Specific frequency or specific examples |
| "He's not detail-oriented" | "Three of the last five PRs needed follow-up commits to address review comments that were in the diff" |
| "She lacks confidence" | "In meetings with 5+ attendees, she contributes less than in smaller forums" |
| "Abrasive / intense / aggressive" | Describe the behavior and its observed impact |
| "I argued for a higher rating" | (Cut — discuss outside the written review) |
