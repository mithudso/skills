# Social Psychology — Hierarchy, Status, Conformity & Identity

The **"how does it work / what does the evidence actually show"** spoke of the `psychology` hub for **group-level** phenomena: how rank is acquired, how status is signaled, how people calibrate themselves against others, how groups bend individual behavior, and how identity flips on the in-group/out-group switch.

This field is the **epicenter of the replication crisis in psychology**. Several of its most famous studies (Stanford Prison, the textbook reading of Milgram, the original handicap principle) are wrong, exaggerated, or actively misrepresented in popular accounts. This spoke is built to give you the *robust* finding and flag the *fragile* one in the same breath. Treat the dramatic single-study anecdote with suspicion; trust the meta-analytic and cross-cultural regularity.

> **Boundary.** This spoke owns the *science of groups, rank, and social pressure*. **Persuasion/compliance tactics, cult dynamics, inoculation, resistance** → `psychology-influence-depth`. **Self-esteem / impostor / identity as an individual trait** → `psychology-confidence-identity`. **"How do I *use* this to nudge / drive adoption / win the room"** → `applied-psychology`. Rule of thumb: *"how does it work / what's the evidence?" → here. "what do I do with it?" → applied-psychology.*

> **Not clinical or political advice.** Educational synthesis of the research literature.

## 1. Social Hierarchy: Dominance vs Prestige (the dual-strategy model)

The central modern framework for human status. **Henrich & Gil-White (2001)** and **Cheng, Tracy, Foulsham, Kingstone & Henrich (2013, *JPSP*)** argue humans evolved **two distinct, viable routes to social rank**:

- **Dominance** — rank taken through *coercion, intimidation, fear, and control of resources/costs*. Phylogenetically ancient (shared with other primates). Status is **not freely conferred**; it is extracted. Correlates: aggression, narcissism, Machiavellianism, disagreeableness. Signaled by expansive nonverbal displays, anger.
- **Prestige** — rank *freely granted* by others in exchange for valued knowledge, skill, or generosity. Uniquely (or near-uniquely) human; rests on **cultural learning**: people defer to and copy skilled models, paying deference as the "price" of proximity and information. Correlates: agreeableness, conscientiousness, genuine self-esteem, social acceptance. Signaled by others' attention and deference, not by intimidation.

**Why this matters:** both strategies *work* ("Two Ways to the Top"). They are **independent dimensions**, not endpoints of one scale; a person can be high on both, one, or neither. They predict influence through different channels (fear/avoidance vs respect/copying) and produce different emotional signatures (hubristic vs authentic pride, per Tracy & Robins).

**Replication:** the dominance/prestige distinction is **well-supported** across self-report, peer-report, behavioral, and cross-cultural data. The evolutionary *origin story* for prestige (cultural-learning-driven deference) is a strong theory but, being a historical claim, is inferential rather than experimentally proven.

## 2. The biobehavioral basis of rank (handle with care)

Status has a real but **over-hyped** neuroendocrine layer.

- **Winner/loser effect** — testosterone tends to **rise after a win, fall after a loss** in competition, in both sexes (lower absolute levels in women). Reasonably replicable, though effect sizes are modest and moderated by mood and whether the outcome felt earned.
- **Challenge hypothesis** (Wingfield) — testosterone rises to meet status challenges and facilitates status-seeking/aggression. **Only partially supported in humans**: Dekkers et al. (2019) meta-analysis found the human evidence weaker and more conditional than the animal literature implies.
- **Dual-hormone hypothesis** — testosterone's link to dominance/status-seeking is **moderated by cortisol**: the T→status-behavior relationship holds when cortisol is *low* and weakens or reverses when cortisol is *high*. Intuitive and influential, but later large replications have been mixed; treat as a promising-but-unsettled interaction, not a law.

**Flag:** popular "testosterone = dominance, do a power pose to boost it" claims badly overstate this. Power-posing's hormonal/behavioral effects **failed to replicate** (Ranehill et al. 2015; Carney later disavowed the original). Rank is overwhelmingly *social and learned*; hormones are a modest, context-dependent modulator, not a driver.

## 3. Status signaling & costly signaling

Why do people burn resources to display rank? **Costly signaling theory**: a signal is *honest/credible* precisely because it is **expensive** — only high-quality (wealthy, fit, skilled) signalers can afford the waste, so receivers can trust it.

Intellectual lineage (note the cross-disciplinary convergence):
- **Veblen (1899)** — *conspicuous consumption*: the leisure class signals status through visibly wasteful spending and idleness. The original social-science statement.
- **Spence (1973)** — *job-market signaling*: costly education credibly signals worker quality even if it adds no skill; earned Spence a Nobel. The economics formalization.
- **Zahavi (1975)** — the **handicap principle**: extravagant biological traits (the peacock's tail) are honest signals *because* they are survival handicaps only fit individuals can bear.
- **Grafen (1990)** — game-theoretic model that made the handicap principle respectable; later recognized as essentially rediscovering Spence.

**Major replication-honest caveat:** Zahavi's *original* handicap principle is now widely regarded as **wrong as a mechanism**. **Penn & Számadó (2020, *Biological Reviews*)** detail how "an erroneous hypothesis became a scientific principle": the verbal handicap argument doesn't actually work; what salvaged honest-signaling theory was Grafen's *differential-cost* model (and index/Spence-style signals), not Zahavi's logic. So: **the phenomenon (costly displays as honest status signals) is real and the economic/Spencian core is sound, but do not present Zahavi's handicap reasoning as established; it's a famous case of a flawed idea canonized by repetition.**

Modern downstream: status signaling drives luxury markets, "countersignaling" (the secure high-status deliberately *under*-signal — quiet luxury), and digital status games.

## 4. Social comparison theory (Festinger, 1954)

**Festinger's** founding claim: people have a **drive to evaluate their opinions and abilities**; when *objective, non-social* yardsticks are unavailable, they evaluate **by comparison to other people** — preferably *similar* others. Nine formal hypotheses; the core drive-to-evaluate is among social psychology's most durable ideas.

Direction was added later:
- **Upward comparison** (Wheeler 1966) — comparing to someone *better*. Can **inspire** ("that's attainable → motivation") or **demoralize** ("I'll never get there → envy, lowered self-esteem"), depending on perceived attainability and self-relevance.
- **Downward comparison** (Wills 1981) — comparing to someone *worse*. Typically **boosts self-esteem / buffers threat**, but can produce guilt or fear ("that could be me").

The same comparison can help or hurt the *same person* depending on framing — a key nuance often lost in pop accounts.

**Relative deprivation** (Stouffer; Runciman; Crosby) is the sibling construct: discontent comes not from absolute lack but from **comparison to a reference group**. Explains why objectively well-off people feel aggrieved and why inequality salience drives unrest more than poverty alone.

**Modern application & honest caveat:** social media is a near-pure **upward-comparison engine** (curated highlight reels), and correlational work links heavy comparison-laden use to lower wellbeing. But the **causal** size is contested and often smaller than headlines claim — the comparison *mechanism* is robust; the "social media is destroying mental health" magnitude is an active, unsettled debate, not settled fact.

## 5. Conformity

**Sherif (1935), autokinetic effect / norm formation.** In a dark room a stationary point of light *appears* to move (autokinetic illusion). Individuals' estimates converge to a shared **group norm** that persists even when alone afterward, and is internalized. This is **informational influence**: ambiguity → others as a source of *truth*. Sherif also replicated norm-convergence patterns in adult and industrial-worker fieldwork (rarely cited next to the sexier studies).

**Asch (1951–56), line judgments.** With an *unambiguous* task (which line matches?), confederates unanimously give the wrong answer. ~**75% of participants conformed at least once**; ~**33% mean conformity rate** across critical trials. Because the answer is obvious, this isolates **normative influence**: going along to avoid standing out, often with *private disagreement* (public compliance ≠ private acceptance).

**Two mechanisms (Deutsch & Gerard 1955) — keep them straight:**
- **Informational** influence → you change your *belief* (you think the group knows better). Drives private acceptance. Stronger under ambiguity/expertise.
- **Normative** influence → you change your *public behavior* to fit in / avoid rejection, often without believing it.

**Asch's moderators (robust):** conformity collapses with even **one dissenting ally**; rises with group size up to ~3–4 then plateaus; rises with task difficulty/ambiguity; falls when responses are private.

**Replication & cultural correction — Bond & Smith (1996, *Psychological Bulletin*) meta-analysis** of 133 Asch-type studies across 17 nations:
- Conformity was **~50% higher in 1950s US samples than in later replications** — i.e., partly a **product of its conformist era**, declining over decades.
- Conformity is **higher in collectivist than individualist cultures**, and culture-level values predicted conformity *more strongly* than classic situational moderators like majority size.
- **Takeaway:** Asch's effect is real and replicable but **not a fixed human constant** — it's historically and culturally contingent. Cite the *mechanism* confidently; cite the *rate* with the era/culture attached.

## 6. Obedience to authority (Milgram) — the interpretation is the story

**Milgram (1961–63).** Participants ("teachers") were prodded by an experimenter to deliver escalating "shocks" to a "learner." **~65% went to the maximum 450 V** in the baseline condition. Milgram's framing: ordinary people will inflict harm under legitimate **authority** ("agentic state," blind obedience).

**What replicates:** the *behavior* is robust. **Burger (2009)** ran an ethics-constrained **"obedience-lite"** version stopping at 150 V: **~70% continued past 150 V** (vs Milgram's ~82.5% at that point), comparable decades later. A 2024+ modernized online replication likewise found **~86% continuing to 150 V**, with **no significant effect of prestige/venue/framing** (university vs not, science vs commercial), surprising since Milgram's own institutional-prestige claims predicted otherwise.

**Why the textbook story is wrong — three converging critiques:**
1. **Gina Perry (*Behind the Shock Machine*)** — archival work showed Milgram's results were messier and more variably reported than the clean "65%" headline; many participants saw through the deception; the experimenter improvised beyond the four scripted prods.
2. **Engaged followership (Haslam, Reicher, Birney)** — participants were **not blindly obedient**. Analysis of the prods shows obedience **rose with appeals to the *scientific mission*** ("the experiment requires that you continue") and **collapsed under the direct command** ("you have no choice, you must go on," the most order-like prod, produced the *most* defiance). People continued because they **identified with the experimenter's cause and saw themselves as helping science**, not because they surrendered agency. It's *identification and followership*, not Eichmann-style thoughtless obedience.
3. **Ethics** — the original studies caused severe, undisclosed distress and could not be run today; this is *why* modern work is "lite," which itself bounds what can be inferred.

**Bottom line:** Don't say "Milgram proved people blindly obey authority." Say: "people will harm others when an authority frames it as serving a *legitimate, valued goal they identify with* — obedience is **engaged followership**, modulated by identification, not mindless submission." The most-cited interpretation in the field's most-cited study is the part that *didn't* survive.

## 7. In-group / out-group & Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner)

The field's most **robustly replicated** body of work.

**Minimal group paradigm (Tajfel 1970, 1971).** Strangers are split into groups by a *trivial, meaningless* criterion (over- vs under-estimating dots; preferring Klee vs Kandinsky paintings; sometimes assignment was openly random). With **no prior contact, no self-interest, no competition, no interaction**, participants nonetheless **allocate more reward to anonymous in-group members**, and will even *sacrifice absolute in-group gain to maximize the in-group's gain over the out-group* (relative differentiation > absolute payoff). **Mere categorization is sufficient to produce in-group favoritism.** This is one of social psychology's most reliably reproduced effects.

**Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner 1979).** Part of the self-concept derives from group memberships (**social identity**). To maintain **positive self-esteem**, people seek **positive distinctiveness** for their in-group → favorable in-group/out-group comparisons → in-group bias, stereotyping, and (under threat) out-group derogation. Three processes: **categorization → identification → comparison.**

**Self-Categorization Theory (Turner et al. 1987).** The cognitive engine underneath SIT: the self is categorized at varying levels of abstraction (me / us / human); when a **social** identity is salient, people **depersonalize**, perceiving self and others as interchangeable group exemplars (prototypes). Explains stereotyping, group cohesion, and **group-based social influence** (you're swayed by in-group members because their view defines the prototype). SCT reframes conformity itself as *referent informational influence*: agreeing with those you categorize as similar.

**Realistic Conflict Theory (Sherif, Robbers Cave, 1954/1961).** Intergroup hostility from **real competition over scarce resources**; cooperation toward **superordinate goals** (goals neither group can achieve alone) reduces it. *Caveats:* Robbers Cave was a small (n≈22), researcher-managed boys'-camp study, a prior iteration "failed" and went unpublished, and experimenters actively engineered the conflict, so the dramatic narrative is shaky. But the **core relationship** (perceived competition → out-group hostility) holds up in broader observational and quasi-experimental work even when the resource is *symbolic*. Note: the **minimal-group** finding shows competition is *not necessary* for bias (categorization alone suffices), so RCT describes one important *amplifier*, not the root cause.

## 8. Hierarchy-justifying ideology: SDO & System Justification

Two frameworks for *why people accept and defend inequality* — including members of disadvantaged groups.

- **Social Dominance Theory / Orientation (Sidanius & Pratto 1999).** Societies organize into group-based hierarchies; **Social Dominance Orientation (SDO)** is an individual-difference variable = preference for **group-based hierarchy and inequality** in general. High-SDO individuals endorse "legitimizing myths" that justify dominance. **Ho et al. (2015)** refined SDO into two facets — **SDO-D** (active dominance/aggression toward subordinate groups) and **SDO-E** (preference for anti-egalitarianism/unequal distribution). SDO is a **robust, well-validated predictor** of prejudice, ideology, and political attitudes.
- **System Justification Theory (Jost & Banaji 1994).** People are motivated to **defend, bolster, and rationalize the status quo** — even at the expense of self- and group-interest, producing the striking phenomenon of *out-group favoritism among the disadvantaged* (e.g., implicit pro-high-status bias in low-status groups). The **measurement and core phenomena are solid**; the strong "system-justification *motive*" causal claim is debated (some effects re-explain via SDT or self-interest).

**Contrast for routing:** SIT (§7) explains in-group *favoritism* and identity; SDT/SJT explain hierarchy *maintenance and legitimation*. They are complementary lenses, sometimes rivals, on the same intergroup terrain.

## 9. The Stanford Prison Experiment — what is actually true (a debunk you must know)

**Do not cite the SPE as evidence for anything.** Zimbardo's 1971 study (college men randomly assigned "guard"/"prisoner" roles; "guards" turned cruel; ended early) is among the most famous studies in psychology and **among the most discredited.**

**Le Texier (2019, *American Psychologist*)**, using Zimbardo's own archives and participant interviews, documented:
- **Guards were coached** — given explicit instructions to be tough and create an oppressive atmosphere; cruelty was *demanded*, not spontaneously emergent.
- A key "spontaneous" breakdown was **acted** (a "prisoner" admitted faking distress to get out to study for an exam).
- The study had **no real control, a tiny n, demand characteristics everywhere**, was modeled on a class exercise run months earlier, and was **never a controlled experiment** — closer to improvised theater.
- It **does not replicate.** The **BBC Prison Study (Reicher & Haslam 2002/2006)**, a genuine attempt, found participants did *not* automatically conform to brutal roles; tyranny emerged only under specific identity conditions, supporting an **identity/followership** account over Zimbardo's "the situation/role makes good people evil."

**Correct takeaway:** the SPE's lesson is *methodological* — how charisma, demand characteristics, and a great story can canonize a non-finding for 50 years. Its substantive claim ("toxic situations automatically corrupt anyone") is **not supported**; the better-evidenced view is that harm-doing requires **active leadership and identification** (cf. §6 engaged followership).

## Practical applications (mechanism → use)

- **Reading a room / org politics:** distinguish *dominance* (compliance via fear) from *prestige* (deference via respect) — they need opposite responses; only prestige scales trust. → operator framing in `applied-psychology`.
- **Designing groups:** to cut intergroup hostility, build **superordinate goals** and a **shared superordinate identity** (recategorization) — competition isn't required to *create* bias, so removing competition alone won't remove it.
- **Conformity in teams/meetings:** dissent collapses under normative pressure; **a single visible ally** restores independent judgment; **private/anonymous input** (pre-mortems, blind voting) defeats normative conformity; surface *informational* uncertainty explicitly so people stop using the loudest voice as truth.
- **Status & marketing:** costly/conspicuous signals work because cost certifies value; expect **countersignaling** from the genuinely secure.
- **Wellbeing/product:** upward social comparison is the core harm vector of comparison-heavy feeds — but state the evidence honestly (mechanism robust, causal magnitude contested).
- **Ethics guardrail:** when invoking Milgram/SPE for "the situation made them do it," **correct the record** — harm-doing runs through *identification and active leadership*, which makes responsibility (and prevention) more, not less, tractable.

## How to use this skill well

1. **Lead with the robust finding, flag the fragile one in the same breath.** This field's fame is inversely correlated with reliability for several headline studies.
2. **Never cite SPE as evidence**; cite it only as a cautionary tale, and pair the Milgram *behavior* with the *engaged-followership* correction.
3. **Separate mechanism from magnitude** — the social-comparison and conformity *mechanisms* are solid; their *effect sizes* are historically/culturally contingent.
4. **Two independent dimensions, not one scale** — dominance/prestige, SDO-D/SDO-E, normative/informational. Don't collapse them.
5. When the user wants to *act* on any of this, hand off to `applied-psychology`; stay in the "what's true" lane here.
