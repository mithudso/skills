# Job description writing

## Overview

A job description is a marketing document, a legal document, and a filter — at the same time. It must attract candidates the company wants, deter candidates who would be a poor fit, comply with US pay-transparency law in 15+ states, and avoid language that systematically suppresses applications from underrepresented groups.

The default failing pattern is a JD that reads like a wish list: 20 requirements, a salary listed as "competitive," and three "rockstar" anti-patterns in the first paragraph. The default failing posture is to copy the previous JD for the same title and change two words. This skill provides the structure, the inclusivity checklist, and the compliance rules to do better.

## Core concepts

### 1. The structural skeleton: what you'll do / what we look for / what we offer

Every effective JD answers three questions, in order:

1. **What you'll do** — Responsibilities. Day-to-day work. Outcomes the role owns.
2. **What we look for** — Requirements split into must-haves (3-5 dealbreakers) and nice-to-haves (open-ended). Skills, experiences, attributes.
3. **What we offer** — Compensation range (legally required in many states), benefits, growth path, team context, working model (remote/hybrid/on-site), location.

Many companies add a fourth section: **Who we are** (company mission, team description). Keep it short — 2-3 sentences. Candidates skip company-overview boilerplate.

### 2. Responsibilities vs requirements: keep them separated

A common failure is mixing responsibilities and requirements into one list, which makes the JD harder to scan and easier to over-stuff.

**Responsibilities** describe what the person does. Verbs in the present tense: "Design and ship," "Mentor," "Own."

**Requirements** describe what the person must already have. Adjectives and nouns: "5 years of," "Experience with," "Familiarity with."

Separating them forces the writer to count both lists and notice when either is bloated.

### 3. Must-haves vs nice-to-haves — and the application-gap evidence

**Cap must-haves at 3-5.** Be ruthless. A must-have is a dealbreaker — if the candidate doesn't have it, you won't hire them.

**Everything else goes in nice-to-haves**, with light language: "familiar with," "exposure to," "bonus if you've."

**The evidence:** A widely-cited 2013 HP internal report popularized the claim that women apply for jobs only when they meet 100% of the listed requirements, while men apply at 60%. That specific stat is contested — more rigorous research (Mohr, HBR 2014; Bohnet 2016; LinkedIn 2019) finds the gap is real but smaller (women apply at ~56% qualifications, men at ~52%). But the directional finding — that long requirement lists deter applications from underrepresented groups more than from majority groups — is well-supported across multiple studies. Iris Bohnet's *What Works: Gender Equality by Design* (Harvard, 2016) treats this as design-grade evidence: shorten the list, attract a broader pool.

**Heuristic:** If your must-haves list has more than 5 items, ask which two you'd drop. Then drop them.

### 4. Inclusive language: the gender-coded word audit

Textio's research on 78,000+ engineering job posts found that gender-coded language measurably skews who applies. Roles where men were hired had nearly twice as many masculine-coded phrases as feminine-coded ones; roles where women were hired had the inverse. Gender-neutral content correlates with ~40% more applications across industries; Textio scores above 70 correlate with 30-50% more applications from underrepresented groups.

**Masculine-coded words to avoid or balance:** aggressive, ambitious, analytical, assertive, challenge, competitive, confident, decisive, determined, dominant, driven, fearless, forceful, independent, individual, ninja, outspoken, principled, rockstar, self-reliant, superior.

**Feminine-coded words (rarely flagged, but worth checking for tone):** collaborative, committed, connected, considerate, cooperative, dependable, enthusiastic, honest, interpersonal, kind, loyal, nurturing, polite, responsible, supportive, sympathetic, together, trust, understanding, warm.

**Action:** Run the JD through a tool like Textio, Gender Decoder, or Datapeople. Aim for balance or feminine-leaning if the role is historically male-coded (engineering, sales, finance).

### 5. Anti-acronym, anti-jargon discipline

If a candidate has to Google an acronym to understand the JD, you've narrowed your pool to current insiders.

**Common offenders:** internal product names, internal team names, internal tooling acronyms, industry acronyms used without expansion ("PRD," "TPM," "SDR").

**Rule:** Expand every acronym on first use. Cut internal product/team names unless the candidate can plausibly know them.

### 6. Skills-based vs experience-based listings

Two competing schools:

- **Experience-based:** "5+ years of experience in X." Easy to write, easy to filter, but tends to over-credit time-in-seat and under-credit demonstrated skill.
- **Skills-based:** "Ability to design and ship a production-grade X system." Harder to write, but better at attracting career-changers, non-traditional candidates, and self-taught talent.

**Best practice (Bohnet, Project Include):** Lead with skills-based language. If years-of-experience is a real constraint (regulated industries, security clearance), state the minimum and explain why. Avoid "10+ years" unless it's a senior IC or executive role and you actually mean it.

### 7. US state pay-transparency laws (as of 2026)

States that require salary range disclosure in job postings as of mid-2026 (verify before posting):

- **California** (15+ employees) — required
- **Colorado** (all employers with 1+ CO employee) — required
- **Connecticut** — on request and at offer
- **Hawaii** (50+ employees) — required
- **Illinois** (15+ employees, effective Jan 2025) — required
- **Maine** (10+ employees, effective Jan 2026) — required
- **Maryland** — required
- **Massachusetts** (25+ employees, effective Oct 2025) — required
- **Minnesota** — required
- **Nevada** — to candidates after interview
- **New Jersey** (10+ employees, effective Jun 2025) — required
- **New York** — required statewide; NYC has additional rules
- **Rhode Island** — on request
- **Vermont** — required
- **Washington** (15+ employees) — required + benefits description
- **Washington D.C.** — required
- **Delaware** — enacted, effective September 26, 2027

**Practical rule:** If you post a JD that may be viewed or applied to from any of these jurisdictions (which is essentially any remote role posted publicly), include a good-faith salary range. The range should be defensible — listing "$50,000–$500,000" is non-compliant in most states.

### 8. Day-in-the-life sections

A short "A day in the life" or "Your first 90 days" section is one of the highest-leverage additions to a JD. It signals concreteness, reduces application uncertainty, and disqualifies poor-fit candidates before they apply.

**Example structure:**

```
In your first 90 days, you'll:
- Week 1-2: Onboard with the team, shadow on-call, complete platform tour.
- Week 3-6: Ship your first small change end-to-end with a mentor.
- Week 7-12: Own a piece of the [X] subsystem; lead one design review.
```

This is also where vague culture claims get cashed out into observable behavior.

### 9. DEI-friendly criteria

Beyond gender-coded language, watch for:

- **Age bias signals:** "digital native," "energetic," "fast-paced young team."
- **Ability bias signals:** "must be able to stand for 8 hours" (only if literally required), "healthy," "active."
- **Education bias:** "degree from a top university" — replace with the actual skill the degree proxies for, or specify the field of study and state that equivalent experience is accepted.
- **Citizenship bias:** "must be US citizen" — only legally permissible for specific roles (clearance, ITAR). For other roles, use "must be authorized to work in the US."
- **Cultural-fit signals:** "must love startups," "ping-pong table," "we're a family." These select for sameness.

### 10. The opener: who is the JD for?

The first 2-3 sentences are skimmed. They should answer:

- What is this role?
- What team or product is it on?
- What's the most concrete thing the person will do?

**Weak opener:** "We're looking for a passionate, driven Software Engineer to join our innovative team and help us change the world."

**Stronger opener:** "We're hiring a backend engineer for the Payments team. You'll own the refund pipeline that processes $40M/month and ship two new payment provider integrations this year."

## Templates and examples

### Template: inclusive JD skeleton

```
[Role title] — [Team/Product] — [Location/Remote policy]

# About the role
[2-3 sentences: the team, the product, the most concrete piece of work the person will own.]

# What you'll do
- [Verb + object, present tense. 5-8 bullets.]
- [Outcome-oriented when possible: "Ship X" beats "Work on X."]

# What we look for (must-have)
- [3-5 dealbreakers only.]
- [Skills-based language; mention years-of-experience only when meaningful.]

# Nice to have
- [Open-ended list. Use "familiar with," "exposure to," "bonus if."]
- [This is where the wishlist lives. It can be long.]

# A day in the life / Your first 90 days
- [Optional but high-value. Week-by-week or month-by-month.]

# What we offer
- Compensation: $[low]–$[high] [base]. [Equity range if applicable.] [Bonus if applicable.]
- Benefits: [Health, retirement, time-off summary.]
- Working model: [Remote/hybrid/on-site] / [Time zones] / [Travel %].
- Growth: [Team size, manager, IC ladder, mentorship.]

# About [Company]
[2-3 sentences. Cut the boilerplate.]

[Company] is an equal opportunity employer. We welcome applicants from all
backgrounds and do not discriminate on the basis of [list per local law].
We are committed to providing reasonable accommodations for candidates with
disabilities — please contact [email] if you need one.
```

### Example: inclusive backend engineer JD (compliant, balanced language)

```
Senior Backend Engineer — Payments Platform — Remote (US)

# About the role
We're hiring a senior backend engineer for the Payments Platform team. You'll
own the refund-and-dispute pipeline, which processes about $40M/month across
four payment providers, and lead the design of a new ledger subsystem in 2026.

# What you'll do
- Design and ship features in the refund pipeline (Go, PostgreSQL, Temporal).
- Lead the ledger redesign — gather requirements, write the RFC, drive review.
- Partner with the Finance team on reconciliation tooling.
- Mentor two engineers on the team; participate in on-call rotation (one week
  every six).
- Improve operational telemetry — dashboards, alerts, runbooks.

# What we look for (must-have)
- Production experience designing and operating a transactional system (payments,
  ledger, ordering, or similar). Years are less important than what you've shipped.
- Strong design instincts — you can walk a team through tradeoffs and document them.
- Comfortable in a typed, statically-compiled language (Go, Rust, Java, Kotlin, C#).

# Nice to have
- Experience with payment provider integrations (Stripe, Adyen, Braintree).
- Familiarity with double-entry accounting concepts.
- Exposure to Temporal, Cadence, or a similar workflow engine.

# Your first 90 days
- Weeks 1-2: Onboard, shadow on-call, complete the platform tour.
- Weeks 3-6: Ship your first refund-pipeline change end-to-end with your mentor.
- Weeks 7-12: Take point on a small piece of the ledger redesign; lead one
  design review.

# What we offer
- Compensation: $185,000–$230,000 base + equity (range 0.05–0.15%) + 10% bonus target.
- Benefits: Full medical/dental/vision, 401(k) with 4% match, 20 days PTO, 12 weeks
  parental leave, $2,000/year learning budget.
- Working model: Fully remote within the US. Overlap with Pacific time required
  4 hours/day. Two team off-sites per year (travel covered).
- Growth: Team of 6, reporting to the Engineering Manager. Senior IC ladder open
  to Staff in 18-24 months for the right candidate.

# About Acme
Acme processes payments for 8,000 small businesses across the US. We're a 220-person
company, profitable since 2023, and we ship every weekday.

Acme is an equal opportunity employer. We welcome applicants from all backgrounds
and do not discriminate on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, sexual
orientation, gender identity, national origin, age, disability, or veteran status.
If you need a reasonable accommodation during the interview process, contact
accessibility@acme.com.
```

### Example: rewrite from biased to inclusive

**Before:**
> We're looking for a rockstar engineer who can dominate the backend stack. You should be aggressive about shipping, fearless in the face of legacy code, and a digital native who lives and breathes JavaScript. 10+ years required. Must be a hard-charging self-starter who thrives in a fast-paced, high-pressure environment. Salary: competitive.

**After:**
> We're hiring a backend engineer to ship features on our checkout service. You'll work with the existing Node.js codebase, partner with the platform team on a planned TypeScript migration, and own one or two services end-to-end. We're looking for someone with production experience in a typed backend language (TypeScript, Go, Java, etc.) and comfort working with legacy code. Salary range: $145,000–$180,000 base + equity. Remote within the US.

## Anti-patterns

1. **Rockstar / ninja / guru / wizard / superstar / 10x.** Gender-coded and exclusionary. Use the actual job title.
2. **The 20-item must-haves list.** Cap at 3-5. Move everything else to nice-to-haves.
3. **"Competitive salary."** Non-compliant in 15+ states. Defaults to "we'll pay you as little as you'll accept."
4. **"Digital native" / "energetic" / "fast-paced young team."** Age-coded.
5. **"Must love [company]" / "We're a family."** Cultural-fit signaling that selects for sameness.
6. **Internal acronyms without expansion.** Filters for current insiders.
7. **"Bachelor's degree required" for roles where the degree isn't actually required.** Filters out career-changers and self-taught talent.
8. **The copy-paste from the last JD.** Roles evolve; the JD should too.
9. **The 800-word company-overview opener.** Candidates skip it. Move it to the bottom and cut it to 2-3 sentences.
10. **Years-of-experience inflation.** "10+ years of React" — React is 13 years old, and rounding to "senior" is more honest.
11. **Missing remote-policy clarity.** "Remote-friendly" without specifying time zones, in-office days, or travel expectations is a recipe for mismatched expectations.
12. **The salary range that spans 3x.** "$50,000-$150,000" is not a good-faith range. Most states require a defensible range, typically ≤30% spread.

## Decision heuristics

- **Count the must-haves.** If more than 5, cut.
- **Count the gender-coded words.** Aim for balance; lean feminine if the role is historically male-coded.
- **Is a salary range posted?** If the role could be applied to from CA, CO, NY, WA, or any other pay-transparency state, yes.
- **Could a smart non-insider read this and know what the job is?** If acronyms or internal product names are blocking that, expand or cut them.
- **Does the JD say where the person will work?** Remote / hybrid / on-site / travel — be specific.
- **Is the "About us" section under 100 words?** If not, cut.
- **Does the JD signal a working model that matches reality?** Don't promise "work-life balance" if the team ships nights and weekends.

## Compliance checklist (US, 2026)

- [ ] Salary range posted if applicants in CA, CO, HI, IL, ME, MD, MA, MN, NJ, NY, VT, WA, or DC may apply.
- [ ] Benefits summary included (required in CO, WA).
- [ ] Equal-opportunity statement included.
- [ ] Reasonable-accommodation contact provided.
- [ ] No protected-class requirements (citizenship outside cleared roles, age, marital status, disability unless bona-fide).
- [ ] If the role is remote, time-zone and overlap expectations stated.
