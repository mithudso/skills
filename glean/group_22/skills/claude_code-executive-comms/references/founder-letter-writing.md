# Founder Letter Writing

## Overview

A founder letter is the annual public document a CEO or co-founder writes directly to owners — shareholders, users, or the general "fellow travelers" audience — to account for the year, reaffirm operating principles, admit mistakes, and frame what comes next. It is the genre Warren Buffett perfected at Berkshire Hathaway (1977–present), Jeff Bezos institutionalized at Amazon (1997–2020), and Patrick and John Collison adopted at Stripe (2019–present).

The form is distinct from:

- An internal executive memo (which talks down the org chart, not out to owners)
- A press release or marketing post (which is selling, not reporting)
- An op-ed (which argues a public-interest point, not company-specific accountability)
- A whitepaper (which makes a technical or policy case, not a stewardship report)

What makes a founder letter work is the combination of **candor**, **operating principles that repeat year over year**, and a **long-horizon frame** ("Day 1," "10-year zoom-out," "owner's manual") that lets the reader weigh the current year against the company's deeper trajectory. Done well, the letter is the single document that most clearly shows whether the founder is a missionary building a real business or a mercenary flipping a story.

## Core Concepts

### 1. The owner-as-partner stance

Write to a specific reader who is a long-term owner, not a quarterly trader. Buffett's now-famous frame is to write "as if to my sister Bertie" — an intelligent non-specialist who owns shares and deserves the truth in plain English. The pronoun is "we" or "your company," never "the corporation." Jargon, lingo, and acronyms are introduced from the familiar before the unfamiliar, or banished entirely.

### 2. The Toulmin-style spine

The letter follows a three-beat argument:

1. **Where we are** — the year's results in plain terms, the operating metrics that matter, and what the numbers do and do not say.
2. **What we learned** — including what went wrong, what surprised us, and what changed our mind.
3. **Where we're going** — the unchanging principles plus the bets we are placing for the next 5–10 years.

This spine is load-bearing. Optional sections (capital allocation, M&A, employee notes, industry commentary) hang off it.

### 3. Recurring-principles section

The most-quoted founder letters repeat a short list of operating principles every year, often verbatim. Bezos re-attached the 1997 letter to every subsequent letter for 20 years. Buffett restates Berkshire's "owner-related business principles" in nearly every letter. Stripe restates its mission and "increase the GDP of the internet" framing.

Repetition is a feature, not a bug. Owners learn what the company will and will not do, and new readers can onboard in one document.

### 4. "What I was wrong about" honesty

The credibility-buying section. Name a specific mistake from the last year (or earlier), explain why you made it, and say what you changed. Buffett famously confessed his Dexter Shoe acquisition cost shareholders billions. Jack Dorsey's Block letters walk through "what we're fixing." Netflix has written that the company is "nowhere near where we want to be."

Vague self-criticism ("we have work to do") does not count. Name the mistake, name the dollars or users or quarters it cost, name the change.

### 5. Day 1 / long-horizon frame

A reusable mental model that locates the current year in a much longer arc. Amazon's "It's still Day 1" answered the implicit question "are you a mature, declining business yet?" with "no, we are still acting like a startup, and here is how." Buffett's variant is the 10- and 20-year per-share book value comparison printed on the first page. Pick a frame, stick to it, and refer to it in every letter.

### 6. Missionary vs mercenary framing

Bezos's 2007 frame: "Mercenaries try to flip their stock. Missionaries love their product, service, and customers, and are trying to build a great service. Paradoxically, it's usually the missionaries who make more money." A founder letter should make clear which side of that line the company sits on, in the form of decisions made (or refused) during the year.

### 7. Capital allocation as moral act

For public-company letters and serious private-company letters, treat capital allocation as the most important decision the CEO makes that year. What was earned, what was reinvested, what was returned to owners, what was acquired, what was passed on. Buffett's letters are essentially capital-allocation reports with biographical commentary. If your letter has no capital section, owners assume you don't think about it.

### 8. Operating metrics over GAAP theater

Pick 3–5 metrics that genuinely run the business and report them every year. Amazon used free cash flow per share. Stripe reports total volume processed. Berkshire reports operating earnings and per-share book value. Avoid presenting non-GAAP metrics that flatter the picture without grounding them in real economics.

### 9. Voice: short sentences, plain words, dry humor

Buffett uses short, light transitions ("but," "yet," "and," "so") and conversational sentence rhythm. Imperatives ("Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut") are welcome. Avoid corporate passive voice, McKinsey nouns, and AI-isms ("leverage synergies," "robust framework," "comprehensive solution"). If you would not say it out loud to a smart friend, cut it.

### 10. The signed sign-off

End with a signature and a date. Two co-founders sign together if the company is co-founded. No PR ghostwriting credit — owners are paying for the founder's judgment, not the comms team's polish. If the letter is heavily ghostwritten, the founder should at minimum dictate the spine and write the honesty section themselves.

## Templates and Examples

### Founder-letter skeleton (1,500–4,000 words)

```text
To our shareholders / users / fellow owners,

[OPENING — 100–200 words]
Anchor the year in one sentence. State the headline result (revenue, volume,
users) and the headline disappointment, side by side. Restate the long-horizon
frame ("It's still Day 1," "Year N of a 30-year company," etc.) in one line.

[WHERE WE ARE — 400–800 words]
The 3–5 operating metrics, reported the same way every year.
Plain-English commentary on what the numbers mean and what they do not mean.
One paragraph each on the major business lines or product segments.
A short "industry context" paragraph if (and only if) it changes how owners
should read the numbers.

[WHAT WE LEARNED / WHAT I WAS WRONG ABOUT — 300–600 words]
One named, specific mistake. The cost. The change.
One named surprise (positive or negative) that updated our model.
Optionally, one paragraph on a debate inside the company that the founder
lost or changed sides on.

[OPERATING PRINCIPLES — 200–500 words, mostly verbatim year to year]
The 4–8 things the company will always do and will never do.
Owner-related principles (how we think about your money).
Customer-related principles (who we are building for).
Employee-related principles (what kind of company we are inside).

[WHERE WE'RE GOING — 400–900 words]
The 2–4 bets for the next 5–10 years. Each bet stated as a falsifiable claim,
not a marketing slogan.
The capital-allocation plan: how this year's earnings will be deployed.
The unchanged long-horizon frame, restated.

[CLOSING — 100–200 words]
A thank-you to owners, customers, and employees, in that order.
A signed sign-off with the founder's name(s) and the date.

— [Founder name]
[Co-founder name, if applicable]
[Month Year]
```

### Sample opening (Buffett register)

> To the Shareholders of Acme Corp:
>
> Your company earned $412 million in operating profit in 2026, up 18% from 2025. We deployed $290 million of that into two acquisitions and a buyback at prices we will defend on a separate page. We also wrote off $44 million on a 2023 acquisition that did not work out, and I will explain that below. It is still early — year nine of what we have always told you is a 30-year company.

### Sample "what I was wrong about" section

> Last year I told you we would have our second product line generating $50 million in revenue by year-end. We finished at $11 million. The mistake was not the product; the mistake was mine. I assigned the launch to a team that was already running the core business, and I underweighted how much of their attention the core would demand in a year when it grew 40%. We have since hired a dedicated GM and reset the plan. I expect $25 million from the line in 2027, and I am telling you that number now so you can hold me to it.

### Sample operating-principles block (reusable)

> **What we will always do.** Report the metrics that actually run the business, in the same form, every year. Treat capital as scarce. Treat customers as the people who pay our salaries. Treat employees as adults.
>
> **What we will never do.** Manage to quarterly earnings. Issue stock to ourselves outside of an approved plan published to you. Buy back shares above intrinsic value. Acquire companies whose economics we cannot describe to you in one paragraph.

### Sample Day-1 / long-horizon close

> It is still Day 1 for us. The market we are in is, at most, 8% built out. Our job over the next decade is to make the next 92% boring — to keep the company small in its instincts even as it gets large in its footprint. If we do that, the numbers will take care of themselves. If we forget, no number on this letter will save us.

## Anti-Patterns

- **PR-spin letter.** Every metric reframed to look like a win, no mistakes named, no specific dollar costs admitted. Owners discount the entire letter, including the parts that are true.
- **Earnings-call regurgitation.** The letter recaps last quarter's investor deck in prose. Wastes the one annual document where the founder gets to speak directly.
- **Mission-statement-as-letter.** Three pages of "we believe" with no numbers, no specifics, no admissions. Reads as a recruiting brochure and tells owners the founder is not running the business at the level of capital.
- **First-year-only candor.** A founder writes one brutally honest letter, gets praised for it, then quietly retreats to anodyne language in years two and three. Owners notice.
- **Ghostwritten throughout.** Voice shifts between paragraphs, principles change every year, the honesty section is generic. The mercenary tell.
- **No recurring frame.** Each year invents a new metaphor, a new principles list, and a new structure. Owners cannot tell year-over-year whether the company is improving.
- **Cute over clear.** Long opening anecdote, extended sports metaphor, or a vacation story that buries the news. Buffett earns folksy because he reports the numbers first.
- **No signature, no date.** Reads as a corporate document, not a founder document. The credibility-buying move is the signature; do not skip it.
- **AI-flavored prose.** "Leverage," "robust," "comprehensive," "best-in-class," "synergies," "delight our customers." If the sentence could appear in any company's letter, cut it.
- **Hiding the mistake in a footnote.** If the loss is material, it belongs in a paragraph in the body, not a parenthetical or a Note 14.

## Decision Heuristics

- **Should this be a founder letter or an executive memo?** Founder letter if the audience is owners/users/the public and the cadence is annual. Executive memo if the audience is staff/board and the cadence is monthly or event-driven. Use `executive-comms` for the latter.
- **How long?** 1,500 words minimum to do the spine justice. 4,000 words is the comfortable ceiling. Bezos averaged ~2,500. Buffett often runs 10,000+, but he has 50 years of permission. First-time founder letters should aim for 2,000–2,500.
- **First-person singular or plural?** Singular ("I") for the honesty section and capital-allocation judgments. Plural ("we") for the company's actions and principles. Mixing them on purpose, in the right places, is part of the voice.
- **Include forward guidance?** Specific numbers ("$25 million from the new line in 2027") build credibility if you are willing to be held to them. Vague guidance ("strong growth ahead") destroys credibility. If you will not commit to a number, do not gesture at one.
- **Should the founder write the draft?** Yes, at least the honesty section and the spine. Polish from comms is fine. Full ghostwriting is a tell that owners learn to read.
- **Publish under company brand or personal byline?** Berkshire signs as Buffett. Amazon signed as Bezos. Stripe signs as Patrick and John. The personal byline is the genre. Avoid "From the desk of the CEO."
- **Reattach the original founding letter?** Bezos did this for 20 years. Recommended once the company has a real story and the original letter has aged well. Don't manufacture a "founding letter" retroactively.
- **What if there's no good news?** Then the letter is more important, not less. Read Buffett's 2001 letter (post-September 11, dotcom collapse) for the template. Name the conditions, name what you did and didn't do, name what you will do next.
- **First-ever founder letter (year 1)?** You have no prior year to reference, so the spine compresses: shorter "Where we are" (one paragraph of founding context plus current state), no "What we learned vs last year's letter" callback, full-length "Operating principles" (this is the letter that defines them for every future year), and a longer "Where we're going" to compensate. Aim for 1,500–2,000 words. The 1997 Bezos letter is the canonical example — keep a copy of it open while drafting.
- **Private company vs public company?** Public-company letters carry SEC-adjacent obligations: forward statements need risk-factor language, material disclosures cannot be made first in the letter, and the audience includes traders alongside owners. Private-company letters (Stripe, pre-IPO Amazon-style) can be more candid, more specific about strategy, and more willing to name competitors. Both keep the same spine; public letters trade some candor for compliance.
