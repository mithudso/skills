# Medicaid Estate Recovery Program (MERP)

> **Educational only — NOT legal or financial advice.** Estate recovery rules are complex, vary by state, and interact with probate law; consult a NC elder law attorney for individual situations.

**verified-as-of: 2026-06-22**

---

## 1. What Is Medicaid Estate Recovery?

**Medicaid Estate Recovery** is a federal requirement that states seek repayment from the **estates of deceased Medicaid enrollees** for certain Medicaid costs. Federal law (42 U.S.C. § 1396p(b)) requires states to have an estate recovery program; the scope of recovery and hardship exemptions vary by state.

The most well-known scenario: Medicaid paid for nursing home care for an elderly person, and after the person's death, Medicaid files a claim against the person's home or estate to recover those costs.

---

## 2. Who Is Subject to Estate Recovery

### Mandatory Recovery
States MUST attempt recovery for services provided to individuals who were:
- **Age 55 or older** when they received Medicaid services (including nursing facility care, hospital services, and prescription drugs)
- **Permanently institutionalized** regardless of age (nursing facility, ICF/I/DD, or other medical institution)

### What Services Can Be Recovered
At minimum: nursing facility services, home and community-based services, and related hospital/prescription drug services for individuals 55+ or permanently institutionalized.

States MAY (optionally) expand recovery to **all Medicaid services** for individuals 55+. NC's approach is described below.

### ACA Expansion Enrollees
The ACA prohibited estate recovery for services provided to **expansion adults** (19–64, below 138% FPL) **except** for nursing facility services and long-term care. This protects younger Medicaid expansion enrollees from estate recovery for routine medical care.

---

## 3. Protected Assets During Life (Not Estate Recovery Rules)

Estate recovery happens **after death** — it does not affect assets or property while the person or their spouse is alive. While alive:
- **Spousal impoverishment protections**: A community spouse (the non-Medicaid spouse) keeps the home, minimum monthly income allowance, and Community Spouse Resource Allowance — Medicaid cannot force sale of the home while a spouse is alive.
- **Dependent children**: Recovery is delayed if there are minor or disabled dependent children.

---

## 4. What "Estate" Means

**Probate estate (federal minimum)**: Assets that pass through probate — property the individual owned alone at death, without a surviving joint tenant, beneficiary designation, or trust.

**Expanded estate (optional)**: States may expand recovery to **non-probate assets** — joint tenancy property, life estates, living trusts, annuities, pay-on-death accounts. NC's expanded definition is described below.

---

## 5. NC Medicaid Estate Recovery (MERP)

**NC MERP** is administered by NCDHHS Division of Health Benefits (DHB). Key NC rules:

### What NC Recovers
NC pursues estate recovery for:
- Medicaid payments for **nursing facility**, **ICF/I/DD**, **intermediate care**, and **home and community-based services** for individuals age 55+
- Related hospital and prescription drug costs in connection with those services
- NC does NOT pursue routine medical care recovery from most expansion enrollees

### Expanded Estate Definition in NC
NC uses an **expanded estate** definition (GS 108A-70.5 et seq.):
- Probate assets
- **Life estates and interests in real property** (including remainder interests)
- **Living trusts**
- **Assets in which the deceased had an interest** at death that pass outside probate

The key practical implication: **a home transferred to a family member with a retained life estate** is still subject to recovery in NC. This is different from states that only recover probate assets.

### Liens
NC may place a **lien** on real property owned by a Medicaid recipient who is permanently institutionalized and cannot reasonably be expected to return home. The lien does not force sale while a protected class lives in the home (spouse, minor child, sibling with equity interest, child who provided care and resided in the home).

---

## 6. Hardship Waivers

Federal law requires states to have **hardship waiver** procedures. NC DHB will waive estate recovery claims when recovery would cause undue hardship, including:

- **Sole income-producing asset**: The asset is the primary income source for surviving family (e.g., a family farm or small business)
- **Primary residence of limited value**: NC has a low-value home exemption threshold
- **Heirs who are low-income**: Recovery would leave heirs impoverished
- **Other compelling circumstances**

Hardship waiver applications must typically be filed within 60 days of receiving the estate recovery notice. Contact NCDHHS MERP unit to apply.

---

## 7. Sequence of Claims

Estate recovery is a claim against the estate — it competes with other creditors:
- In NC, estate recovery is a **general unsecured claim** — it follows funeral/burial expenses and secured debts, but comes before most general creditors
- The executor/administrator must notify NCDHHS of the death and any estate proceedings

---

## 8. Medicaid Planning Implications

**Common strategies attempted (consult an elder law attorney)**:
- **Irrevocable Medicaid Asset Protection Trusts (MAPTs)**: Transfer assets to an irrevocable trust well before nursing home entry; the trust assets are not counted for eligibility after the look-back period and may be protected from estate recovery — but NC's expanded estate definition and the 60-month look-back require careful advance planning
- **Caregiver child exception**: Home may be transferred to a child who lived in the home and provided care for ≥2 years before institutionalization (avoids asset transfer penalty)
- **Annuities**: Complex Medicaid-compliant annuities can convert countable assets to income stream — subject to beneficiary designation rules favoring Medicaid

**Lookback period**: Medicaid applies a **60-month (5-year) lookback period** for asset transfers to prevent last-minute giving; transfers for less than fair market value within 60 months can result in a penalty period of ineligibility.

---

## 9. Anti-Patterns

**"Medicaid will take your house while you're alive"** — False. Estate recovery happens after death. The home is generally exempt as a primary residence for eligibility purposes while the applicant lives there (or is expected to return). Liens can be placed on property of permanently institutionalized individuals but don't force sale while certain relatives live there.

**"Adding your child to the deed before entering a nursing home avoids Medicaid recovery"** — Dangerous oversimplification. This may be a countable asset transfer triggering the lookback penalty, or NC's expanded estate definition may still reach remainder interests. Consult an elder law attorney.

**"Estate recovery only applies to nursing home care"** — It covers nursing facility, ICF/I/DD, HCBS, and related hospital/Rx for individuals 55+. The scope depends on state rules.
