# Integrated Treatment: ADHD Comorbid with Substance Use Disorder

## Core Concepts

- **Sequential model**: treats SUD first, then ADHD — historically preferred but flawed; untreated ADHD symptoms drive relapse and dropout
- **Integrated model**: simultaneous pharmacotherapy + psychotherapy for both disorders; now guideline-preferred given bidirectional ADHD-SUD relationship
- **Bidirectionality**: ADHD impairs executive function and impulse control → facilitates SUD onset/severity; SUD worsens ADHD symptom expression and reduces treatment engagement
- **Stimulant diversion concern**: drives clinician reluctance; mitigated by extended-release/prodrug formulations (OROS-MPH, lisdexamfetamine) with lower misuse liability
- **Treatment gap**: adults with ADHD+SUD are less likely to receive ADHD-specific treatment despite higher clinical severity, earlier onset, and greater polysubstance use

## Mechanisms / Neurobiology

- ADHD-SUD comorbidity prevalence: ~50% of adults in SUD treatment have ADHD; ~23% of adults with ADHD develop SUD (vs ~10% general population)
- Shared dopaminergic hypofunctionality in striatum and prefrontal cortex underlies both impulse dysregulation and substance reinforcement
- OROS-methylphenidate: osmotic delivery produces flat pharmacokinetic curve, reducing reinforcing peak-and-trough; 24-week RCT (up to 180 mg/day) in amphetamine dependence + ADHD showed ADHD improvement without increasing relapse risk
- Lisdexamfetamine (LDX): prodrug requiring enzymatic cleavage limits IV/intranasal misuse; one RCT in cocaine use disorder showed ADHD improvement but no reduction in cocaine use — benefit may require concurrent behavioral treatment
- Atomoxetine: non-stimulant option with lower diversion risk; evidence weaker than stimulants for ADHD symptom reduction in SUD context, but useful when stimulants are contraindicated

## Clinical Picture

- ADHD+SUD patients present with earlier substance use onset, more polysubstance use, greater psychiatric comorbidity (mood, anxiety, conduct disorder), and more hospitalizations than SUD-only patients
- Persistent ADHD symptoms — particularly inattention — are independent predictors of SUD relapse and premature treatment dropout
- Diagnosing ADHD during active SUD is valid if symptoms predate substance use and persist in early sobriety; waiting for prolonged abstinence is no longer recommended if clinical picture supports ADHD
- Stimulant medications in supervised SUD treatment settings show no significant increase in adverse events vs placebo (2024 VA Evidence Synthesis, 27 trials reviewed)

## Evidence & Treatment

- **Safren CBT protocol** (adapted for ADHD): adds organizational/planning skills training and ADHD psychoeducation to standard SUD CBT; RCT data show significant additive ADHD symptom reduction beyond SUD CBT alone
- **Motivational Interviewing (MI) adaptations**: MI for ADHD+SUD targets ambivalence about ADHD diagnosis acceptance and medication adherence, not just substance behavior change; brief sessions with frequent check-ins accommodate attentional deficits
- **Contingency management (CM)**: effective for SUD in this population; ADHD does not appear to diminish CM responsiveness, though organizational barriers (appointment keeping, voucher tracking) require scaffolding
- **MAT + stimulant combinations**: buprenorphine/naltrexone + ADHD pharmacotherapy is clinically feasible with no documented pharmacokinetic interactions; opioid agonist therapy does not contraindicate stimulant treatment; combined management improves retention
- **Treatment retention**: the primary challenge — ADHD patients drop out of SUD treatment at higher rates; integrated programs (single-site, co-located services, case management) show higher retention than parallel/sequential arrangements

## Key Facts

- ~50% of adults presenting to SUD treatment meet criteria for ADHD (vs ~5% general adult prevalence)
- ADHD onset before age 12 is a DSM-5 diagnostic requirement; retrospective reporting is acceptable and standard in SUD populations
- 24-week RCT of OROS-MPH (up to 180 mg/day) in amphetamine-dependent adults with ADHD: significant ADHD improvement, no increase in drug relapse rates
- LDX RCT in cocaine SUD: ADHD symptoms improved significantly; cocaine use outcomes not significantly different from placebo — behavioral co-treatment likely required for substance endpoint
- Integrated care (combined pharmacotherapy + psychotherapy, co-located) produces higher patient satisfaction, greater symptom awareness, and more stable recovery trajectories than sequential models (INCaS study)
