## 1. Overview — Where SUD and Family Law Intersect

NC family law and SUD intersect in three primary contexts:

| Context | Governing Law | Who Initiates |
|---|---|---|
| **Custody disputes** (private parties) | GS Chapter 50 (domestic relations) | Parents / guardians |
| **CPS investigations / abuse-neglect-dependency (AND) proceedings** | GS Chapter 7B (Juvenile Code) | NC DSS / parents |
| **Termination of parental rights (TPR)** | GS 7B-1111 et seq. | NC DSS or guardian ad litem |

In all three contexts, **the child's best interests** is the controlling standard under NC law. SUD is not per se grounds for losing custody or parental rights — the question is always whether and how substance use affects a parent's ability to care safely for the child.[^1]

---

## 2. Child Custody — Substance Use as a Factor

In private custody disputes under GS Chapter 50, NC courts apply the **best interests of the child** standard (GS 50-13.2).[^1]

**SUD-relevant factors courts consider:**
- Whether substance use impairs the parent's capacity to care for the child
- Whether the parent has undergone SUD treatment and duration of sobriety
- Whether the child has been exposed to dangerous environments or substances
- Whether substance use has led to domestic violence or neglect
- A parent's history of substance-related arrests or convictions (a factor, not disqualifying)

**Drug testing in private custody:** Courts may order drug testing of a parent as part of a custody order or modification proceeding. A positive test is a factor, not a conclusive finding of unfitness.[^2]

**Modification standard:** A parent seeking to modify custody based on the other parent's SUD must show a **substantial change in circumstances** affecting the child's welfare since the last custody order.

## 3. Drug Testing in Family Court

Courts in both private custody (GS Chapter 50) and AND proceedings (GS Chapter 7B) may order drug testing.[^2]

**Types of tests ordered:**
- Urine drug screen (UDS) — most common; typically detects use within 1–3 weeks
- Hair follicle testing — detects use over longer periods (up to ~90 days)
- Nail testing — 3–6 month window
- EtG (ethyl glucuronide) urine test — detects alcohol use within ~3 days

**Consequences of positive test:**
- May result in reduced parenting time, supervised visitation, or modification of custody
- May trigger referral for a formal SUD evaluation
- Does NOT automatically result in termination of parenting time

**Consequences of refusal:**
- Courts may draw an adverse inference from refusal to submit to a court-ordered test
- Refusal may be treated as a violation of a custody order

## 4. CPS Investigations Involving Parental SUD

When NC DSS (Department of Social Services) receives a report alleging child abuse, neglect, or dependency involving parental substance use, the CPS Assessment Policy (updated May 2025) applies.[^3]

**Response timelines:**
- **Abuse reports:** Assessment must be initiated **within 24 hours** of receipt
- **Neglect/dependency reports:** Assessment must be initiated **within 72 hours**

**Indicators that trigger SUD-focused assessment:**
- Active substance use observed by DSS worker
- Drug paraphernalia in the home
- Child showing signs of exposure or neglect linked to parental substance use
- Hospital notification of substance-exposed newborn
- Domestic violence with SUD involvement

**Assessment outcomes:**
1. **No action:** Insufficient evidence of maltreatment
2. **Services only:** Family offered voluntary services; no court involvement
3. **Non-secure custody:** Child temporarily removed; proceeding filed with court
4. **AND petition filed:** Formal court proceeding (see Section 6)

**Important:** A CPS report does not automatically create a finding of maltreatment. DSS must substantiate the report.[^3]

## 5. Substance-Exposed Newborns — Notification and Plan of Safe Care

**Federal framework:** The Comprehensive Addiction and Recovery Act of 2016 (CARA), through amendment to CAPTA (Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act), requires states to have a notification system and Plan of Safe Care process for substance-affected infants.[^4]

**NC definition — substance-affected infant:** An infant qualifies if the infant has any of the following:
- Positive urine, meconium, or cord segment drug screen with confirmatory testing
- Mother had a medical evaluation indicative of an active SUD during pregnancy or at birth
- Infant manifests clinically relevant drug or alcohol withdrawal (neonatal abstinence syndrome / NAS)
- Known prenatal alcohol exposure with clinical concerns[^4]

**Notification:** Health care providers must notify **NC DSS** when they are involved in the delivery or care of a substance-affected infant. Implemented under NC GS 7B-302 (the mandatory reporting statute for abuse/neglect/dependency).[^5]

**Critical clarification:** The notification requirement **does not mean prenatal substance use is per se child maltreatment.** The purpose is to connect families with services, not to prosecute mothers for drug use during pregnancy.[^4]

### Plan of Safe Care (POSC)

The **NC Plan of Safe Care (NC POSC)**, launched August 2017 in coordination with NCDHHS, requires a written plan addressing:[^4]

- The infant's health and substance exposure needs
- The caregiver's SUD treatment needs and referrals
- Which agency is responsible for developing and monitoring the plan

The POSC is developed jointly by hospital/health providers, DSS, and the family. It is **not** automatically an AND petition — it is a service coordination document. However, failure to comply with a POSC can support a subsequent AND petition.

## 6. Juvenile Abuse/Neglect/Dependency (AND) Proceedings

When DSS substantiates a CPS report involving parental SUD, it may file an AND petition under **GS Chapter 7B**.[^5]

**Key phases:**

| Phase | Timeline | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Non-secure custody hearing | Within 7 days of removal | Child remains in or returns to custody; DSS services begin |
| Adjudication hearing | Within 60 days of filing | Court determines if child is abused, neglected, or dependent |
| Disposition hearing | At or after adjudication | Court orders appropriate services and permanency planning |
| Review hearings | Every 6 months | Progress on case plan; reunification vs. alternative permanency |
| Permanency planning | 12 months from removal | Court sets permanent plan |
| TPR / adoption | If reunification fails | Filed within 60 days of TPR plan adoption |

**SUD-specific case plan requirements:**
- Parent must engage in SUD assessment (typically ASAM-level assessment)
- Parent must complete recommended level of SUD treatment
- Parent must submit to drug testing as directed
- Parent must demonstrate sustained sobriety (NC courts generally require 6–12 months clean before reunification is ordered)

**Family Treatment Court** coordinates with AND proceedings for eligible parents — see Section 8.

## 7. Termination of Parental Rights — SUD Grounds (GS 7B-1111)

NC GS 7B-1111 establishes specific grounds for TPR.[^6] SUD is most commonly litigated under two provisions:

### Ground (a)(1) — Neglect

> The parent has abused or neglected the juvenile. The juvenile shall be deemed to be **abused or neglected** if the court finds the juvenile to be an abused juvenile within the meaning of GS 7B-101 or a neglected juvenile within the meaning of GS 7B-101.

**SUD-related neglect** includes failure to provide proper care, supervision, or discipline when the parent's substance use is a contributing cause. Active addiction that renders a parent unable to meet basic needs of the child supports a neglect finding.[^6]

### Ground (a)(6) — Parental Incapability

> The parent is incapable of providing for the proper care and supervision of the juvenile such that the juvenile is a dependent juvenile within the meaning of GS 7B-101, and **(A) there is a reasonable probability that such incapability will continue for the foreseeable future.** Incapability under this subdivision may be the result of **substance abuse**, intellectual disability, mental illness, organic brain syndrome, or any other cause or condition that renders the parent unable or unavailable to parent the juvenile and the parent lacks an appropriate alternative child care arrangement.

GS 7B-1111(a)(6) explicitly names **substance abuse** as a basis for the incapability ground. The key elements are:
1. The parent is currently incapable of proper care/supervision
2. The incapability is likely to continue for the foreseeable future (not just current inability)
3. No appropriate alternative care arrangement exists[^6]

### Ground (a)(2) — Foster Care Beyond 12 Months

> The parent has willfully left the juvenile in foster care or placement outside the home for more than 12 months without showing to the satisfaction of the court that **reasonable progress under the circumstances has been made in correcting those conditions which led to the removal of the juvenile**.

Failure to complete court-ordered SUD treatment, continued positive drug tests, and non-engagement with services can constitute failure to make "reasonable progress" under this ground.[^6]

### Adjudicatory vs. Dispositional Stages in TPR

TPR proceedings have two stages:
1. **Adjudication** — Did grounds exist? (Clear and convincing evidence standard)
2. **Disposition** — Is TPR in the child's best interests? (Even if grounds exist, court has discretion)

The fact that grounds exist (e.g., SUD incapability) does NOT automatically result in TPR — the court must also find TPR is in the child's best interests.

## 8. Family Treatment Court

**Family Treatment Court** is a specialized track within NC Recovery Courts (GS 7A-790 et seq.) for parents with SUD who are involved in the child welfare system (AND proceedings).[^2]

**How it works:**
- Parent participates in intensive SUD treatment while the AND case is pending
- Interdisciplinary team (judge, DSS, LME-MCO, treatment provider) collaborates
- Closely coordinates with DSS case plan and AND court timeline
- Successful completion supports reunification; failure may accelerate TPR

**Geographic coverage:** Family Treatment Courts operate in a subset of NC's 100 counties. See [nccourts.gov/courts/recovery-courts](https://www.nccourts.gov/courts/recovery-courts) for current county coverage.

**For more on Recovery Court mechanics:** see `nc-drug-treatment-courts.md` (hub reference).

## 9. Privacy of SUD Records in Family Court (42 CFR Part 2 Intersection)

A critical tension exists between **42 CFR Part 2** (SUD record confidentiality — see `nc-sud-confidentiality.md`) and family court/AND proceedings.[^1]

**Key rules:**
- A standard subpoena in a custody or AND case **does NOT authorize** disclosure of Part 2 SUD treatment records.
- DSS or a party seeking SUD treatment records must obtain a **Part 2-specific court order** (42 CFR § 2.61–2.67), which requires a finding that the public interest and need for disclosure outweighs potential harm AND other avenues are unavailable.
- A parent's **voluntary consent** to disclosure of treatment records is the most straightforward path and is often required as a condition of a case plan.
- NC GS 122C-52/53 provides parallel state confidentiality protections.

**Practical implication:** In AND proceedings, DSS may include a consent-to-release-records provision in the case plan. Refusal to consent can be used as evidence of non-compliance with the case plan.

## 10. Anti-Patterns / Common Misunderstandings

**"A positive drug test automatically means losing custody"**
No. In both private custody and AND proceedings, a positive drug test is a factor, not an automatic outcome. Courts consider context, treatment history, engagement, and impact on the child.[^1][^2]

**"Substance-exposed newborn = criminal charge or automatic loss of custody"**
No. The NC Plan of Safe Care notification process is focused on service connection, not prosecution. NC does not criminalize prenatal substance exposure by statute. However, failure to engage with services or DSS can lead to AND proceedings.[^4]

**"If I complete SUD treatment, the TPR case goes away"**
Not automatically. Completing treatment is important, but TPR under GS 7B-1111(a)(1) or (a)(6) also depends on the child's circumstances, timeline, and the child's own best interests at the dispositional stage. The court must still find TPR is NOT in the child's best interests even after grounds are found.[^6]

**"SUD records are discoverable by DSS via subpoena"**
No. 42 CFR Part 2 bars disclosure via standard subpoena. DSS must seek a Part 2 court order or obtain consent. However, a parent who consented to release records as part of a case plan cannot later claim they were improperly disclosed.[^1]

**"Family Treatment Court replaces the AND proceeding"**
No. Family Treatment Court is a parallel track within the AND proceeding. The court still enters orders and DSS still monitors compliance. Family Treatment Court is a mechanism to intensify treatment engagement alongside the legal process, not to bypass it.
