# Mental Health Comorbidities in Narcolepsy

## Core Concepts
- Psychiatric comorbidities affect the majority of people with narcolepsy; depression, anxiety, and ADHD are the three most prevalent
- Relationships are bidirectional: narcolepsy symptoms (EDS, cataplexy, social disruption) drive psychiatric illness, while psychiatric disorders worsen functional impairment
- Hypocretin-1 deficiency is the shared pathophysiological link — orexin modulates serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, and histamine circuits underlying mood and attention
- Delayed diagnosis (mean ~10 years) expands the window for secondary psychiatric morbidity to accumulate before treatment
- Distinguishing primary psychiatric disorder from narcolepsy-driven symptoms is clinically challenging; misdiagnosis as depression or ADHD is common

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Orexin neurons project to the locus coeruleus (NE), dorsal raphe (5-HT), and VTA (DA) — loss of orexinergic tone destabilizes all three monoamine systems implicated in depression and anxiety
- Histaminergic deficiency from orexin loss reduces arousal/motivation drive, phenocopying anhedonia and fatigue
- Disrupted REM sleep architecture (REM intrusions, fragmented sleep) independently elevates depression and anxiety risk via impaired emotional memory consolidation
- ADHD overlap likely reflects shared dopaminergic dysfunction: orexin promotes tonic DA release in prefrontal cortex; deficiency impairs sustained attention and inhibitory control
- Mendelian randomization studies (2023) support a causal bidirectional relationship, not merely phenotypic overlap — narcolepsy increases psychiatric disorder risk and vice versa
- Cataplexy triggered by positive emotions (laughter, excitement) drives social avoidance, reinforcing isolation and secondary depression

## Clinical Picture
- Depression prevalence in narcolepsy: 39–57% (vs ~7% general population); anxiety: 35–53%; ADHD: ~25% (2024 meta-analysis)
- 83% of patients report feelings of isolation; 81% depression; 80% anxiety on self-report surveys
- Work interference: 88%; social life interference: 86%; narcolepsy patients show 3x higher antidepressant use than matched controls (37.3% vs ~12%)
- NT1 patients most commonly prescribed venlafaxine (15%) and fluoxetine (13%) for comorbid mood/anxiety
- Psychotic disorders and eating disorders are elevated but less prevalent; NT1 (with cataplexy) carries higher psychiatric burden than NT2

## Evidence & Treatment
- SSNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine) serve dual purpose: treat depression/anxiety AND suppress cataplexy via NE reuptake inhibition — preferred first-line for comorbid presentations
- Stimulants (modafinil, solriamfetol, amphetamines) may worsen anxiety and psychosis vulnerability; careful titration required; monitor for emergent psychiatric symptoms
- Some antidepressants (sedating TCAs, mirtazapine) and antipsychotics worsen EDS — avoid or use cautiously; drug interactions with sodium oxybate require particular vigilance
- CBT-I and behavioral interventions improve mood and quality of life independent of pharmacotherapy; group therapy addresses social isolation
- Adequate narcolepsy treatment (reducing EDS, cataplexy) independently improves depression/anxiety scores — treat the sleep disorder first before escalating psychiatric pharmacotherapy

## Key Facts
- Psychiatric misdiagnosis precedes narcolepsy diagnosis in ~30–50% of patients; depression or ADHD are the most common initial labels
- Antidepressant use in narcolepsy is 3× higher than matched general population
- OR for anxiety disorder at narcolepsy diagnosis: 4.56 (vs general population)
- 25% ADHD comorbidity rate confirmed by 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis
- Depression prevalence increases from ~40% at diagnosis to ~50% with prolonged observation, suggesting progressive secondary accumulation
