# Social Determinants of Addiction Risk & Recovery

## Core Concepts
- **Social determinants of health (SDOH)**: Conditions in environments where people are born, live, work, and socialize that shape vulnerability to and recovery from SUD
- **ACEs (Adverse Childhood Experiences)**: Abuse, neglect, household dysfunction; cumulative dose-response relationship with SUD — ≥4 ACEs yields 7× higher risk of alcohol dependence, 10× illicit drug use
- **Recovery capital**: Sum of internal/external resources (social support, employment, housing, health care access) that reduce barriers to and sustain recovery
- **Structural racism**: Systemic policies producing differential exposure to poverty, incarceration, and trauma; drives SUD disparities across race/ethnicity independent of individual-level factors
- **Syndemic framework**: SUD co-occurs with poverty, trauma, homelessness, and HIV/hepatitis as mutually reinforcing epidemics requiring integrated social-medical response

## Mechanisms / Biology
- ACEs dysregulate the HPA axis (elevated basal cortisol, blunted stress response), amplifying CRF/norepinephrine signaling that drives negative-reinforcement drug use
- Early-life trauma reduces prefrontal cortical volume and top-down inhibitory control, increasing impulsivity and allostatic load that sustains compulsive use
- Neighborhood poverty elevates chronic psychosocial stress, suppressing dopaminergic reward tone and increasing incentive salience of substances as relief
- Housing instability disrupts sleep, nutrition, and medication adherence, impairing neurobiological recovery and increasing relapse risk
- Discrimination and racism activate threat-detection circuitry (amygdala hyperreactivity), raising baseline arousal states that self-medication with alcohol/opioids temporarily relieves

## Clinical Picture
- 20.4 million Americans met SUD criteria in 2023 (SAMHSA); prevalence 2–3× higher in individuals experiencing homelessness
- Poverty increases SUD risk ~2× after controlling for mental health; concentrated neighborhood poverty independently predicts opioid overdose mortality rates
- Black Americans face 30–50% lower rates of SUD treatment initiation despite equal or higher prevalence, driven by stigma, criminal justice diversion, and insurance gaps
- Unstable housing is the strongest predictor of treatment dropout; patients without housing are 3× more likely to discontinue MOUD within 90 days
- Women with SUD face compounding barriers: childcare, intimate partner violence, and co-occurring PTSD (present in ~50% of women with OUD)

## Evidence & Treatment
- **Housing First**: RCTs (Pathways to Housing) show 80% housing retention vs 30% in treatment-first models; substance use outcomes equivalent without sobriety requirement
- **MOUD + social services integration**: Adding case management to buprenorphine increases 6-month retention by 20–30% (NEJM 2022 meta-analysis)
- **Trauma-Informed Care (TIC)**: Seeking Safety RCTs show significant reductions in PTSD and SUD symptoms vs standard care; now SAMHSA best-practice standard
- **ACE screening + intervention**: Pediatric ACE screening with family navigation reduces early substance initiation; CDC Essentials for Childhood framework
- **Transportation/social support**: Providing transportation to MOUD clinic increases 6-month retention by ~25%; peer recovery coaches reduce relapse rates 15–20% in RCTs

## Key Facts
- ACE score ≥4: 7× alcohol dependence risk, 10× illicit drug use risk, 12× IV drug use risk
- Homelessness increases overdose death rate ~10× vs housed peers
- ~60% of incarcerated individuals meet SUD criteria; post-release period carries 12× elevated overdose mortality in the first 2 weeks
- Low-income individuals are 3× less likely to receive MOUD (buprenorphine/naltrexone) than higher-income counterparts
- Employment and social support are the two strongest predictors of sustained recovery at 1 year across SUD types
