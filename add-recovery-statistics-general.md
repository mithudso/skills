# SUD Recovery Statistics and Epidemiology

## Core Concepts
- **Recovery from SUD is common** — the majority of people with SUD achieve sustained remission in their lifetime, most without formal treatment (natural recovery / self-change)
- Recovery is a heterogeneous process; rigid abstinence-only definitions undercount actual recovery; the field increasingly uses **functional recovery** frameworks (quality of life, functioning, reduced harm, not only abstinence)
- **Treatment utilization gap:** only ~10-12% of people with SUD receive any specialty treatment in a given year — a persistent public health failure
- Overdose mortality has risen dramatically (COVID accelerating fentanyl/polysubstance era), but recovery prevalence remains high for those who survive
- **Recovery capital** (social support, housing, employment, health, cultural identity) strongly predicts sustained recovery and is increasingly a target of intervention

## SUD Prevalence (United States)

**NSDUH 2022 estimates (SAMHSA annual survey, ages 12+):**
- Past-year SUD: **46.8 million** people (16.7% of population ≥ 12)
  - Alcohol use disorder: 29.5 million
  - Drug use disorder: 27.2 million (overlap common)
- Past-year opioid use disorder: 6.1 million (heroin: 1.0M; prescription opioids: 5.5M)
- Past-year stimulant use disorder: 5.0 million (methamphetamine: 2.5M; cocaine: 2.2M)
- Past-year cannabis use disorder: 16.3 million (reflects prevalence, not identical to severity)
- Past-year specialty SUD treatment received: 4.0 million (8.6% of those with SUD — **treatment gap persists**)

**Lifetime SUD prevalence (NESARC-III, alcohol and drug):**
- Lifetime AUD: ~29% of US adults (highest SUD by lifetime prevalence)
- Lifetime DSM-5 drug use disorder: ~10-12% of US adults
- Cannabis lifetime: ~6%; opioid: ~5%; stimulant: ~4%

## Recovery Prevalence
- **NSDUH 2019 (pre-COVID):** 21.1 million American adults self-report being in recovery or having recovered from SUD — approximately 3 in 4 people who ever had SUD eventually resolve it
- **Natural recovery (without formal treatment):** estimated 50-80% of lifetime SUD resolves without specialty treatment (substance-dependent on severity, type, demographics)
- Recovery prevalence is higher than treatment exposure — underscoring that most SUD remission occurs outside the healthcare system

## Recovery Rates by Substance

**Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD)**
- ~75% lifetime recovery rate (NESARC); ~50% achieve stable abstinence; ~25% achieve low-risk drinking (per natural history studies)
- 1-year abstinence after treatment: ~40-50% in clinical trials; lower in naturalistic (20-30%)
- **Spontaneous remission** without treatment: ~1-2% per year (lower severity predicts natural recovery)
- AUD pharmacotherapy: naltrexone reduces relapse by ~35%; acamprosate modestly helps abstinence; disulfiram requires adherence support

**Opioid Use Disorder (OUD)**
- Without MAT: 12-month remission rates as low as 5-10% (heroin); high early overdose mortality is a major competing risk
- **With buprenorphine-naloxone:** 50-60% treatment retention at 6 months (key surrogate for outcomes); mortality reduction ~50-60% vs untreated or non-MAT
- **With XR-naltrexone (Vivitrol):** comparable outcomes to buprenorphine when successfully inducted (~50% retention at 6 months); initiation requires full detox (barrier)
- **Long-term buprenorphine:** 12-month retention ~35-50% in real-world settings; each additional month in treatment improves outcomes; abrupt discontinuation increases OD risk
- Heroin to opioid shift: illicit fentanyl now predominates; OUD lethality markedly increased — mortality rates 7-8× higher than general population

**Stimulant Use Disorder (including meth)**
- No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy; contingency management (CM) is gold-standard behavioral intervention
- Natural recovery rates for cocaine/meth SUD: estimated 40-60% lifetime remission (similar to alcohol) but data sparser
- 12-month abstinence after treatment: ~30-40% in best-performing trial settings (CM-based)
- ADAPT-2 (bupropion+XR-naltrexone for meth, NEJM 2021): 13.6% vs 2.5% abstinence rate — a real benefit with a small absolute magnitude (NNT ~9)
- CM shown to achieve ~30% abstinence in stimulant SUD; recent large-scale implementation (PRIZM trial, California contingency management program via Medicaid)

**Cannabis Use Disorder (CUD)**
- Considered the "most undertreated" SUD; help-seeking low; most CUD resolves naturally over time
- Among those seeking treatment: 12-month abstinence ~35-45%; relapse common
- No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy; N-acetylcysteine (NAC) showed modest benefit in adolescents; gabapentin and CBT evidence moderate

## Relapse as Part of Recovery
- **Relapse rates across SUD:** 40-60% within first year of treatment — comparable to other chronic conditions (hypertension, asthma, type 2 diabetes)
- Relapse does not equal treatment failure; chronic disease model reframes relapse as recurrence requiring continued management
- Average number of serious quit attempts before sustained remission: 2-3 for alcohol; similar for other substances — "a process, not an event"
- Post-relapse overdose risk is **highest in the 2-4 weeks after release from incarceration or residential treatment** (tolerance loss)

## Overdose Mortality Trends

**US overdose deaths (CDC provisional data, 2022):**
- **~109,680 overdose deaths** — all-time high; approximately 70-80% opioid-involved
- **Opioid-involved:** ~80,400; synthetic opioids (illicit fentanyl) drove ~67,300 deaths (84% of opioid deaths)
- **Stimulant-involved:** ~32,500 meth/cocaine-involved; majority now involve *both* fentanyl and stimulant (polysubstance — "stimulant overdose deaths" increasingly reflect polysubstance poisoning)
- Overdose death rate: ~33/100,000 (age-adjusted); highest in males 35-44; racial disparities: Native American/Alaska Native highest rate; significant increase in Black Americans (primarily fentanyl-cocaine polysubstance)
- COVID impact: OD deaths increased ~30% from 2019 to 2021 (drug supply disruption, isolation, reduced harm reduction access)

**Trajectory:** OD deaths increased 400% from 2010 to 2022; public health interventions (naloxone distribution, fentanyl test strips, buprenorphine access expansion, CM) aim to bend the curve

## Treatment Utilization Gap
- **Only ~10-12% of people with past-year SUD receive specialty SUD treatment** (NSDUH 2022)
- Barriers: stigma (greatest reported barrier), cost/insurance, access (geographic), denial/not perceiving need, family/work conflict
- **Only 14.9%** of treatment facilities offer all three FDA-approved OUD medications (buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone)
- Rural areas: acute shortage of MAT providers; telehealth buprenorphine prescribing (Ryan Haight Act flexibilities post-COVID) partially addressed access

## Recovery Capital Framework
- Developed by William White; adopted by SAMHSA and recovery advocacy movement
- **Four domains:**
  1. **Social capital:** family support, peer recovery networks (AA/NA, SMART Recovery, recovery housing), sponsor/mentor
  2. **Human capital:** education, employment, coping skills, health literacy, recovery experience
  3. **Physical/material capital:** housing stability, income, transportation, healthcare access
  4. **Cultural/community capital:** faith community, cultural identity, community belonging
- Recovery capital more predictive of long-term outcomes than treatment episode characteristics; drives recovery coaching and peer support model (see narc-psychosocial-behavioral for psychosocial framework)

## Long-Term Recovery Patterns
- **Natural recovery timing:** median time to SUD remission without treatment: 3-4 years for alcohol; data suggest many people eventually recover given sufficient time and life-course changes (employment, relationship, parenthood as turning points — "maturing out")
- "Spontaneous remission" (misleading term — usually involves significant internal motivation change, social context shift, and multiple failed attempts): well-documented especially in cannabis and alcohol
- **Desistance research:** SUD often follows a chronic-relapsing course with declining severity over decades in survivors; mortality selection means long-term naturalistic studies undercount true remission

## Key Facts
- 21 million Americans self-report being in recovery from SUD — 3x more people are in recovery than currently in treatment
- Buprenorphine for OUD: risk of overdose death reduced 50-60% vs untreated; each month in treatment associated with improved outcomes
- Only 10-12% of people with SUD receive any specialty SUD treatment — the largest unmet healthcare need in the US
- Post-incarceration release = highest OD mortality window: 12.7× higher risk in first 2 weeks post-release (tolerance loss)
- Contingency management (CM): largest effect size of any behavioral intervention for stimulant SUD; effective regardless of severity; severely underutilized due to regulatory barriers
- 109,680 US overdose deaths in 2022 — ~300/day; exceeded motor vehicle accidents as cause of injury death by 3:1
