# Secondary (Acquired) Narcolepsy — Etiology Comparison

## Core Concept
Secondary narcolepsy = narcolepsy-like EDS with documented CSF orexin deficiency (≤110 pg/mL) from an identifiable structural, metabolic, or immunological cause — not primary NT1 autoimmune orexin neuron destruction. CSF orexin is low in all confirmed cases; the etiology determines reversibility, workup, and treatment.

## Etiologies

### 1. Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)
- Most prevalent secondary cause; ~4–6% of moderate-to-severe TBI patients develop EDS meeting narcolepsy criteria
- Orexin neuron loss is proportional to injury severity: shear forces preferentially damage the long, unmyelinated axons of lateral hypothalamic orexin neurons
- Acute post-TBI hypersomnia (days–weeks) can resolve; chronic TBI-EDS (>3 months) with CSF orexin ≤110 pg/mL rarely reverses
- 6/7 patients with craniocerebral trauma in landmark autopsy series showed significant orexin neuron reduction (Baumann et al., 2005)
- Key workup: MRI brain (hypothalamic contusion/shear), polysomnography + MSLT, CSF orexin

### 2. Paraneoplastic
- Anti-Ma2 antibodies (testicular germ-cell, lung, breast tumors) targeting Ma2 antigen expressed in orexin neurons → NT1-like phenotype with cataplexy
- Anti-LGI1, anti-NMDAR, anti-CASPR2 paraneoplastic encephalitis occasionally produce EDS + orexin deficiency
- Often presents with limbic encephalitis features (memory loss, psychiatric symptoms) preceding hypersomnia
- Tumor resection + immunotherapy may partially restore orexin if neurons not fully destroyed
- Key workup: paraneoplastic antibody panel (anti-Ma2 priority in young males), CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET scan, scrotal ultrasound

### 3. Niemann-Pick Disease Type C (NPC)
- Lysosomal cholesterol trafficking defect → progressive orexin neuron loss; cataplexy in ~20–25% of NPC patients
- Unlike other causes, cataplexy may precede severe neurological decline and can be the presenting feature in adults
- CSF orexin typically low but variable; correlates with NPC neurological severity
- Key workup: filipin staining (fibroblasts), NPC1/NPC2 gene sequencing, plasma oxysterols (7-KC, C-triol); genetic testing essential to differentiate from NT1

### 4. Prader-Willi Syndrome (PWS)
- Hypothalamic dysfunction from chromosome 15q11-q13 deletion/UPD → partial orexin deficiency (not full depletion)
- EDS and hyperphagia are cardinal features; CSF orexin typically 110–200 pg/mL (borderline-low, not the frank deficiency of NT1)
- MSLT may not meet NT1 criteria despite severe EDS; hypocretin loss is partial
- Key workup: chromosomal microarray or methylation testing; genetic diagnosis precedes sleep evaluation

### 5. MS and Demyelinating Disease
- Demyelinating lesions in lateral hypothalamus or hypothalamo-spinal orexin tracts → secondary hypersomnia
- Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) with anti-AQP4 antibodies: several cases with frank orexin deficiency and cataplexy reported
- Lesion location on MRI predicts orexin impact; diencephalic MS plaques most relevant
- Key workup: brain/spinal MRI (T2/FLAIR hypothalamic lesion), anti-AQP4, anti-MOG antibodies

### 6. Post-Encephalitic (Including Post-COVID)
- H1N1 encephalitis precedent: orexin neuron destruction by direct viral invasion or immune response
- Post-COVID narcolepsy reports (2020–2022): autoimmune hypothesis (anti-orexin antibodies, molecular mimicry with SARS-CoV-2 spike protein); small case series only, no validated antibody test as of 2025
- Encephalitis lethargica (von Economo's) historical model: post-influenza hypothalamic encephalitis causing hypersomnolence
- Key workup: CSF PCR/antibody panel, EEG, MRI; anti-orexin antibodies not yet clinically validated

### 7. Hypothalamic Tumors and Sarcoidosis
- Craniopharyngioma, hypothalamic glioma, hamartoma, pituitary macroadenoma with suprasellar extension → mass effect on orexin neurons
- Neurosarcoidosis: granulomatous infiltration of hypothalamus; orexin deficiency documented in multiple case series
- Hypersomnia may be the presenting symptom before tumor identification
- Key workup: MRI with gadolinium (suprasellar/hypothalamic sequence), ACE level + chest CT (sarcoid), ophthalmology (sarcoid)

### 8. Methamphetamine-Induced
- Meth selectively neurotoxic to orexin neurons via ROS/oxidative stress and dopaminergic excitotoxicity; CSF orexin ≤110 pg/mL in chronic meth users with EDS
- **Unique features vs other secondary causes**:
  - *Partially reversible*: orexin recovery documented after sustained abstinence (months–years); no other secondary etiology reliably reverses
  - *High prevalence*: underdiagnosed given diagnostic confound — drug use history may mask hypersomnia as "expected withdrawal"
  - *Diagnostic confound*: active meth use acutely elevates alertness; hypersomnia emerges in withdrawal/abstinence; MSLT validity requires drug-free period (typically ≥14 days off all stimulants)
  - Cataplexy uncommon despite low orexin — mechanism unclear, possibly dopaminergic compensation
- Key workup: urine/hair drug screen, abstinence verification before MSLT, serial CSF orexin measurements to track recovery

## Diagnostic Differentiators

| Etiology | CSF Orexin | Reversible? | Key Distinguishing Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| NT1 (autoimmune) | ≤110 pg/mL | No | HLA-DQB1*06:02, no structural lesion |
| TBI | ≤110 (severe) | Rarely | MRI hypothalamic injury, trauma history |
| Paraneoplastic | ≤110 pg/mL | Partially | Anti-Ma2 panel, tumor imaging |
| NPC | Variable, low | No (progressive) | NPC1/NPC2 gene testing, oxysterols |
| PWS | 110–200 pg/mL | No | Chromosomal methylation |
| MS/NMOSD | Low when diencephalic | Partial | MRI lesion, anti-AQP4 |
| Post-encephalitic | Low during active phase | Potentially | CSF encephalitis panel |
| Hypothalamic tumor | ≤110 pg/mL | Post-resection | MRI gadolinium |
| Meth-induced | ≤110 pg/mL | Partial/Yes | Drug screen, abstinence × 14d, serial orexin |

## Key Facts
- Secondary narcolepsy accounts for <5% of all narcolepsy diagnoses; primary NT1 is the overwhelming majority
- In new-onset narcolepsy without HLA-DQB1*06:02, secondary cause workup is mandatory before diagnosing NT1
- Cataplexy is less common in most secondary forms compared to NT1, despite equivalent CSF orexin deficiency — likely reflects different downstream circuit involvement
- Anti-Ma2 paraneoplastic narcolepsy is rare but treatable if caught early; tumor search is urgent
- Meth-induced is the only etiology with documented orexin recovery, making abstinence support a disease-modifying intervention
