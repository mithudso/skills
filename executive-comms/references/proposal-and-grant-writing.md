# Proposal and Grant Writing

## Overview

Grant proposals are a constrained writing genre. Reviewers (program officers, peer panels, federal scorers) read against a rubric, in a hurry, under fatigue. A proposal that "reads well" but ignores the funder's stated criteria scores low. A proposal that follows the rubric mechanically but tells a coherent change story scores high.

This skill covers ten load-bearing elements that recur across federal (Grants.gov, NIH, NSF, SAMHSA, OJP), foundation (Kresge, Rockefeller, Ford), and NGO contexts. It is not a structural writing reference — for inverted pyramid, BLUF, SCQA, Minto, see `rhetorical-frameworks-deep`. Use this skill for the genre-specific moves: problem statement, demonstrated need, theory of change, logframe, SMART objectives, NIH Specific Aims, budget narrative, sustainability plan, executive summary, and attachments hygiene.

When to invoke: the operator is drafting or reviewing any section of a grant or proposal, building a logic model, justifying a budget, or trying to diagnose a low-scoring application.

- The user is writing a resume, CV, or cover letter → use `resume-and-cv-writing`.

## Core Concepts

### 1. Problem statement anchor

The problem statement is the proposal's gravitational center. Every later section — aims, activities, budget, evaluation — must trace back to a problem the funder cares about. Three reviewer-tested moves:

1. **Open in the funder's frame.** Quote the funding opportunity's priority area in the first two sentences. The reviewer must instantly recognize: "this applicant read the FOA."
2. **Quantify the problem at the right scope.** National statistics are weak without local data; local data is weak without national context. Pair them.
3. **End on the gap.** Close the problem statement with the specific knowledge, service, or capacity gap your project will fill. The gap is the bridge to the rest of the proposal.

### 2. Demonstrated need (vs. felt need)

"We need money" is a felt need. Demonstrated need is evidence — a quantified disparity, an unmet target, a documented service desert — that an external reviewer can verify. Federal reviewers are trained to score "Statement of Need" against three criteria: severity, prevalence, and evidence quality. Pull from peer-reviewed literature, government data (Census, BLS, CDC, HRSA), and community needs assessments. Avoid undated statistics; reviewers flag anything older than 5 years unless it is the canonical source.

### 3. Theory of change as the red thread

A theory of change is the narrative map of how change happens: if we do X (activities), given Y (assumptions), then Z (outcomes), because of M (mechanism). It connects activities to vision. Reviewers use it as the "red thread" that holds the proposal together — they check whether the aims, methods, and evaluation plan trace back to the theory of change. Without it, proposals read as disconnected activity lists.

### 4. Logframe (logical framework matrix)

The logframe compresses the theory of change into a structured grid. Standard four-column matrix:

| Level | Description | Indicators | Means of Verification | Assumptions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Goal | Long-term impact | Impact indicators | Population data, longitudinal study | External conditions |
| Outcome | Behavior/condition change | Outcome indicators | Survey, administrative data | Stakeholder cooperation |
| Output | Direct deliverables | Output indicators | Activity logs, attendance | Resource availability |
| Activity | What you do | Process indicators | Workplans, timesheets | Staffing capacity |

Critical distinction reviewers test: **outputs are not outcomes**. "Conducted 100 workshops" is an output. "Participants reduced X behavior by Y%" is an outcome. Confusing these tanks the score.

### 5. SMART objectives

Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Weak: "Improve youth literacy." SMART: "By June 2027, increase the proportion of 4th-grade students at our three partner schools reading at grade level from 42% (2025 baseline) to 60%, as measured by the state ELA assessment." Each SMART objective should map to one logframe outcome row.

### 6. NIH Specific Aims four-paragraph structure

The de facto NIH standard. One page. Four paragraphs:

1. **Hook + significance.** Open with the broad problem. Narrow to the specific gap in knowledge in 3-5 sentences.
2. **Central hypothesis + rationale.** State your hypothesis. Explain why it is plausible (preliminary data, literature). Establish that you are the right team.
3. **Specific Aims.** 2-4 aims. Each aim is independent — failure of one does not kill the others. Use confident verbs: "We will determine," "We will characterize," not "We hope to" or "We will attempt to."
4. **Payoff.** State the expected outcomes and their impact on the field. Tie back to the funder's mission.

### 7. Budget narrative

The budget narrative justifies every line. Each cost category (personnel, fringe, travel, equipment, supplies, contractual, indirect) gets a paragraph that answers: what, why, how calculated, why this rate. Reviewer red flags: round numbers without basis, salaries above the federal cap, equipment without quotes, indirect rates without a federally negotiated rate agreement (NICRA). Confirm the narrative numbers match the spreadsheet to the dollar.

### 8. Sustainability plan

Federal and most foundation reviewers require a sustainability section: how does the work continue when this grant ends? Strong sustainability plans show diversified funding (other grants secured or pending, earned revenue, in-kind commitments), institutionalization (policy adoption, embedded staff lines), and capacity built (trained personnel, data systems left behind). Weak plans say "we will pursue additional funding."

### 9. Executive summary as standalone document

The executive summary may be read alone by senior reviewers, board members, or program officers triaging applications. It must contain: the problem, the proposed solution, the requested amount and duration, the lead organization's qualifications, expected outcomes, and the sustainability commitment. Roughly 250-500 words. Write it last. Do not introduce concepts here that the body does not develop.

### 10. Attachments hygiene

Every attachment listed in the funding opportunity must be present, named exactly as specified, in the format specified (PDF most often), within the page limit. Common rejection-on-receipt errors: missing letters of support, expired indirect rate agreement, incorrect SF-424 version, biosketches over 5 pages (NIH), budget justification embedded in the narrative instead of as a separate attachment.

## Templates and Examples

### Problem statement template

> [Funder priority area, quoted or paraphrased from the FOA]. [National-scale statistic with citation, ≤5 years old]. In [target geography], the problem is [more acute / particularly visible] because [local data point]. Despite [existing efforts cited briefly], [the specific gap]. This proposal addresses [the gap] through [one-sentence approach preview].

### NIH Specific Aims skeleton

```
[Para 1: Significance]
[Problem context — 2 sentences]. [Narrow to gap — 2 sentences]. [Why the gap matters — 1 sentence].

[Para 2: Hypothesis + team]
[Central hypothesis — 1 sentence]. [Rationale from preliminary data + literature — 2-3 sentences]. [Team's unique qualifications — 1 sentence].

[Para 3: Aims]
Aim 1: Determine [X]. Hypothesis: [Y]. Approach: [Z].
Aim 2: Characterize [X]. Hypothesis: [Y]. Approach: [Z].
Aim 3: Test [X]. Hypothesis: [Y]. Approach: [Z].

[Para 4: Payoff]
The expected outcome is [specific deliverable]. This will [impact on field / impact on funder's mission].
```

### SMART objective template

> By [date], [target population] will [observable change] from [baseline] to [target], as measured by [data source].

### Logframe row template

| Outcome | Indicator | Baseline | Target | Means of Verification | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4th-graders read at grade level | % at grade level on state ELA | 42% (2025) | 60% (2027) | State assessment data | Schools administer assessment on schedule |

### Budget narrative line template

> **Personnel — Project Director (0.5 FTE, Year 1):** $52,500. Calculated as $105,000 annual base × 50% effort. Project Director (Dr. Smith) will lead protocol development, oversee evaluation, and serve as primary funder liaison. Salary is within the institutional range for this rank and below the federal executive Level II cap.

### Sustainability plan checklist

- [ ] Named follow-on funders (secured + pending) with amounts
- [ ] Earned-revenue model if applicable
- [ ] In-kind commitments quantified
- [ ] Institutional policy or budget line adoption
- [ ] Capacity left behind: trained staff, data systems, partnerships
- [ ] Risk of discontinuation acknowledged and mitigated

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Activity list disguised as a proposal.** Listing workshops, trainings, and convenings without tying each to an outcome the funder cares about.
2. **Outputs masquerading as outcomes.** "We will train 500 people" is not an outcome. "Trained staff will demonstrate competency X on assessment Y" is.
3. **Generic problem statement.** Opening with a global statistic that has no connection to the specific funder priority or the target geography.
4. **Hedge language in aims.** "We hope to," "we will attempt to," "we propose to perhaps." Reviewers read this as low confidence. Use "We will determine."
5. **Budget that does not match narrative.** Off-by-one errors, indirect costs computed against the wrong base, fringe rates that drift between sections.
6. **Sustainability as boilerplate.** "We will pursue additional funding" is not a plan. Name the funders, name the strategy, name the institutionalization path.
7. **Executive summary written first and never updated.** It still references the original scope after three revision rounds changed the project.
8. **Reviewer fatigue traps.** 11-point single-spaced text, no headings, dense paragraphs that span a full page. Reviewers skim. Use headers, white space, and short paragraphs.
9. **Theory of change as decoration.** A graphic stuck in an appendix that the narrative never references. The reviewer should be able to point to the theory of change while reading any section.
10. **Letters of support that are obviously templated.** Identical language across three partners signals the applicant wrote them all. Vary voice, specifics, and committed contribution per partner.

## Decision Heuristics

- **Federal vs. foundation:** federal funders weight rigor, evaluation, and sustainability heavily; foundations weight alignment with their theory of change and innovation. Calibrate emphasis accordingly.
- **NIH vs. SAMHSA vs. OJP:** NIH wants Specific Aims and a hypothesis. SAMHSA wants a logic model and cultural competence section. OJP wants performance measures aligned to its national reporting framework. Read the FOA twice before drafting.
- **Page-limit triage:** when over the limit, cut from background first (the reviewer assumes baseline knowledge), then from organizational history, then from methods detail. Never cut from aims, evaluation, or budget justification.
- **Pre-submission review:** before submission, give the proposal to (a) a colleague outside your field — if they can't summarize your aims in one sentence, the aims are unclear, and (b) someone with study-section or panel experience — they will flag rubric misses.
- **Boilerplate library:** maintain a versioned library of organizational capability statements, biosketches, indirect rate agreements, and letters of fiscal sponsorship. Update annually, not at submission time.
