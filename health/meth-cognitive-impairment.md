# Meth-Associated Cognitive Impairment

## Core Concepts
- Meth use disorder produces moderate-to-severe deficits across multiple cognitive domains; meta-analyses (Potvin et al., 2018) confirm impairment in 40–60% of regular users
- Domains most impaired: executive function, working memory, attention/processing speed, verbal learning, decision-making, and social cognition
- Relatively spared: visual learning and visuospatial abilities
- Hypofrontality — reduced prefrontal cortex activity — underlies impulsivity, poor inhibitory control, temporal-order memory failures, and treatment non-adherence
- Impairments are graded by use intensity; heavy chronic users show deficits comparable to mild traumatic brain injury

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Meth reverses dopamine transporter (DAT) direction, flooding synapses; downregulates striatal dopamine D2 receptors and DAT density by 15–20% in chronic users (PET imaging)
- Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction kill dopaminergic and serotonergic terminals in striatum and prefrontal cortex
- Meth-induced neuroinflammation (microglial activation, elevated TNF-α, IL-6) independently damages white-matter integrity
- Blood-brain barrier disruption via β-catenin downregulation increases CNS vulnerability to oxidative insult
- 2024 research identified a glutamate-to-GABA neurotransmitter switch in the prelimbic prefrontal cortex — augmented GABAergic transmission suppresses executive circuits; mechanism shared with PCP
- White matter damage concentrated in left superior longitudinal fasciculus and right corticospinal tract correlates with impulsivity and executive dysfunction on neuroimaging

## Clinical Picture
- Global cognitive impairment persists an average of 11+ months post-abstinence; not fully explained by depression or sleep disruption alone
- Executive dysfunction (planning, set-shifting, inhibition) is the most treatment-relevant deficit — predicts relapse; early age of first use is an independent risk factor
- Processing speed and working memory recover partially with extended abstinence (1–2 years) but remain below normative levels in heavy users
- Social cognition deficits (emotion recognition, theory of mind) impair therapeutic alliance and group therapy participation
- Cognitive severity correlates with cumulative dose, duration, route (IV/smoked > intranasal), and co-occurring stimulant use disorder or HIV co-infection

## Evidence & Treatment
- No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy specifically for meth-associated cognitive impairment; bupropion + naltrexone (ADAPT-2, 2021) improves abstinence but cognitive outcomes not primary endpoint
- Cognitive remediation therapy (CRT) — structured neuropsychological training — shows moderate effect sizes (d ≈ 0.4–0.6) in attention and executive function in short RCTs
- Matrix Model and contingency management improve engagement; adapt psychoeducation pacing for processing speed deficits
- Aerobic exercise (3×/week, 12 weeks) associated with improved working memory and increased striatal dopamine receptor availability in abstinent users
- Meth-associated cognitive impairment requires treatment accommodation: simplified written instructions, shorter sessions, frequent check-ins, and motivational interviewing adapted for low insight

## Key Facts
- 40–60% of meth users meet criteria for clinically significant cognitive impairment
- DAT density recovers ~15–20% after 9–14 months abstinence (PET studies), but full recovery is rare in >5-year users
- Cognitive deficits are detectable after as few as 3 months of regular use
- Executive dysfunction at treatment entry predicts 12-month relapse with OR ≈ 2.8 (Frontera et al., 2022)
- Global prevalence of amphetamine-type stimulant use: ~0.8% (2015 UNODC); meth accounts for the majority of stimulant use disorder cases in East/Southeast Asia and the US West
