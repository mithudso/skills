## Tobacco Harm Reduction in SUD — Meth Context

### Prevalence & the treatment gap

>80% of people in treatment for stimulant/methamphetamine use disorder smoke cigarettes — 3–4× the general-population rate. Programs historically avoided addressing tobacco during SUD treatment ("too much at once"), but evidence now contradicts this. Addressing tobacco in active SUD treatment does NOT worsen SUD outcomes; in several studies it improves them. SAMHSA 2020 guidelines recommend integrating tobacco treatment into SUD programs. Some studies find SUD patients in early recovery are *more* motivated to quit tobacco than the general population.

### Why complete cessation is hard at acute intake

Meth depletes dopamine and norepinephrine for weeks post-cessation. During this window (roughly days 1–14), motivational capacity is impaired and adding a nicotine quit attempt risks demoralizing the patient before recovery stabilizes. Optimal timing to assess tobacco readiness: **4–6 weeks into meth recovery**, when dopaminergic function partially restores and genuine motivation capacity returns.

### Harm reduction hierarchy (best to least clean)

1. **Complete NRT switch** (patch + gum/lozenge): eliminates all combustion toxicants; most evidence-supported; patch alone reduces cigarettes smoked even without quitting
2. **Nicotine pouches (Zyn) or snus**: smokeless; snus has FDA Modified Risk Tobacco Product (MRTP) designation; eliminates CO, tar, acrolein, formaldehyde
3. **Heated tobacco products (IQOS)**: FDA MRTP designation for reduced toxicant exposure; not zero-risk but substantially lower combustion products
4. **E-cigarettes (vaping)**: eliminates combustion products; net harm reduction vs smoking is likely positive despite high nicotine delivery (JUUL 5% salt ≈ 40–60 cigarettes/pod); may perpetuate or increase nicotine dependence; MRTP designation still under FDA review
5. **NRT patch while continuing to smoke**: reduces cigarettes/day and CO exposure; previously discouraged but now standard harm-reduction practice — not dangerous

### Meth-specific cardiovascular amplification

Meth causes direct cardiac toxicity (cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, coronary vasospasm). Combusted tobacco adds: CO-mediated hemoglobin binding, nicotine vasoconstriction, and oxidative damage. The **combination** has disproportionate CV risk vs either alone. Tobacco harm reduction in meth recovery therefore has outsized cardiovascular benefit beyond what general-population cessation studies show.

### Pharmacotherapy

**Varenicline (Chantix/Champix)** — preferred when patient is cessation-ready:
- Partially agonizes nAChRs → reduces withdrawal AND attenuates smoking reward
- Weak TAAR1 activity: pilot data suggest concurrent reduction in meth craving
- Black box neuropsychiatric warning relaxed 2016; EAGLES trial confirmed safety in psychiatric/MH populations (NPS event rates: varenicline 4%, NRT patch 3.9%, placebo 3.7%)
- Note: EAGLES excluded active SUD patients (required 12-month remission); evidence in acute SUD is extrapolated + observational; multimodal studies in methadone-maintenance and alcohol-disorder populations show good tolerability

**Bupropion + NRT patch**: well-tolerated in stimulant-using populations; bupropion's norepinephrine/dopamine reuptake inhibition may partially offset the anhedonia of early meth recovery; avoid if seizure history

**NRT monotherapy**: lowest-barrier entry point; appropriate when patient not ready for full cessation; combination patch + short-acting (gum/lozenge) more effective than patch alone

### Carbon monoxide (CO) monitoring as MI leverage

Exhaled CO at SUD appointments provides real-time biofeedback:
- <5 ppm: non-smoker level
- >20 ppm: heavy smoker
- Reduction visible within **hours** of switching to NRT or stopping combusted tobacco
- Powerful motivational interviewing anchor: "Your CO dropped from 32 to 8 since you started the patch three days ago" is more persuasive than abstract health messages

### Clinical workflow

| Recovery phase | Tobacco action |
|---|---|
| Intake / days 1–14 | Screen, document, do not push cessation; offer NRT patch as harm reduction option |
| Weeks 2–4 | Brief readiness assessment; provide psychoeducation on meth + tobacco CV risk |
| Weeks 4–6 | Full readiness/motivation assessment; introduce cessation options if ready |
| Weeks 6+ | Active cessation plan (varenicline or combo NRT); CO monitoring at visits |

### Scope boundaries
- Distinct from nicotine-sleep-disruption skill (circadian/sleep effects of nicotine in SUD recovery)
- Not a substitute for a full pharmacotherapy consultation; varenicline/bupropion require prescriber
- EAGLES SUD exclusion: extrapolation to acute SUD is reasonable but not RCT-confirmed; flag for clinicians
