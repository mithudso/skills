## The Neuroprotective Window

Acute meth binge → peak ROS generation within 24–48h → mitochondrial damage → apoptosis of lateral hypothalamus (LH) orexin neurons. Hyperthermia is the primary co-amplifier: core temperature >38°C dramatically accelerates ROS-mediated neurotoxicity in animal models. Intervening within this window is theoretically the highest-leverage point for limiting permanent orexin neuron loss.

## Evidence-Based Interventions

**Temperature control (highest priority)**  
Hyperthermia is the single most modifiable amplifier of meth neurotoxicity. In rodent models, preventing core temperature rise blocks dopamine oxidation product accumulation and long-term terminal damage even without other interventions. Clinical implication: aggressive cooling during meth intoxication — cooling blankets, benzodiazepines to reduce agitation-driven hyperthermia — is already standard EM care and likely confers neuroprotective benefit to LH structures.

**Benzodiazepines**  
Beyond sedation, benzos reduce meth-induced hyperthermia by attenuating agitation and autonomic hyperactivation. They also blunt excitotoxic glutamate release. Already first-line in meth intoxication management; dual mechanism suggests they are the most immediately accessible neuroprotective tool available in clinical settings.

**N-Acetylcysteine (NAC)**  
Preclinical evidence: NAC pretreatment or same-day administration significantly reduces meth-induced dopaminergic neuron apoptosis in rodents — partially attenuates apoptotic cell death, upregulates glutathione (GSH), and completely abrogates oxidative stress markers. Mechanism: GSH precursor replenishment that directly quenches the ROS burst. Human equivalent doses: 1200–2400 mg/day oral or 50–100 mg/kg IV. No RCT yet targets LH orexin neuroprotection specifically; NAC is used in SUD treatment contexts with separate evidence. For active users not ready to stop, oral NAC supplementation is low-risk and may reduce cumulative LH damage.

**Minocycline**  
Tetracycline antibiotic with potent anti-neuroinflammatory properties independent of its antimicrobial action. Inhibits microglial NLRP3/caspase-1 activation; in rodent meth models, attenuates microglial-driven cytokine release and dopaminergic neuron loss in a dose-dependent manner. Phase I data exist for meth addiction treatment (separate indication). No LH-specific human data; considered adjunctive and experimental.

**Ascorbate (Vitamin C) and Tocopherol (Vitamin E)**  
Preclinical protective effect in meth neurotoxicity models. High-dose IV ascorbate crosses the blood-brain barrier and scavenges ROS directly. Used informally in some EM settings during meth overdose management. Evidence base is weaker than NAC; reasonable as adjunct given safety profile.

## Clinical Translation Gap

No human RCT has tested any of these interventions specifically to protect LH orexin neurons during meth intoxication. All LH-specific evidence is from animal models. However:
- Temperature control and benzodiazepines are already standard of care
- NAC is already used in SUD contexts with established safety
- The interventions are additive (not competing) and low-risk

## Harm Reduction Frame

For active users not engaged in treatment: NAC supplementation (1200 mg/day), avoiding high-ambient-temperature environments during use, and hydration to prevent hyperthermia represent harm reduction steps that may reduce cumulative LH orexin neuron loss over repeated meth use cycles.
