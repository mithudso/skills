## Mechanism: Glutamate Homeostasis

Chronic drug use suppresses the glial cystine-glutamate exchanger (system Xc⁻/xCT) in the nucleus accumbens (NAc). This reduces extracellular (perisynaptic) glutamate, lowering tonic activation of presynaptic mGluR2/3 autoreceptors. With mGluR2/3 inhibition diminished, synaptic glutamate release surges during cue exposure and drug-seeking — the neurochemical correlate of craving-driven relapse.

NAC is deacetylated to cysteine → dimerized to cystine → taken up by xCT in exchange for intracellular glutamate. This restores perisynaptic glutamate, re-engages mGluR2/3 autoreceptor brake, and normalizes synaptic glutamate release. GLT-1 transporter upregulation (inhibits reinstatement independently) contributes a second mechanism.

## Clinical Evidence by Substance

**Cocaine.** Mardikian et al. (2007) open-label and LaRowe et al. (2006) double-blind pilot both showed NAC (1,200–2,400 mg/day) reduced craving and cocaine-cue viewing time in cocaine-dependent outpatients. Four clinical studies collectively show reduced craving, desire to use, and cocaine-related spending. Evidence is pilot-scale; no large Phase III RCT yet.

**Cannabis use disorder (CUD).** Gray et al. (2010) adult pilot and Gray et al. (2012) double-blind RCT in cannabis-dependent adolescents (1,200 mg BID × 8 weeks) are the key studies. The 2012 adolescent trial showed significantly higher odds of negative urine cannabinoid tests vs. placebo (OR ≈ 2.4). A 2022 systematic review confirmed consistent signal across trials. CUD in adolescents is a leading application given safety profile and limited pharmacotherapy options.

**Nicotine.** Small RCTs show craving reduction; evidence weaker than cannabis.

**Gambling disorder.** Shares the glutamate mechanism. RCTs (Kim 2001, Grant 2007) show NAC reduced gambling urges and behavior — supporting the glutamate hypothesis beyond substance disorders.

**Methamphetamine.** Preclinical evidence strong; clinical RCT (eClinicalMedicine 2021) showed NAC did not significantly outperform placebo on meth use outcomes at 1,200 mg BID, though craving sub-scores improved.

**Opioids.** NAC explored as adjunct to buprenorphine (rationale: meth and opioids both dysregulate NAc glutamate); trials ongoing; not yet standard.

**Alcohol use disorder.** Negative: a well-powered RCT showed no benefit. Possibly because alcohol's primary mechanism involves GABA/glutamate via NMDA rather than xCT suppression — patient-selection mismatch.

## Dosing

Typical oral: **1,200–2,400 mg/day** in divided doses (most trials use 600–1,200 mg BID). No established titration protocol. IV NAC used in overdose/toxicology contexts is not SUD dosing.

## Tolerability

Generally well-tolerated. Common: GI upset (nausea, diarrhea), sulfurous odor. Rare: headache, rash. No significant drug interactions relevant to SUD populations. Low cost (~$0.20–$0.50/day OTC).

## Why Results Are Mixed

1. **Glutamate hypothesis limitations**: Not all SUDs share equal xCT suppression; alcohol may dysregulate glutamate via different nodes.
2. **Dose-timing**: xCT tone restoration may require consistent NAC during abstinence; episodic dosing less effective.
3. **Patient selection**: Severity, comorbid psychiatric disorders, and concomitant use affect glutamatergic baseline.
4. **Outcome heterogeneity**: Craving ≠ abstinence; trials measure different endpoints.
5. **Publication bias**: Positive pilot studies precede failed replications (meth, alcohol).

## Clinical Bottom Line

NAC is a low-cost, safe adjunctive option with the strongest evidence in CUD (especially adolescents) and cocaine craving reduction. Glutamate homeostasis provides a coherent mechanistic rationale across SUDs, but RCT evidence is largely pilot-scale. Not recommended for alcohol use disorder. Best positioned as add-on to behavioral treatment where pharmacotherapy options are scarce.
