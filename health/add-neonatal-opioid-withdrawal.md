# Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS)

## Core Concepts
- **NOWS** (preferred over NAS): withdrawal syndrome in neonates following in-utero chronic opioid exposure — endogenous, prescribed (MAT), or illicit
- Distinct from neonatal opioid toxicity (acute sedation/respiratory depression at delivery vs. delayed hyperexcitability post-delivery)
- Incidence rose ~5-fold between 2002–2012 in the US; estimated 1 infant born with NOWS every 15 minutes by 2012; >80,000 cases/year at peak
- Severity depends on opioid type, dose, duration of exposure, polysubstance use, gestational age, and genetic/epigenetic factors
- NOWS is not a contraindication to MAT in pregnancy — untreated opioid use disorder carries higher maternal/fetal risk than MAT-induced NOWS

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Exogenous opioids cross the placenta and bind fetal mu-opioid receptors (MOR), downregulating receptor density and desensitizing the cAMP pathway during a critical developmental window
- Abrupt removal at birth triggers **rebound noradrenergic hyperactivity** via locus coeruleus disinhibition — the primary driver of CNS and autonomic symptoms
- Serotonin, dopamine, and glutamate systems are also dysregulated; HPA axis hyperresponsiveness contributes to stress sensitivity
- Epigenetic modifications (DNA methylation, histone acetylation) at MOR gene (OPRM1) and stress-axis genes persist postnatally and may affect long-term neurodevelopment
- Buprenorphine's partial agonism and slower receptor kinetics produce milder, shorter NOWS vs. methadone (full agonist, long half-life)
- Genetic polymorphisms in OPRM1 (A118G), COMT, and SLC6A4 predict NOWS severity and pharmacotherapy duration

## Clinical Picture
- **Onset timing**: heroin/short-acting opioids → 24–72 hrs; methadone → 48–96 hrs (up to 5–7 days); buprenorphine → 12–48 hrs
- Triad: **CNS excitability** (high-pitched cry, seizures in ~2–11%), **ANS dysregulation** (diaphoresis, mottling, fever), **GI dysfunction** (poor feeding, vomiting, diarrhea, failure to thrive)
- **Finnegan Neonatal Abstinence Scoring System (FNASS)**: 21-item tool scored q3–4h; score ≥8 on two consecutive assessments typically triggers pharmacotherapy; modified Finnegan (mFINNEGAN) and ESC (Eat Sleep Console) increasingly replace it
- **Eat Sleep Console (ESC)**: function-based tool assessing ability to eat ≥1 oz, sleep ≥1 hr, and be consoled within 10 min — reduces pharmacotherapy rates and hospital LOS vs. Finnegan
- Preterm infants (<35 weeks) show attenuated NOWS severity but greater medical complexity; NOWS risk exists regardless of gestational age

## Evidence & Treatment
- **Non-pharmacological (first-line)**: rooming-in with mother, breastfeeding (unless contraindicated by illicit drug use or HIV), skin-to-skin, reduced stimulation, swaddling, non-nutritive sucking — ESC-guided non-pharm care cuts morphine treatment rates by ~50% vs. Finnegan-triggered protocols (Vermont Oxford Network data)
- **First-line pharmacotherapy**: oral morphine (0.04–0.1 mg/kg q3–4h), oral methadone, or sublingual buprenorphine; BBORN trial (2023) showed buprenorphine superior to morphine — shorter treatment duration (15 vs. 28 days), shorter hospital LOS, no safety difference
- **Second-line adjuncts**: clonidine (α2 agonist, targets noradrenergic component, 0.5–1 mcg/kg q4–6h) or phenobarbital — used for refractory or polysubstance-exposed NOWS
- **MAT in pregnancy**: methadone or buprenorphine are standard of care (ACOG, SAMHSA); buprenorphine/naloxone combination generally avoided in first trimester but used with monitoring; switching MAT drugs during pregnancy not recommended due to destabilization risk
- Hospital LOS averages 16–23 days for pharmacotherapy-requiring NOWS; rooming-in models cut LOS by ~30% and cost by ~$5,000/admission

## Key Facts
- ~60–80% of opioid-exposed neonates develop clinically significant NOWS; only ~40–60% require pharmacotherapy
- FNASS threshold for treatment: score ≥8 on ≥2 consecutive assessments, or single score ≥12
- Buprenorphine-exposed infants (vs. methadone) average 40% shorter NOWS treatment duration and 10% lower pharmacotherapy rate
- Breastfeeding is recommended for mothers on stable MAT (methadone or buprenorphine) without illicit drug use — minimal drug transfer, strong bonding benefit
- Long-term outcomes: increased risk of attention/behavioral disorders, language delay, and altered stress reactivity through age 5–6; early nurturing environment is the strongest modifiable protective factor
