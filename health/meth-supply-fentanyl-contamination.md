# Fentanyl-Contaminated Meth Supply

## Core Concepts
- Fentanyl adulteration of illicit meth refers to the presence of fentanyl (or analogues) in street methamphetamine, either through deliberate cutting or cross-contamination in shared supply chains
- Prevalence is regionally heterogeneous: nationally <5% of meth samples contain fentanyl, but Northeast states exceed 10% co-occurrence rates (2017–2023 data)
- Contamination poses outsized risk to opioid-naive meth users who lack opioid tolerance and are unaware of exposure
- Two exposure pathways: (1) unknowing ingestion of adulterated product; (2) intentional polysubstance co-use (meth to offset opioid sedation)
- Fentanyl test strips (FTS) and drug-checking services are the primary detection tools available to people who use drugs

## Mechanisms / Biology
- Fentanyl is a full μ-opioid receptor (MOR) agonist ~100× more potent than morphine; 50–100 mcg produces analgesia; 2 mg can cause fatal respiratory depression in opioid-naive individuals
- Methamphetamine's stimulant effect (dopamine/norepinephrine release) can mask early opioid sedation, delaying recognition of impending overdose
- Concurrent stimulant use does NOT reliably block fentanyl-induced respiratory depression — naloxone reversal studies confirm opioid-mediated death risk persists in the presence of stimulants
- Meth-induced tachycardia and hypertension combined with opioid bradycardia create unpredictable cardiovascular stress
- Fentanyl analogues (acetylfentanyl, carfentanil, parafluorofentanyl) vary in potency and duration; carfentanil is ~100× more potent than fentanyl, making even trace contamination lethal
- FTS detect fentanyl in dissolved drug residue; sensitivity is high for fentanyl but may miss novel analogues or non-uniform distribution ("hot spots") within a pressed pill or powder batch

## Clinical Picture
- ~50% of the ~73,000 US synthetic opioid overdose deaths in 2023 involved a stimulant (meth or cocaine) — polysubstance deaths are now the modal overdose pattern
- 62% of individuals who tested positive for fentanyl in 2024 also tested positive for methamphetamine (up 14% from 2023)
- Overdose presentation mirrors pure opioid OD: pinpoint pupils, cyanosis, apnea, unresponsiveness — meth's stimulant signs (diaphoresis, tachycardia) may coexist but do not reliably differentiate
- Many meth-involved fentanyl OD decedents had no known opioid use disorder and may not have been aware of fentanyl exposure
- Drug-checking services using GC-MS have found regional fentanyl co-occurrence in meth ranging from <1% (West Coast) to >10% (Northeast), with a rising national trend since 2017

## Evidence & Treatment
- Naloxone reverses fentanyl-induced respiratory depression even in the context of stimulant co-intoxication — high-dose or repeat dosing (2–4 mg IN or 0.4–2 mg IV/IM) may be needed given fentanyl potency and analogues
- Take-home naloxone (THN) programs now explicitly target stimulant users, not just those with opioid use disorder, reflecting the contaminated-supply reality
- Fentanyl test strip distribution in harm reduction settings reduces overdose risk; studies show FTS use increases safer drug-use behaviors
- No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy specifically addresses stimulant-opioid polysubstance use; MOUD (buprenorphine/methadone) is indicated if OUD is present
- The 2024 US overdose death decline (~34% drop in fentanyl-related deaths vs. 2023) is attributed in part to expanded naloxone access, FTS distribution, and syringe service programs

## Key Facts
- Fentanyl lethal dose in opioid-naive adults: ~2 mg; a single contaminated line of meth could contain this amount
- National fentanyl prevalence in meth samples: ~1% overall, but >10% in some Northeast markets (2023)
- 50% of synthetic opioid OD deaths in 2023 involved a co-occurring stimulant
- Meth's stimulant masking effect delays overdose recognition by an estimated 15–45 minutes in case series
- Over-the-counter naloxone (Narcan, RiVive) approved in 2023; 4 mg IN formulation is first-line for community reversal
